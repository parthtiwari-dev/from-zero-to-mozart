The left hand has a habit to build now, before it builds a bad one: **fingers drop, and
fingers stay.** A hand that lifts every finger for every note — the "typewriter" hand — is
slow, and it lands late and out of tune.

## How a finger moves

It **drops from the base knuckle**, curved, landing on the **tip** near the nail — like a
small hammer, straight down. Not the pad (the fleshy part lets the pitch wander, because the
notes are so close together). Not a twist from the side.

**Minimum pressure.** The string only has to reach the fingerboard — that's a light touch,
not a squeeze. If the base of your first finger or your thumb is clenching, the hand freezes.

## And fingers stay down

When you play a higher note, **leave the fingers behind it down** on the string. Going back
down the string, they're already there — no travel, no re-aiming.

## The 1↔3 drill

Fingers 1 and 3, back and forth on the D string — **keep the 2nd finger hovering** just above
the string the whole time, and keep 1 down when 3 goes on. This is Suzuki's "jumping finger".

## The other silent drill

One more, away from any tune: **hold all four fingers down** on the A string, then lift and
tap one at a time — 1, 2, 3, 4, then 4, 3, 2, 1 — keeping the other three planted. It isolates
each finger's independence without the bow.

## The usual mistake

Lifting the whole hand off between notes, or lifting fingers you don't need to lift. Also
**pressing hard** — a squeezed finger can't drop quickly and can't make the small in-tune
adjustment.

<TryThis>
  **Left-hand pizzicato:** with the bow down, pluck the A string with your 4th finger — a
  quick, firm little flick. Then the 3rd, the 2nd, the 1st. It teaches the drop, and it builds
  the strength gently.
