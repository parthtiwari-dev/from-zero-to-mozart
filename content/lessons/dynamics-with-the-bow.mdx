Back in Stage 3 you learned to *read* `pp` through `ff`, hairpins, `sf`, `subito`. And one
lesson there — `dynamics-are-a-bow-thing` — planted a flag: **on a violin, loud and soft are
bow decisions, not a volume knob.** This is where you make them.

## The two recipes

Everything you know about the three controls (`speed-weight-and-sounding-point`), pointed at
dynamics:

| | louder (**→ f**) | softer (**→ p**) |
|---|---|---|
| bow speed / amount | **faster / more bow** | slower / less bow |
| weight | **more** arm weight | lighter |
| sounding point | toward the **bridge** (lane 2–3) | toward the **fingerboard** (lane 3–4) |
| hair | **flat** (all of it) | tilted (less) |

All four move **together**, in the same direction. That's the physics from
`speed-weight-and-sounding-point`: the controls are a set, and a dynamic is "move the whole
set one way".

## Crescendo: two situations, two methods

- **Across a phrase** (a hairpin over several notes or bars): **use gradually more bow** per
  note into the crescendo, gradually less into the diminuendo. Weight and lane drift along
  with it.
- **On one long held note** (a hairpin on a single note): you **can't** just speed the bow up
  — you'd run out. Instead, keep the bow speed roughly even and **add weight and slide toward
  the bridge** for the crescendo, then release for the diminuendo. (That's *messa di voce* —
  its own lesson.)

## `sf` and `fp` are a bow attack

A **sforzando** (`sf`, `sfz`) is a **prepared bite** on that one note — the front end of a
martelé (`martele`) — then straight back to the prevailing dynamic. **`fp`** is that bite, then
instantly soft. You already have the gesture; here it lands on a single marked note in a line.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Open D, one long note: play it p (slow, light, over the fingerboard), then the same note f
  (fast, weighted, near the bridge), then a smooth crescendo from one to the other across a
  slow count of eight. Then take a tune you know and add a dynamic scheme with the bow — a
  quiet first phrase, a loud answer.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to crescendo a **long held note by speeding the bow up** — you reach the tip with half
the note still to play and nothing left to give. On one note, crescendo with **weight and
lane**; save the bow-speed method for **across** notes. The other mistake: making a dynamic
with weight alone — pressing for *f*, which just chokes the tone (`speed-not-pressure`).

<Cite source="Ivan Galamian, Principles (1962) — bow speed as the tool of nuance; The Strad, '10 views on dynamics for bowed strings'">
  Galamian: "the climax note should have more bow, while the closing note, tapering off as it
  ought to, will have less." Dynamics on a string instrument are, first and last, a decision
  about how the bow moves.
</Cite>
