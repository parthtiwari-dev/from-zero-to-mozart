Scales are built out of just two sizes of step. Get these two straight and the rest of the
stage falls into place.

A **half step** (or *semitone*) is the smallest ordinary step in Western music — one note to
its closest possible neighbour. A **whole step** (or *tone*) is two half steps. Steps are
**distances**, not notes: "whole" and "half" describe the gap *between* two notes, not the
notes themselves.

## Where the half steps hide

Here's the catch that makes scales interesting. On the musical alphabet — A B C D E F G — most
neighbours are a **whole** step apart, but two pairs are only a **half** step:

**E → F** and **B → C.**

There's no note between E and F, and none between B and C. Everywhere else — C→D, D→E, F→G,
G→A, A→B — you *can* fit a note between (that's what the sharps and flats are for), so those
gaps are whole steps.

## On the violin: fingers touching

You already know this shape from Stage 1's finger patterns. **Fingers apart = a whole step.
Fingers touching = a half step.** You feel the difference; there's nothing to see on the
fingerboard.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the D string, find every half step you can reach in first position: E→F (1st finger, then
  low 2nd against it), F♯→G (high 2nd against 3rd), G♯→A (high 3rd against 4th). Each time, feel
  the two fingers come together. Then play D→E, a whole step, and feel the gap.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Assuming every letter-to-next-letter step is the same size, and spacing your fingers evenly by
default. They aren't and you can't. E–F and B–C are *half* the size of the others, and that
built-in unevenness is the whole reason a scale has a shape — and the reason some keys need
sharps and some need flats.
