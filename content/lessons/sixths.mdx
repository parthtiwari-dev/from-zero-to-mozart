A bigger interval than a third — 8 or 9 semitones instead of 3 or 4 — and a hand shape most
players find needs a little more time to settle in. The interval itself — sixths, everywhere
in real harmony as inverted thirds — is worth the investment.

## Same tuning method, a bigger target to miss

The wiggle-to-lock check from `thirds` applies here too. A bigger interval gives the ear less
to compare the two notes against directly — it's easier to be a little off and not notice
until you actually listen for the beating.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the minor sixth, then the major sixth, keeping the 1st finger anchored on A the whole
  time and only adjusting the 2nd finger's low/high position. Feel how little that finger has to
  move for such a clear change in colour.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Tensing the hand while reaching for the low/high choice on the upper finger, instead of letting
it stay loose. A gripped hand can't make the small adjustment that tells major from minor —
tension there costs you precision, not just comfort.

  Sixths are described across the pedagogy literature as needing more hand flexibility than
  thirds in general — though exactly how much stretch any one sixth takes depends on the
  specific fingering, not just the interval name. Trust what your own hand feels, not an
  assumption about how wide it "should" be.
</Cite>
