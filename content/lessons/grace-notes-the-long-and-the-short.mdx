A **grace note** is a small-print note tucked in front of a full-size one. There are two
kinds, and the difference between them is a **rhythm** question: does the little note steal
time from the beat, or does it sneak in just before it?

## The two

| Name | Symbol | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **appoggiatura** ("leaning note") | a small note, **no slash** through the stem, slurred to the main note | lands **on** the beat, and **takes time from the main note** — the rule of thumb is **half** its value (**two-thirds** if the main note is dotted) |
| **acciaccatura** ("crushed note") | a small note **with a slash** through the stem | crushed in **before** the beat, as fast as possible, **steals no time** — the main note still lands squarely on its beat |

The appoggiatura is the important one. The weight goes **on the small note** — it's a
dissonance that leans, then resolves onto the main note. Often it matters more than the note
it "decorates."

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play D (a half note), then C (a quarter) into B (a quarter) — that's the appoggiatura, on
  the beat, C carrying the weight. Now play D, then a lightning C right before the beat and B
  full-length on the beat — that's the acciaccatura. Same two pitches, completely different
  rhythm.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing **every** small note fast and before the beat. That's the modern, Romantic-inherited
habit — and for **Mozart the default is on the beat**, taking real time. And giving the
appoggiatura no weight, so the sigh becomes a hiccup.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756), Ch. IX">
  His entire ninth chapter is the appoggiatura. His rule: a small note before a descending
  quarter, eighth or sixteenth is a *long* appoggiatura, taking half the value — on the beat,
  and leaned upon.
</Cite>
