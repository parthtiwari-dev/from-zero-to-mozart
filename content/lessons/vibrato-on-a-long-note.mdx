The first place vibrato actually earns its keep is the **long, held notes of a tune you already
play**. Not every note — the long ones, the ones with time to bloom, usually at the top of a
phrase or where it comes to rest. Short notes and open strings stay plain.

## Vibrato and the swell, together

Growing the *sound* of a long note (the messa di voce from Stage 5) and growing the *shimmer*
at the same time is one combined gesture — and it's exactly what Louis Spohr prescribed in
1832: vibrato "on long sustained notes with dynamic swells." The two belong together. A long
note that swells and shimmers and then settles is the most basic unit of lyrical playing.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take *Ode to Joy* or *Amazing Grace* — a tune you know cold. Find the two or three longest
  notes in it. Play the whole tune with vibrato on *only* those notes and everything else dead
  plain. It'll feel too sparse. It isn't — that's roughly how a Classical player would do it,
  and it already sounds like music instead of a scale.
</TryThis>

## Hear it

Listen to the opening of **Bach's *Air on the G String*** in almost any recording: the long
notes are alive with vibrato, the moving notes between them much less so. The shimmer marks the
arrivals.

## The usual mistake

Vibrating everything the instant you can vibrate at all — the line turns to soup and the long
notes lose their specialness. The opposite mistake, oddly common: vibrating the busy short
notes (where it barely registers) and leaving the long notes plain (where it would matter
most). Long notes first.

<Cite source="Louis Spohr, Violinschule (1832); this course's Stage 5 messa-di-voce">
  Spohr's rule — vibrato for "long sustained notes with dynamic swells" — is still how the
  device is introduced 190 years later. The swell and the vibrato are one gesture, not two.
</Cite>
