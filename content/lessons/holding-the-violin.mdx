The violin is **not held up by the left hand.** It's balanced between your collarbone and the
gentle weight of your head, with the shoulder rest bridging the gap underneath. That balance is
what frees the left hand to move.

## How it sits

- The back of the violin rests on your **collarbone**, tucked in against the neck.
- Your **jaw** settles onto the chin rest — which is really a *jaw* rest — just left of the
  tailpiece. Not clamped: the weight of your relaxed head is enough.
- The **scroll** points out to your left and slightly down, roughly at nose height.
- Tilt the violin so the strings are close to level — just enough that the bow can reach the
  G string without hitting your leg.

<Margin>
  **Shoulder rest:** fit it so the violin still touches your collarbone — it fills the gap so
  you don't lift your shoulder or clamp your head. It's not there to jack the violin up for a
  long neck at the cost of contact. If the violin slides down, the rest is too low.
</Margin>

## The "let go" test

If the setup is right, you can **take your left hand off the neck entirely** for a few seconds
and the violin stays put, held by your head. Try it now. If it droops, adjust the chin rest or
shoulder rest, or let your head actually rest its weight — don't just carry on.

## The usual mistake

The temptation is to make it *safe* by squeezing — jamming the violin between chin and
shoulder so it can't fall, or propping it up with the left hand.

- **Propping with the left hand** — then the hand can't move freely, and every shift and finger
  fights the job of holding the instrument.
- **Clamping the jaw down hard** — neck and jaw ache within minutes, and the tension travels
  straight down the arm into the bow.
- **Hiking the shoulder** or letting the **scroll droop** toward the floor.

Aim for the lightest contact that still holds it. You'll spend months refining this, and so
does everyone.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Violinschule (1756), Ch. II">
  gives two ways of holding the violin and shows the one used since: against the neck, the jaw
  over the tailpiece side. Plenty of great players — Heifetz, Milstein, Menuhin — used no
  shoulder rest at all; it's a fitting question, not a rule.
</Cite>
