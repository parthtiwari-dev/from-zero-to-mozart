The vibrato pulse can come from two places: the **forearm**, or the **hand rocking at the
wrist**. They produce slightly different sounds, and — this is the honest part — **teachers
genuinely disagree about which to learn first.** This course teaches both, and lets your hand
find whichever comes more naturally.

## The two motions

<VibratoMotionDiagram />

**Arm (forearm) vibrato.** The impulse comes from the forearm; the whole hand-and-wrist unit
swings together, pivoting roughly at the elbow. It's the **bigger** motion, which makes it
easier to feel and control when you're starting — you can *sense* the forearm moving. It tends
to sound warm and can go wide. The risk: if the swing gets too big it can knock the violin or
transmit into the bow arm.

**Wrist (hand) vibrato.** The forearm stays comparatively still; the hand rocks back and forth
at the wrist joint, the **base knuckles** (where the fingers meet the hand) leading the motion.
The fingertip stays planted on the string but supple. It's often described as even and able to
go fast. It needs a wrist that doesn't collapse inward — which is why the readiness check
mattered.

In both, the fingertip **rocks from more on its tip to more on its pad and back** — that
rolling of the contact point is what actually bends the pitch.

## Which one first?

There's no settled answer. Some respected teachers start every student on **arm** vibrato
because the larger motion is easier to feel. Others teach **wrist** as the core motion from the
start. Galamian's textbook lays out arm, hand and finger vibrato without prescribing an order.
Simon Fischer teaches both.

So: try the arm motion for a few minutes, then the wrist motion. **Whichever feels less
forced, start there** — and come back to the other one later. Most players end up using a blend
of both anyway. Henryk Szeryng's line was that "a perfect vibrato is a combination of finger,
wrist and forearm."

**Hear the two colours** in recordings: arm vibrato tends to sound rounder and broader (much of
the old Russian-school playing — early Oistrakh); wrist vibrato can sound tighter and quicker.
But the difference is subtle and most great players blend them — don't chase it, just find the
motion that's loose.

## The usual mistake

Trying to *hold* the forearm perfectly still to do "pure wrist vibrato," which locks the wrist
and kills the motion — or letting the whole arm shake when you meant to rock the hand. Isolate
gently, not rigidly. And forcing yourself onto the type a video told you was "correct" when
your hand clearly prefers the other — the loose one is the right one.
