Two shapes that bend the "strong beat on beat one" default without breaking the pulse itself.

## Pickup notes (anacrusis)

Some pieces don't start on beat one — they start with a short **pickup**, an incomplete first
bar that leads into the first full strong beat. *Ode to Joy* and *Twinkle* both do this.

<NotatedExample
  abc={`X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C clef=treble
D | E F G A | B4 |]`}
  caption="A single pickup note before the first complete bar. Count it as 'and-a-4' leading into beat one of the next bar, or simply feel it as an upbeat."
  defaultBpm={72}
/>

The pickup changes **how you count the phrase**, not the pitches — you just need to notice the
short first bar before you start, so you don't miscount where "beat one" actually is.

## Syncopation

Syncopation puts emphasis somewhere **other** than the expected strong beat — often on an
off-beat "and", or by tying into a beat rather than restriking it. It's the rhythmic surprise
that makes a line feel alive instead of mechanical.

<NotatedExample
  abc={`X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C clef=treble
z/2D/2 D D/2E/2 z |]`}
  caption="A rest on the downbeat throws the accent onto the off-beat that follows — the note arrives a half-beat 'early' relative to where you'd expect it."
  defaultBpm={66}
/>

Both are common in fiddle tunes and pop arrangements alike — this is REFERENCE for now; you'll
meet real examples once repertoire starts in Stage 9.
