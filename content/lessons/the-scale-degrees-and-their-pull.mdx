Every note of the scale has a **number** (1 to 7, then 8 = 1 again) and a **name**. The names
aren't decoration — each one tells you what that note *does* in the key. Write the number with
a little roof on it: **^1, ^2, ^3** … that's the standard shorthand for "scale degree one, two,
three".

| Degree | Name | Why that name |
|---|---|---|
| ^1 | **Tonic** | *the* tone — home, and the key is named after it |
| ^2 | **Supertonic** | "just above the tonic" |
| ^3 | **Mediant** | "in the middle" — halfway between tonic and dominant; it's also the note that makes a key major or minor |
| ^4 | **Subdominant** | "the dominant below" — a fifth *below* the tonic |
| ^5 | **Dominant** | the second-most important note after the tonic — a fifth above, the note the harmony pulls through on the way home |
| ^6 | **Submediant** | "the mediant below" — a third below the tonic |
| ^7 | **Leading tone** | it *leads* to the tonic — a half step below it, and it aches to go up |

The names come in mirror pairs around the tonic: **dominant** is a fifth up, **subdominant** a
fifth down; **mediant** a third up, **submediant** a third down. The scale is symmetrical in
its *naming*, even though it isn't in its steps.

## Hear it — the notes that rest, and the notes that lean

Three of the seven are **stable**: ^1, ^3, ^5 — the notes of the tonic chord. Land on one and
a phrase can stop there.

The other four — ^2, ^4, ^6, ^7 — **lean**, and mostly toward their nearest stable neighbour:
**^7 → ^1** (up — the strongest pull of all), **^4 → ^3** (down), **^2 → ^1** (down),
**^6 → ^5** (down).

## The usual mistake

Reading ^7 as "just the note before the top". It's the **leading tone** — a half step from
home, the most restless note in the key, and the engine behind almost every "we've arrived"
moment in music. Composers *raise* it in minor keys precisely to get that half-step pull
(you'll see that in the minor scales). Treat it with respect: when you land on ^7, something
has to happen next.

<Cite source="the technical names — theory tradition; 'dominant' and 'subdominant' from Jean-Philippe Rameau, Traité de l'harmonie (1722)">
  Rameau worked out that the chords a fifth above and a fifth below the tonic do most of the
  harmonic work in a key — so those degrees got the strongest names. "Leading note" is even
  more direct: it's the note that leads your ear to the tonic.
</Cite>
