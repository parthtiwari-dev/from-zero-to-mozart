Every major scale — in every key — is built from the **same recipe** of whole (W) and half (H)
steps, starting from the tonic:

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H**

Learn that one pattern and you never have to memorise a key's notes again. You *walk* the
pattern from any starting note and it hands you the scale — sharps, flats and all.

## It's two matching halves

Split the scale in the middle: **C D E F** and **G A B C**. Each is a **tetrachord** ("four
notes"), and each has the *same* internal shape — **W W H**. A whole step joins them.

On the violin this is literally true: in G major the lower tetrachord (G A B C) and the upper
tetrachord (D E F♯ G) use the **same first-position hand shape** — the lower one on the G
string, the upper one on the D string, one string higher. The scale is one hand shape, stacked.

## Build one on any note

Start on **G** and walk the formula:

G –(W)→ A –(W)→ B –(H)→ **C** –(W)→ D –(W)→ E –(W)→ **F♯** –(H)→ G

The formula *demanded* an F♯: to make the step from the 7th degree up to the tonic a **half**
step, plain F wasn't close enough — it had to be raised. Nobody had to tell you G major has one
sharp; the recipe produced it.

## Hear it — what happens if you break the recipe

Play C major, then play it again with the **7th degree lowered** — B♭ instead of B. The last
step is now a whole step, not a half, and the scale stops sounding "major" — it goes flat and
folk-ish (that's actually a different scale, the *Mixolydian* mode, but that's for later).

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Build D major out loud, walking the formula from D: D –W→ E –W→ F♯ –H→ G –W→ A –W→ B –W→ C♯
  –H→ D. Say "whole, whole, half…" as you go. You should find *two* sharps — F♯ and C♯. Then
  play it, first position: D E F♯ G on the D string, A B C♯ D on the A string.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Learning each key's notes as a separate list to memorise. There's one recipe. If you can walk
W-W-H-W-W-W-H from a starting note, you can spell any major scale — and later, work out its key
signature — without looking anything up.

<Cite source="the major scale = the Ionian mode, named by Heinrich Glarean, Dodecachordon (1547)">
  Glarean added "Ionian" to the list of church modes to account for the bright, folk-flavoured
  scale that was taking over secular music. Over the next 150 years it became *the* reference
  scale, and Western harmony has been built on it since.
</Cite>
