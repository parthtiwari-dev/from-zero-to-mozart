Some notes, played in tune, make the whole instrument light up — the sound blooms and keeps
going after the bow has moved on. That's **sympathetic resonance**, and it's a free intonation
check built into the violin.

## How it works

Your open strings — G, D, A, E — are always sitting there ready to vibrate. When you play a
stopped note that **matches** one of them, or matches a strong **overtone** of one (from the
harmonic series), that open string picks up the vibration through the bridge and **rings along
on its own**, even though you never touched it. The note you're playing gets fuller and
louder.

If your note is even slightly out of tune, the open string *doesn't* catch — so the ring is a
yes/no signal: **it rings → you're in tune with your instrument.**

## The ring-tone map — first position

These are the notes that ring, string by string:

| String | Ringing notes |
|---|---|
| **G string** | G, A, B |
| **D string** | D, E, G |
| **A string** | A, B, D |
| **E string** | E, F♯, A |

(The A's, D's and G's ring because they match open strings; **B rings** because it's a strong
overtone of the open G.)

## Use it as you practise scales

Play a G major scale slowly and, on the ringing notes (B, D, E, the top G), pause and check
for the bloom. If a note is a little flat or sharp, the ring dies — nudge the finger until it
comes back.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the D string, play the octave D (3rd finger, up near halfway) and move it by hair's
  breadths until the open D underneath rings as loudly as possible. Memorise where your finger
  is. Then do the same for A on the D string against the open A, and B on the G string against
  the open G (that one's an overtone match, subtler — listen carefully). Your instrument is
  teaching you where the notes live.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Not listening for it — playing scales fast and mechanically so the resonance never has a
chance to speak. Slow down. The ring is the single best free feedback you have, and it works
in every sharp key. It's also, not coincidentally, how a Hindustani player checks a swara
against the tanpura.

  Suzuki built a whole practice — *tonalization* — around finding and sustaining the ringing
  tone. It's the fastest way to build a hand that lands in tune without tapes or a tuner.
</Cite>
