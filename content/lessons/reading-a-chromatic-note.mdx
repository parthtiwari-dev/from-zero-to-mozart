A note with an accidental in front of it — a sharp, flat or natural that isn't in the key
signature — looks like a hazard the first few times. It isn't. A chromatic note **almost
always leans somewhere**, and once you know where it's leaning you can read it at full speed
without breaking the pulse.

## A chromatic note is a half step from a note you already know

You don't read C♯ as a brand-new pitch. You read it as **"the C, nudged up a half step"** —
one finger, creeping toward the bridge (high 2 instead of low 2 on the A string). The eye
sees the notehead you know plus a small instruction. That's much faster than treating it as
an unrelated note.

## It's going to resolve — predict where

Most accidentals are one of three shapes (all met in Stage 4):

- **A raised leading tone** — ♯4 pulling to 5, or the raised 7th in a minor key. It *aches*
  upward by a half step. Sing the resolution before you play the accidental.
- **A chromatic passing tone** — filling the gap between two scale notes a whole step apart
  (C → C♯ → D). It keeps moving in the same direction.
- **A ♭6 or ♭2** — a note bent *down*, borrowed from the minor. It sags toward 5, or toward 1.

## Read it without stopping the beat

The accidental sits *in* the pulse, not outside it. When you pre-read, mark the accidentals —
circle them in your mind — and rehearse the finger move (low 2 → high 2) before you start.
Then when you arrive, the pulse carries you through it. The accidental only holds for the
rest of that bar; the bar-line puts the note back to its key spelling, and a courtesy natural
in the next bar is just a reminder.

## The usual mistake

Freezing at the accidental — stopping to work out "what note is that" — and dropping the
beat. The other one: playing the accidental and then *forgetting it's cancelled at the
bar-line*, so you keep the sharp into the next bar. Accidental lasts to the bar-line, then
the key signature takes over again.
