The **blues scale** is the minor pentatonic with **one note added** — a **♭5**, the "blue
note." Six notes, and it carries the whole vocal, bent, aching sound of the blues.

## From minor pentatonic to blues

Minor pentatonic: ^1 ♭^3 ^4 ^5 ♭^7. Add a **♭5** between the ^4 and the ^5, and you have the
blues scale: **^1 ♭^3 ^4 ♭^5 ^5 ♭^7.**

## The blue note is meant to be bent

In real blues playing, the ♭5 (and often the ♭3 and ♭7) aren't fixed pitches — they're
**bent**, slid into, played a little sharp or flat of dead centre. On the violin you can
actually do this: place the ♭5 slightly low and slide up toward the ^5, or add a wide, slow
finger vibrato. A blue note played dead in tune barely sounds blue.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the A string, play B♭ (1st finger, pulled back), C (low 2), then slide your 2nd finger up
  a fraction toward D — don't stop cleanly, just smear through — then back down. Keep it loose
  and slightly out of time. That slippery, vocal quality is the blues scale doing its job.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing the blues scale stiff and metronomic, every note in tune and on the beat. The blues is
about **bend, slide and behind-the-beat phrasing** — the notes are a starting point, not the
whole story. A classically-trained ear wants to clean it up; resist that.
