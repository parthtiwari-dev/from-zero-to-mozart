You've spent four stages learning to *name* everything on a page — every note, every rhythm,
every symbol. This stage is about doing it **fast, at a steady pulse, without stopping** — the
difference between *working out* a line and *reading* it. Here's what actually changes.

## Fluent readers don't read notes — they read patterns

Watch someone sight-read well and it looks like magic. It isn't. Their eyes are doing one
thing: **grouping notes into shapes they already know** — a scale run, a broken chord, a
familiar rhythm, a phrase that repeats a step higher — and reading each shape as *one thing*,
not as five separate notes. A beginner reads `G–A–B–C–D` as five decisions. A fluent reader
reads it as "a scale, going up" — one decision.

This is called **chunking**, and it's the whole skill. Everything in Movement A is a way of
learning to *see* the shapes: the scale fragment, the arpeggio, the turn, the sequence, the
leap, the rhythmic cell.

## The eye runs ahead of the hand

While you're playing one note, your eyes are already reading the next few — a **skilled
violinist reads about three to six notes ahead**, and *shrinks* that lead when the music gets
hard. Beginners read one note ahead, or zero. Learning to let the eyes drift forward — onto a
long note, into a rest — is `your-eye-runs-ahead`.

One thing the research is clear about: **"look as far ahead as you can" is bad advice.** The
lead should be *small* — a beat or two — and flexible. Too far ahead overloads you.

## Sight-reading is its own skill — practised easy, and every day

You don't get good at sight-reading by sight-reading hard music badly. You get good by reading
a **large volume of easy music fluently** — something new every day, always a level or two
*below* what you're learning to perform, one look then one go. `the-sight-reading-habit` is
the routine.

## The usual mistake

Believing fluency is about **finger speed**. It almost never is — the bottleneck is how fast
your eye names a note and groups a pattern. A player whose fingers can fly still stumbles if
the eye is decoding letter by letter. The next lessons train the eye.

<Cite source="Kopiez & Lee, 'Towards a general model of skills involved in sight reading music'; eye-tracking research on musical expertise (reviewed in 'Eye-Hand Span in Sight-Reading of Music', 2021)">
  Sight-reading skill breaks down into three trainable parts: **pattern recognition**,
  **prediction** (anticipating where the line goes), and **chunking** (holding the pattern,
  not the notes). Experts have all three; they're not born with them.
</Cite>
