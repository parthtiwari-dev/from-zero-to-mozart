A tempting idea, and a wrong one: that vibrato makes a note "sound musical," so a slightly
sour note with vibrato on it will pass. It won't. **Vibrato wobbles whatever is underneath
it.** A flat note with vibrato is a flat note that also shakes. Worse — vibrato hides the
error from *your own ear*, so you stop noticing it and stop fixing it.

## Why it hides the error

Your ear judges a wobbling pitch by its average. A slow, wide wobble smears that average across
a range wide enough that a 10-cent error disappears inside it — you can't hear your own
mistake any more. This is the exact reason Stage 1 said *play the note cleanly, without
vibrato, while you learn where it lives* (`landing-in-tune`): the clean note is the one your
ear can check.

## The order is always: centre first, then shimmer

Place the note. Check it — against a ringing open string, a drone, the pitch in your head.
*Then*, once it's genuinely in tune, add the vibrato around that centre. Never the other way
round. Even concert players practise scales and hard passages **senza vibrato** precisely to
keep their intonation honest — the vibrato goes back on afterwards.

This is also why vibrato waits until now. Playing by ear (`PRD.md` §3) depends on the loop of
*play a guess, hear the error, correct it.* Vibrato breaks that loop if you add it too early —
you stop hearing the errors.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take any short phrase you know. Play it through with no vibrato at all, slowly, fixing every
  note against an open string or a drone until it's genuinely clean. Only then play it again
  with vibrato on the long notes. If the vibrato'd version sounds worse, the notes weren't
  actually in tune — go back to the plain version.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reaching for vibrato on *every* note, early, to sound expressive — which is both a technical
trap (your intonation quietly rots) and, historically, a style fault. Leopold Mozart's
complaint about players who "tremble on every note as if they had the palsy" is the next
lesson; the point here is simpler: vibrato is a choice you add to a note that is *already*
right.

<Cite source="the source course, Part VI §68; Leopold Mozart, Violinschule (1756); Carl Flesch, The Art of Violin Playing (1923)">
  The source book states it plainly: "Do not use vibrato as a way to make an uncertain note
  sound musical. First learn the center of the pitch." Flesch and every teacher since have
  said the same — vibrato is added to a secure note, never used to prop up an insecure one.
</Cite>
