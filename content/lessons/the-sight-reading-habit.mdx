Nobody sight-reads well from a burst of effort. It's built the way a language is built — **a
little, every day, for a long time.** This lesson is the routine.

## The daily five minutes

At the **start** of every practice session, before your ear tires and your standards slip:

- **5–10 minutes**, no more.
- **Something you have never seen.**
- **A level or two below** the pieces you're learning to perform.
- **One pre-read, one go, no stopping.** Then judge it against the pass condition
  (`how-sight-reading-cards-work`), diagnose if it failed, and move on.
- **Nudge the level up only when a whole level goes cleanly at a slow click** — not before.

Over months, this is the whole difference. Great sight-readers aren't born with fast eyes;
they've read an enormous *volume* of music, mostly easy, mostly once.

## Prepare with scales

A quiet but real link: a **scale is the finger map of a key**, and an **arpeggio is the
common leap pattern**. If you've drilled G / D / A / C scales and arpeggios (Stage 4
`which-scales-to-practise-now`, Stage 5 `stage-5-tone-and-strokes`), then a first-position
line in those keys is *already half-read* — your hand knows the shapes. That's why the
pre-read asks "what key — and do I know that scale?"

## The tools

- **The metronome bar** — set it slow. The click *is* the "no stopping" enforcer: it keeps
  moving whether you're ready or not, and it drags your eyes forward.
- **The card ladder** (`sight-reading-cards`) — your disposable daily stream.
- **When you run out of cards** — the ABRSM *Violin Sight-Reading Trainer* app (Grades 1–5),
  *Read Ahead*, *Sight Reading Factory*. Endless fresh graded material, which is exactly what
  the habit needs. Our cards teach the *method*; the apps sustain the *reps*.
- **Reading with someone** — a duet, a teacher playing along, a backing track. You physically
  *can't* stop when another part is going, so it's the best "keep going" trainer there is.
  Add it as soon as you have anyone to play with.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  For two weeks: first thing every practice, one new card from the ladder. Pre-read, one go,
  slow click, no stopping. Write down whether it passed. Don't replay, don't perfect it — next
  day, next card. At the end of two weeks look at your pass rate. Then decide whether to move
  up a level.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Treating sight-reading as an occasional test — reading a hard piece once every few weeks,
badly, and calling it practice. That builds nothing. The other mistake: making it long and
serious. Five focused minutes of easy fresh material, daily, beats an hour of grinding once a
week — the same rule as scales (`how-to-practise-a-scale`) and bow strokes
(`how-to-practise-a-bow-stroke`).
