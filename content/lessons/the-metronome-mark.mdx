Sometimes, next to the tempo word — or instead of it — there's a tiny equation: a note symbol,
an equals sign, a number. That's a **metronome mark**, and unlike *Allegro* it is exact.

## Reading it

**♩ = 92** means: a quarter note, ninety-two times a minute. Set a metronome to 92 and every
click is one quarter note of the piece.

- The note on the left is the unit you're counting. **♩ = 60** counts in quarters; **♪ = 120**
  in eighths; in compound time you'll see **♩. = 50** — a *dotted* quarter fifty times a
  minute, one click per big beat of a 6/8 bar.
- Sometimes **M.M.** sits in front — *Maelzel's Metronome*, after the man who sold the device.
- A range (**♩ = 88–96**) means "somewhere in here"; a word plus a mark (*Andante, ♩ = 76*)
  gives you the character *and* the number.

## The usual mistake

Trusting an old mark too much. **Beethoven's own metronome marks are famously disputed** —
often faster than seems playable — and the argument (a faulty device, or a different way of
reading the scale) is still unsettled. Treat a Romantic-era mark as strong evidence, not law.

And the bigger one: **Mozart, Haydn and everything Classical have no metronome marks at all** —
the machine didn't exist. If you see **♩ = 100** on an edition of the Mozart concerto, that's
the *editor's* suggestion, not Mozart's (Movement H — editions — comes back to this). For that
music the tempo word and the metre are the real guide.

<Cite source="Grove, on the metronome and Maelzel">
  Johann Nepomuk Maelzel patented the metronome in 1815 — building on a pendulum design by
  Dietrich Nikolaus Winkel. Beethoven, from 1817, was the first major composer to write marks
  into his scores; every composer before him, Mozart included, had none to write.
</Cite>
