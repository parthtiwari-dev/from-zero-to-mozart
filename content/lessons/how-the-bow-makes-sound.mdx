You've been making a sound with the bow since Stage 0. Now it's worth knowing *how*, because
every stroke in this stage is a different way of steering the one thing that's actually
happening between the hair and the string.

## The one thing that happens

The hair of the bow is coated in rosin, which makes it sticky. Drag it across the string and
it **catches** the string and pulls it sideways. The string stretches, its tension builds,
and at some point it **snaps back** the other way — where the hair catches it again and pulls
it back. Catch, pull, release, catch. On the open A string that cycle repeats **440 times a
second**, and that repeating tug is the note.

The bow doesn't "put sound into" the string so much as keep a swing going — like pushing a
child on a swing, one small shove per pass.

## The whole string moves — and wider means louder

The blur is widest halfway between the bridge and your finger (or the nut). The **wider** that
swing, the more the sound carries across a room. And here's the part that catches beginners
out: **the pitch never changes with the width.** Loud and soft are the *same* 440 swings a
second — the string just travels further side to side when it's loud.

## One note is really a stack of notes

When the string swings, it doesn't only vibrate as one long band — it also vibrates in halves,
thirds, quarters, all at once, each adding a faint higher note called a **partial** (this is
Stage 4's harmonic series — `the-harmonic-series`, `why-your-violin-rings`). You don't hear
them separately; together they're the *colour* of the sound. Changing where and how the bow
meets the string changes which partials come through — which is why the next few lessons are
about exactly that.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, play one very slow whole bow and just look at the string. Use more bow
  (faster) and watch the blur widen; use less bow and watch it narrow. Then try pressing down
  harder without moving faster — notice the blur gets *thinner* and the sound gets rougher,
  not louder. The bow's job is to keep the string swinging freely, not to squash it.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Believing "louder means press harder." Pressing down works *against* the sideways swing — past
a point it chokes the string and the tone turns scratchy and small. Loudness comes mostly from
a **wider swing**, and a wider swing comes mostly from a **faster bow** (more bow per note).
The next lesson, `speed-not-pressure`, is entirely about this.

<Cite source="Hermann von Helmholtz, On the Sensations of Tone (1863); described by Simon Fischer as an ultra-slow-motion film of the bow">
  Helmholtz was the first to describe the "catch and release" cycle precisely, and it's still
  called *Helmholtz motion*. Fischer's image for students: imagine the string and the hair
  magnified huge and filmed in slow motion — you'd watch the hair grip the string, drag it,
  and let it spring back, over and over.
</Cite>
