Everything the right arm does comes down to three variables, and learning to hear how they
trade against each other is most of bow technique.

- **Bow speed** — how fast the hair travels across the string.
- **Bow weight** — how much of the arm's weight rests into the string (through a relaxed hand
  — you *lean*, you don't press).
- **Contact point** — where the hair meets the string, between the bridge and the fingerboard.

Galamian's teaching framed it simply: those three are always in balance. Near the bridge, the
string needs more weight and/or slower speed to speak cleanly — get it wrong and it whistles or
crushes. Near the fingerboard, it wants less weight and often more speed, and the sound turns
soft and flute-like. The "middle lane" is the forgiving default you started with in Stage 0.

You also control **how much hair** touches the string (tilt the stick for less, flat for more)
and the **straightness** of the bow (staying parallel to the bridge).

None of this is a stroke to learn — it's the underlying physics that every stroke on the
following pages is a particular *use* of. When a stroke won't work, the fix is almost always
one of these three variables, not a new technique.

<TryThis>
  On one open string, play a slow whole bow and deliberately drift the contact point from near
  the fingerboard toward the bridge and back. Listen to the sound change. Find the spot where
  it's fullest and most stable.
