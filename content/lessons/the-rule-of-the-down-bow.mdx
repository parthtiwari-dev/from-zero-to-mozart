Here's a genuine rule — one with a clear reason and a little arithmetic, which should suit the
way you like to learn. **The strong beat wants a down-bow.**

## Why

A down-bow is naturally the **heavier, stronger** stroke: gravity and the weight of your arm
both help it. An up-bow is naturally lighter. So it makes sense to line them up with the
music: the **first beat of the bar**, and any accented note, falls on a **⊓** (down-bow); weak
beats and upbeats take the **V** (up-bow).

French players called it *"la règle du tiré"* — the rule of the down-bow — and Lully's
orchestra at Versailles was famous for playing every downbeat down-bow in perfect unison.
Leopold Mozart put it plainly in 1756: *a bar that doesn't begin with a rest should begin with
a down-bow.*

## The arithmetic

If you know a passage should **arrive on a down-bow** at the next barline, you can count
backwards:

- An **even** number of notes before the barline → start that group **down-bow** (down, up,
  down, up … lands you on a down at the bar).
- An **odd** number → start **up-bow** (so the count still lands on a down).

When the maths doesn't work out — the phrase would put a strong beat on an up-bow — you fix it
with one of the moves you already have:

- a **retake** (two down-bows in a row, `hooked-bowing-and-retakes`),
- a **hooked bowing** (two notes one direction),
- or **choosing a slur** that re-groups the notes.

## When to bend it

Modern playing breaks the rule all the time for musical reasons — a phrase might *want* to
start soft on an up-bow, or a slur might carry the strong beat. The rule isn't a law; it's the
**first question** you ask when you bow a passage yourself: *does the strong beat land
down-bow?* If yes, you're probably fine. If no, you need a reason, or a fix.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take any tune you know in 3/4 or 4/4. Bow it "by the rule": every bar starts down-bow, and
  you alternate from there, using a retake across the barline when you end a bar on a down-bow.
  Feel how the downbeats come out naturally stronger. Then find a spot where the rule fights
  the music, and decide what you'd do about it.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Following the rule so rigidly that the music suffers — forcing a delicate upbeat to be a heavy
down-bow because "bar one is down". The rule serves the phrasing, not the other way round. The
opposite mistake: never thinking about bow direction at all, so strong beats land on weak
up-bows and the whole passage sounds limp.
