The **chromatic scale** is **all twelve notes** — every half step, in order, with no note left
out. It has no tonic and no key: it's not music you build a tune on, it's a **colour** and a
**connector**.

## How it's fingered

Two common ways, both first position:

- **The creeping fingers** — slide the same finger up a half step (0 → low 1 → 1, then 1 → low
  2 → 2 …). Compact and even.
- **The chromatic slide** — 1–2–3 with the *same* finger sliding for the chromatic steps
  between. Faster, more glissando-like.

Either way, the notes are **very close together** — this is the tightest your fingers ever
sit.

## When composers use it

- **as a connector** — filling the gap between two notes of the melody with the steps between;
- **for tension and colour** — a chromatic line under a held chord winds the harmony up;
- **for a slither** — a fast chromatic run up or down is pure gesture (a rocket, a fall, a
  shiver).

Because it's all twelve notes, the chromatic scale belongs to no key — which is exactly why a
composer reaches for it when they want to *leave* a key, or blur the sense of one.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the D string, play D (open), then 1st finger pulled right back to D♯/E♭, then 1st finger
  in its normal E place, then 2nd finger back for F, then 2nd finger forward for F♯, then 3rd
  finger for G. Six notes, tiny steps. Go slowly and listen — each note should be a genuine
  half step, evenly spaced, not a smear.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing it as a smear — sliding one finger continuously instead of placing twelve distinct
half steps. Each note of a chromatic scale is a real pitch; it should sound like a ladder with
twelve close rungs, not like a siren. Keep the fingers active.

  Bach used chromatic lines for grief ("chromatic fourth" laments); Wagner dissolved whole
  keys in chromatic motion; Schoenberg eventually treated all twelve notes as equals. It's the
  scale that contains every other scale.
</Cite>
