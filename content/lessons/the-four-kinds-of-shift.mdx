Every shift is an arm move (previous lesson) — but *how* the finger travels during that move
comes in four recognised flavours. This is Galamian's classification, and it's the vocabulary
every teacher and method book uses.

## The four types

| Type | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Classical / French shift** | Slide on the **old** (departing) finger to the new spot, *then* place the new finger. Cleanest and most controllable — the default while you're learning. |
| **Combination shift** | Slide part of the way on the old finger, then swap to the new finger for the rest of the glide. A hybrid, used when neither pure type sounds right. |
| **Romantic / Russian shift** | Slide on the **new** (arriving) finger from the very start — you commit to it immediately and glide on it. More audible by nature; an expressive choice as much as a mechanical one. |
| **Delayed shift** | The new finger lands on the new note first, and the *rest* of the hand catches up into the full new position afterward. Used for fast, clean shifts where you don't want any glide heard at all. |

You'll use the **classical shift** almost everywhere at first — it's the one that keeps you
in control while your ear and arm learn the distances. The other three are refinements you'll
reach for once shifting itself feels ordinary.

## The guide note

Whichever finger is doing the sliding passes through the pitches in between on its way — that
passing pitch is the **guide note**. It's not meant to be *heard* as a separate note (that
line is drawn precisely in the next-but-one lesson, `shift-glissando-or-portamento`) — but your
ear uses it to check the shift is tracking correctly, the same way you'd glance at a road sign
without stopping to read it.

**Same-finger shifts** (like the one above) are the simplest case — one finger, start to
finish. **Different-finger shifts** — where the note you land on is played by a different
finger than the one that started the shift — are harder, because the hand has to travel *and*
hand off the job to a new finger. The guide note matters even more there, since it's your one
check that the hand actually arrived where the ear expected.

## The usual mistake

Treating "shift" as one single technique. A shift that's too audible when it should be silent
(using Romantic style by accident, out of sloppiness rather than choice) is just as much a
mistake as a shift that's too abrupt when the music wants an audible connection. Which type you
use is a decision, not an accident.
