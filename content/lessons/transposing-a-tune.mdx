**Transposing** a tune means moving every note up or down by the **same interval**. The
intervals *inside* the tune stay identical, the rhythm stays identical — only the pitch level
changes. It sounds like the same tune, in a different key.

## The tool is movable-do

Because solfège syllables (and sargam) stay glued to the scale degrees whatever the key, a
transposed tune is just **"the same syllables, a new home."** You saw this back in Stage 1:
the shape *do–re–mi–fa* played in G major, then the identical shape in A major.

## On the violin, it's a hand-shift

That's the practical version. Twinkle starting on the open D string is in D major; starting on
the open A string it's in A major — **same finger pattern, moved to the next pair of strings.**
A violinist "transposes up a fifth" by sliding the whole shape across.

## The written method

For reading (not playing by ear): pick the new tonic, work out the new key signature, and move
**every note** by the fixed interval — letter name *and* accidental — then check the notes that
aren't in the new signature.

## Why you'd do it

- to match a **singer's** comfortable range;
- to read a part written for a **B♭ instrument** (clarinet, trumpet), or play along with one;
- to brighten a tune by taking it **up a third**;
- to make a **hard key readable** by mentally shifting it to an easy one while sight-reading.

## Concert pitch and the C instruments

The violin is a **non-transposing, "C" instrument**: what you read is what sounds — a written
C sounds a C. Some instruments aren't: a **B♭ clarinet** reads a written C and sounds a B♭. The
convention lets a player switch between instruments of one family and keep the same fingerings.
You only need this to not be confused when a clarinettist says "my C is your B♭."

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play Hot Cross Buns starting on B (1st finger, A string) — that's in A major. Now play the
  identical finger pattern starting on E (1st finger, D string) — you've transposed it down a
  fourth, into D major. Same shape, same fingers, different string and different key. That's
  transposition on the violin.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Thinking transposition changes the tune. It doesn't — every interval and every rhythm is
untouched. Only the pitch level moves. If a transposed tune sounds *different* (beyond just
higher or lower), you've changed an interval somewhere by mistake.
