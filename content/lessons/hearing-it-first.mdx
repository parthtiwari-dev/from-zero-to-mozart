There's a single mental skill under both halves of Stage 8 — reading fluently *and* playing
by ear. It's called **audiation**: hearing music in your mind when the sound isn't there.

## Audiation is the mind's ear

When a tune "gets stuck in your head," you're audiating it — the sound is playing with no
speaker. Musicians do this on purpose and with control: they can hear a written line before
bowing it, hear the next phrase before they reach it, and hear a target melody clearly enough
to hunt for it on the string. It is not a gift a few people have. It's a trained loop:

1. **Imagine** the sound — a note, a phrase, where the line is going.
2. **Produce** it — sing it, or play it.
3. **Let the ear judge** — did what came out match what you imagined?
4. **Adjust** — and go round again.

Every rep sharpens the imagining. After enough of them, step 2 starts coming out right the
first time.

## Both jobs are the same loop

- **Reading:** you audiate the printed line — hear it off the page — *then* your hands
  confirm what you already heard. That's why `your-eye-runs-ahead` works: the eye feeds the
  inner ear, which feeds the hands.
- **Playing by ear:** you audiate the *target* — hold the tune clearly in your head — then
  hunt for it on the fingerboard, using the loop to correct each guess.

Reading is audiation from a symbol. Playing by ear is audiation from a memory. Same muscle.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Pick a tune you know cold — Happy Birthday, a nursery rhyme. Play the first note on the
  violin to set the pitch. Now, before playing another note, hear the *entire first line* in
  your head, as vividly as you can. Then play it. Where your fingers hesitated is where your
  audiation was fuzzy — go back and hear that bit again, clearly, before replaying.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reaching for the instrument before you've heard anything — letting the fingers guess and the
ear react. It's backwards, and it's slow. Hear it first, even roughly; then play. The other
mistake: expecting vivid, note-perfect inner hearing straight away. It starts vague and gets
sharper with reps, like a photo developing.

<Cite source="Edwin E. Gordon, Music Learning Theory — 'audiation'; the 'sound before sign' principle shared with Kodály">
  Gordon coined *audiation* for hearing and comprehending music not physically present, and
  argued it's to music what thought is to language — the thing all the other skills serve.
  His order is always **sound before sign**: hear it, then read or write it.
</Cite>
