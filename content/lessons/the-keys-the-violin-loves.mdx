Some keys make the violin **ring**. Some make it sound dull and boxed-in. It isn't random, and
it's the reason this whole course has lived in G, D and A.

## The open strings do the work

Your four open strings are **G, D, A, E**. When you play a stopped note that matches one of
them — or a strong overtone of one — that open string **vibrates along with it**, and the note
blooms and keeps sounding. This is *sympathetic resonance*.

So a key gets extra ring whenever its important notes line up with the open strings. **D major
is the most resonant key on the violin**, because *all four* open strings are useful degrees:

**G, D, A and E major** all ring (and so do G, D, A and E *minor* — the open strings don't
change). **Flat keys — D♭, A♭, G♭ — sound muted** by comparison: their tonics and dominants
have no open string to reinforce them, and the resonance shifts onto weaker degrees.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the A string, play D (3rd finger) and move it by tiny amounts until the open D string
  underneath rings loudest — that's the note perfectly in tune. Now do the same for A on the D
  string (4th finger) against the open A. The instrument is telling you when you've found the
  note.
</TryThis>

## Keys used to have personalities

Here's an old idea worth knowing. Before **equal temperament** flattened every key to the same
colour (see *In tune is not one thing*), keys had **characters**. In 1806, C. F. D. Schubart
wrote a whole table: **D major** was "the key of triumph, of hallelujahs, of victory-
rejoicing"; **C minor** was "the lament of unhappy love"; **E♭ major** "the key of love, of
devotion." Charpentier and Mattheson wrote similar lists a century earlier.

Some of that was pure convention, but some was real: on the unevenly-tuned instruments of the
day, D major genuinely *did* sound brighter than D♭. On the violin, a version of it survives —
D major really is more radiant than D♭, because of the open strings. "The symbol tells you
what; the era tells you how" applies to keys too.

## The usual mistake

Fighting a flat key instead of a sharp one when you have a choice — or, in a sharp key,
forgetting to *use* the open strings the key hands you. If a passage in D or A can take an open
string, let it: it locks your intonation and it rings.

<Cite source="C. F. D. Schubart, Ideen zu einer Ästhetik der Tonkunst (1806); violin resonance is standard string-playing knowledge; the 'Stradivari voiced for D major' claim is player folklore, not documented">
  Players have said for generations that the great makers voiced their instruments toward D
  major. Whether or not that's literally true, the open strings make it so: G, D, A and E are
  the violin's home keys, and the instrument sounds most alive in them.
</Cite>
