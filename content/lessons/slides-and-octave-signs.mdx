Two more page things. A **line** drawn between two notes means slide between them. And an
**8va** over a passage means "play this an octave away from where it's written."

## Glissando and portamento

| Mark | What it is | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| **glissando** — "gliss." + a **line** (straight or wavy) between two notes | slide the finger, sounding the pitches on the way | a full, deliberate slide — you hear the journey. Wavy = every step audible; straight = a smooth sweep |
| **portamento** — often **not written at all** | an expressive slide that *carries* one note into the next — shorter, subtler, starts late | a small vocal-style scoop, just catching the arrival note |

**Portamento is a Romantic technique.** Classical and Baroque violinists barely used it — a
slide you choose to add to the Mozart Adagio is a Romantic-flavoured decision of *yours*, not
something the page is asking for.

## Octave signs

| Mark | Means |
|---|---|
| **8va** (*all'ottava*), dotted line **above** a passage | play it **an octave higher** than written |
| **8vb** (*ottava bassa*), below | play it **an octave lower** |
| **15ma** | **two** octaves higher |
| **loco** ("in place") | cancel the octave sign — back to written pitch |

The note **names** never change — only the octave. These keep very high (or very low) passages
off a ladder of ledger lines. You'll lean on them properly in Stage 6, when the notes start
climbing past first position.

**One thing to expect:** a violin part almost never leaves **treble clef**. Where the music
goes very high it uses **8va**, not a switch to another clef — so don't sit waiting for a bass
clef that isn't coming.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the A string, play D with your 3rd finger, then slide that finger slowly down the string
  toward the open A — the bow keeping going, every pitch audible on the way. That's a
  glissando. Now do it as a portamento: hold the D, then a quick light slide catching only the
  last moment before the A. Two very different gestures from the same two notes.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Adding portamento to Classical music because it feels expressive — in Mozart it's a foreign
accent. Sliding a glissando too fast, so the journey vanishes. And reading an **8va** passage
at written pitch — an octave low — because you didn't spot the dotted line.

<Cite source="The Strad, on portamento; standard performance-practice literature">
  Portamento was called "the key to the Romantic style of violin playing." Early recordings —
  Kreisler, Ysaÿe — are full of it. In Classical and Baroque practice it was used sparingly if
  at all, and heavy sliding was considered a fault.
</Cite>
