The stroke you've spent this stage learning as "the default" — a long, even, sustained détaché
with an invisible bow change — was, in a real sense, **invented**. The bow itself changed
shape around 1785, and that change is why Baroque music and Classical-and-later music *feel*
different to play.

<Margin>
  REFERENCE. You'll almost certainly play a modern bow. But knowing what changed explains a
  lot about *why* early music is bowed the way it is.
</Margin>

## Two bows

| | **Baroque bow** (before ~1785) | **Modern (Tourte) bow** (after ~1785) |
|---|---|---|
| stick shape | **convex** or straight — curves *away* from the hair | **concave** — curves *toward* the hair |
| weight | lighter, shorter | heavier, longer |
| the tip | weak — a long note **naturally tapers** toward the point | strong — even hair tension all the way to the tip |
| what it does well | quick, articulated notes; separation without lifting; a speaking, gestural style | a **sustained, even, seamless** tone; big dynamics; a singing line |

## Why it matters

- **Baroque phrasing is articulated by default** partly *because of the bow*. A Baroque bow
  separates notes on its own — you don't have to lift. Long even lines were harder; short
  speaking gestures were natural. So Bach and Vivaldi are full of detail, inflection, and
  notes that "breathe" between them.
- **The modern bow was built for the opposite** — François Tourte designed it, working with
  the violinist **Viotti**, to make a long, powerful, seamless stroke possible. That's the
  sound of a Romantic concerto, and it's why Classical and later music can ask for a phrase
  that never seems to change bow.
- **The off-string strokes matter more in later music** because on a modern bow, getting
  Baroque-style separation now means you have to *lift* the bow (spiccato) rather than letting
  the bow do it for you.

## The keystone, one more time

This is the Stage 3 idea — *"the symbol tells you what, the era tells you how"* — reaching all
the way down to the equipment. A slur, a dot, an accent means one thing on the page; but *how*
you play it depends on the style, and the style was shaped in part by the tool. When you play
Bach on a modern bow, you're consciously doing what a Baroque bow did automatically.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, play a long whole bow and deliberately let the sound **fade toward the
  tip** — don't fight it, don't add weight. That thinning is roughly what a Baroque bow does on
  its own. Then play the same note the modern way — feed weight in toward the tip so it stays
  full. Feel the two different jobs.
</TryThis>

  Viotti's singing, sustained style and Tourte's new bow developed together in Paris in the
  1780s. The bow every modern violinist uses is essentially Tourte's design, unchanged — built
  for a kind of playing that barely existed a generation earlier.
</Cite>
