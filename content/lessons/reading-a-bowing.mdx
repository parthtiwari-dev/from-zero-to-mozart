This is the make-or-break skill of the whole stage. A real part's bowing is **information the
composer or editor is handing you** — and a player who can play a clean détaché but can't read
the plan still can't play a Mozart phrase. Reading a bowing is the right-hand version of
`decode-a-dense-line` from Stage 3: you walk the phrase mark by mark before you play a note.

**The plan, mark by mark:**

- **D and E** are under one slur, marked **⊓** (down-bow) and **f** — so: *two notes, one
  down-bow, loud.* Two notes in one bow means about half the bow; start near the frog. Loud
  means a fast bow, a bit of weight, near the bridge.
- **F♯ and G** have **staccato dots** and are **up-bows** — wait, two up-bows in a row? Yes:
  they're **hooked** (or you retake). Short, stopped, on separate small strokes in the upper
  half — save bow, because you just spent half of it on the slur.
- **Bar 2, A** is **⊓** and marked **p** — a *sudden soft*. So the bow arrives with a new
  setting already prepared: slower, lighter, toward the fingerboard. Half note = more bow, but
  slow.
- **G** is the answering **V**, still soft, easing off.

You'd never work all that out mid-phrase. You read it first, once, like a sentence.

## The `f`–`p` echo needs a bow change, not a knob

A very common Classical marking: a phrase at **f**, then the *same* phrase at **p** — an echo.
This is the Stage 3 promise (`dynamics-are-a-bow-thing`) coming due. **p isn't just quieter:**

- **f** → faster bow, more weight, nearer the bridge.
- **p** → slower bow, less weight, toward the fingerboard, less hair.
- **`< >`** on one long note = *messa di voce* — a bow that speeds up and slides toward the
  bridge, then reverses (its own lesson, `messa-di-voce`).

## Editorial marks vs the composer's

Old music comes to you through an **editor**, and editions mark which is which. The
conventions:

| You see | It usually means |
|---|---|
| a **solid slur** | the composer's own |
| a **dotted or dashed slur** | the editor's addition |
| **square brackets** `[ ]` around a dynamic, bowing or fingering | editorial |
| a preface note like *"bowings above the staff are the composer's, those below are the editor's"* | exactly that — read the preface |

The K.216 solo part has that last footnote. It matters: a bowing Mozart wrote carries
different weight from one an editor guessed.

## Fingerings and positions are not bowings

Worth saying again because it's the classic trap: the little numbers `1 2 3 4` are **fingers**,
the roman `III` `IV` are **positions** — both the **left** hand, both almost always the
editor's, and **neither is a bowing**. A `⊓`, a `V`, `Sp.`, `WB`, a slur — those are the
right arm. (Stage 3 `finger-string-or-position`; Stage 4 `the-first-note-of-mozart`.)

## The usual mistake

Playing first and reading the bowing later — discovering mid-phrase that four notes are
slurred when you've already used all your bow, or that a **p** was coming when you're still
playing **f**. The bowing is part of the reading. Group the notes into bow-strokes with your
eye the same way you already group them into rhythms and pitches.

<Cite source="the annotated-edition tradition; the Stage 3 keystone — 'the symbol tells you what, the era tells you how'">
  A printed bowing is the closest thing you get to the composer standing behind you saying
  "one bow here, lift there, save some for the end." Reading it well is most of what separates
  "I played the notes" from "I played the phrase."
</Cite>
