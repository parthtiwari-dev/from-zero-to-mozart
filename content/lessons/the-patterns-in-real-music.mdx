Real music is made of a small number of shapes, repeated endlessly. Once your eye catches
"that's *that* shape again," a whole bar becomes one chunk. Here are the ones to know on
sight.

## The scale fragment

Notes marching up or down by step — line-space-line-space, one direction. Your hand plays a
piece of a scale it has drilled (Stage 4). See it, and you know the fingering without reading
the notes.

## The broken chord

Notes skipping in the same direction, same-colour to same-colour (line-line-line). Your hand
plays an arpeggio shape (Stage 4 J). G–B–D is the G chord; D–F♯–A is the D chord.

## The turn / neighbour figure

Up-and-back, or down-and-back, around one note. One finger wiggles to its neighbour and

## The sequence — the highest-leverage shape

A small shape **repeated a step or a third higher (or lower)**. Read the *first* copy
carefully; then your eye just says "and again, up a step" — a whole bar in one glance. A
huge amount of Baroque and Classical music is sequences.

## The leap, and the repeated note

A **leap** — a 4th, 5th, 6th, octave — you recognise by size and play as a string crossing
and/or a finger jump; don't name both notes, know the interval and the target. A **repeated
note** — one finger stays put, the bow does the work (Stage 5); read "that note, ×4," not four
notes.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take *Ode to Joy* or *Twinkle*. Go through it once and label every bar with a shape name —
  "steps up," "steps down," "broken chord," "repeated note," "turn." Then play it thinking
  only the shape names. You'll find the whole tune is five or six shapes.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a sequence as if each repetition were new material — decoding "G A B G," then "A B c
A," then "B c d B" from scratch. The point of a sequence is that after the first copy you
*stop reading the notes* and just track the transposition. Same for a scale run: read "up,"
not eight notes.

  The reason experts read fast isn't faster eyes — it's that they carry a large library of
  these shapes in memory and match against it. A sequence, a scale, an arpeggio: each is one
  stored pattern, read as a unit.
</Cite>
