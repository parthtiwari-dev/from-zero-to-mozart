A phrase is one musical thought — and like a spoken sentence, it has **edges** and a **shape**.
This lesson is about reading both, so your bow and your breath have something to serve.

## Phrase mark or slur?

Two curved lines that look similar and mean completely different things:

- a **slur** — a short curve over a few notes — means **one bow**, literally: don't change
  direction (you met this in Stage 2 and Stage 3);
- a **phrase mark** — a long curve, often spanning several bars and crossing barlines — means
  **"shape this as one thought, and breathe at the end."** It is *not* an instruction to play
  it all in one bow.

Tell them apart by **length** and by whether the curve **crosses barlines**. A curve over 3–4
notes inside a bar is a slur; a curve arching over 8 bars is a phrase mark.

## Phrases breathe

At the end of a phrase, you take a **tiny grammatical silence** — a lift, a bow retake, an
easing — the way you pause after a sentence before the next. Nothing in the notation always
marks it; the **cadence** and the **phrase mark** tell you where it belongs.

## Phrases have an arc

Most phrases **grow** toward a high or stressed note and then **settle** toward the cadence.
Playing that arc — a little more bow and weight into the peak, easing out of it — is called
**phrasing**, and it's what turns correct notes into music.

## The bow serves the phrase

Knowing where a phrase peaks lets you **plan the bow** so you're not stranded at the tip on the
important note. If the high point wants a full, singing sound, arrive there with bow to spare —
start the phrase with a shorter stroke, or a retake, so the peak gets a whole bow. (Bow
distribution as a planned skill is Stage 5; this is the reading half of it.)

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take the arc example above. Play it once mechanically — every note the same. Then play it
  again: start soft and unhurried, speed the bow and lean in on the way up to the top G, hold
  that G a hair longer, then float back down and let the last G die gently. That's phrasing.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Treating a long phrase mark as "slur all of this in one bow" and running out of bow halfway,
or playing every note of a phrase at the same volume and weight so the line has no direction.
The phrase mark says *shape and breathe*; the shape is yours to make.

<Cite source="phrasing and the singing line — Leopold Mozart's Violinschule on playing 'as a singer would'; the modern phrasing tradition (Galamian, Fischer)">
  Leopold Mozart told his students to shape a line the way a singer shapes a sung sentence —
  with breath, with direction, with an arrival. The bow is the breath; the phrase is the
  sentence.
</Cite>
