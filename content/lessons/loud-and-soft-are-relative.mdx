How loud to play is written as lowercase letters under the staff, from the Italian *piano*
(soft) and *forte* (loud). The crucial thing about them: they are **relative**. A `p` means
"soft, for this music, right here" — not a fixed number of decibels.

## The ladder

| Mark | Name | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| pp | pianissimo | very soft |
| p | piano | soft |
| mp | mezzo-piano | medium-soft |
| mf | mezzo-forte | medium-loud |
| f | forte | loud |
| ff | fortissimo | very loud |

Beyond the ends: **ppp**, **pppp** and **fff**, **ffff** turn up in Romantic and later music
for extremes. Classical music — Haydn, Mozart, early Beethoven — lives almost entirely between
**pp and ff**; if you're playing that repertoire and you've reached for `fff`, you've probably
overshot.

The mark sits **below the staff**, lined up with the note where the new level begins, and
holds until the next mark changes it.

## Hear it

Same seven notes, twice. First soft, then loud — notice it isn't "the same thing at a
different volume," it's a different mood.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, play one long bow as *soft* as you can while the note still speaks
  cleanly — no scratch, no breathiness. That's your `p` today. Now one long bow as *loud* as
  stays clean and round. That's your `f`. Everything else is somewhere between those two.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Making `p` by going faint — less bow, barely touching — so the note whistles or disappears.
Piano keeps a full, ringing core; it's just smaller. And making `f` by pressing until the
string chokes. Forte is *more* sound made *easily* — more bow, more of the string's ring, not
more force.

<Cite source="Grove, on dynamics">
  Dynamic marks describe relationships, not measured loudness. The same `p` asks for one thing
  from a solo violin in a quiet room and another from a section in a hall — and a `p` after
  `ff` is a different world from a `p` after `mp`.
</Cite>
