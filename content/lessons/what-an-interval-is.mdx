An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Its name has two parts: a **number** and a
**quality**. This lesson is the number; the next one is the quality.

## The number — count the letters, both ends included

From C **up to** E: say the letters — **C, D, E** — that's **three** letters, so it's a
**3rd**. From D up to A: D, E, F, G, A — five letters — a **5th**. Always count the starting
note as "1".

The number ignores sharps and flats. C→E and C→E♭ are both "some kind of 3rd" — the sharp only
changes the *quality*.

On the staff there's a quick visual check: a **2nd** is line-to-neighbouring-space; a **3rd**
is line-to-line or space-to-space (both noteheads on lines, or both in spaces); a **5th** skips
two.

## Melodic or harmonic

The same interval can arrive two ways:

- **melodically** — one note then the other, as a leap in a tune;
- **harmonically** — both together, as a double stop or part of a chord.

On the violin, an interval is also a **hand shape**. A 3rd is two fingers close on adjacent
strings; an octave is the wide 1st-and-4th-finger stretch across two strings. You'll *feel*
common intervals long before you can name every one by ear.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the A string, play open A, then each finger in turn, naming the interval from A each time:
  A→B (1st finger) is a 2nd, A→C♯ (high 2) is a 3rd, A→D (3rd finger) is a 4th, A→E (4th
  finger, or the open E) is a 5th. Say the number out loud as you drop each finger.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Counting the *gaps* instead of the *notes* — saying C to D is a "1st" because you moved one
step. Count the notes, and count the one you start on. C to D touches two letters, so it's a
2nd. C to C (same note) is a **unison** — a "1st".

<Cite source="the word 'interval' — Latin intervallum, 'the space between ramparts'; Guido of Arezzo, c. 1030">
  Medieval theorists measured melody by the *steps between* notes, not the notes themselves —
  which is exactly how a violinist thinks, feeling the distance between one finger and the
  next.
</Cite>
