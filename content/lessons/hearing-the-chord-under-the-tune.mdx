The capstone of the harmony movement, and the ear half of `reading-a-chord-chart`: play a
plain tune and hear, bar by bar, **which chord is under it** — even though no chord is
sounding. This is what lets you harmonise a melody, join a jam, or write the chart yourself.

## Most bars sit on one chord

In simple tunes the harmony changes slowly — often once a bar, sometimes once every two. And
the melody notes in that bar are mostly **chord tones** of whatever chord it is. So:

1. **Which notes does this bar dwell on?** The long notes, the downbeats, the first and last
   notes.
2. **What chord contains those?** Usually I, IV or V of the key.
3. **Check it against the three functions** (`home-leaving-pulling`) — does a resting chord
   fit here, or a pulling one?

## The usual mistake

Trying to name the chord from a single note. One melody note usually fits two or three
chords — B in G major is in G (as ^3), in E minor (as ^5), in a D chord... only later. Use
the *whole bar*, weight the long notes, and lean on function. The other mistake: expecting
the chord to change on every note. In most simple tunes it's one chord per bar or slower —
harmonic rhythm (Stage 4 `harmonic-rhythm`) is usually slow.
