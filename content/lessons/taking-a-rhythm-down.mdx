**Rhythmic dictation** — hear a rhythm, write it down — comes first in the "taking music
down" movement for one reason: **it's the half of ear training you walked in already good
at.** Your tabla ear reads rhythm the way most beginners wish they could read pitch. This
lesson is about pinning that skill to Western notation.

## The method

1. **Get the beat and the metre first.** Tap the pulse. Is it in 2, 3, or 4? Simple or
   compound? Everything hangs off this grid.
2. **Feel the cell, don't count notes.** You don't take a rhythm down note by note — you
   recognise *patterns*: "long–short–short", "the dotted one", "the gallop", a triplet. Your
   tabla training is exactly this: bols are rhythmic cells, not individual strokes.
3. **Place the cells against the grid.** Which beat does each pattern start on? Where do the
   off-beats land?
4. **Clap it back and check.** If your clap matches what you heard, the notation is right.

## The level ladder

| Level | Adds |
|---|---|
| 1 | quarters, halves, whole notes; 4/4 and 3/4 |
| 2 | eighth-note pairs |
| 3 | the dotted-quarter–eighth figure; ties across the beat |
| 4 | sixteenths; the "gallop" and "reverse gallop" |
| 5 | 6/8 (in two); triplets; simple syncopation |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Have someone clap a two-bar rhythm in 4/4 (or use a drill). Tap the pulse first. Then, don't
  transcribe note by note — say what *patterns* you heard ("beat one was a long, then a
  gallop, then two evens"). Write those. Clap your version against the original. This is
  konnakol / bol thinking applied to a staff.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to catch every note individually in real time — you can't, and you don't need to.
Hear in **cells**. The other mistake (rare for you): losing the pulse while writing. Keep a
foot tapping the beat the whole time; the rhythm is only meaningful against it.
