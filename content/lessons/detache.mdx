**Détaché** is French for "detached," and it's the stroke you play when the music tells you
nothing else: **one separate bow per note**, alternating down and up, smooth, using the full
written length of each note. It is the ground every other bow stroke is a modification of, so
it's worth more slow attention than it looks like it deserves.

<Margin>
  "Detached" refers to the **bow** — a separate stroke per note — not to the **sound**. A good
  détaché sounds continuous, with no gap between notes. The thing that stops between notes is
  *staccato*, which is different.
</Margin>

## On the page

There's no symbol for détaché. Its notation is the *absence* of a slur, a dot, a dash or an
accent — plain noteheads, nothing above or below them. When you see that, you play separate
bows.

## What to listen for

Every note should sound **the same** — same attack, same volume, same tone — whether the bow is
travelling down or up. The join between two notes should have no bump and no gap: the sound is
unbroken, only the bow's direction changes. Close your eyes and you shouldn't be able to hear
where each stroke begins.

## Where in the bow

Détaché doesn't have a fixed home — it follows the **speed of the notes**. Fast notes get
**short strokes near the middle** of the bow, moved from the forearm. Slower, broader notes use
**more of the bow**, up to whole bows (that's *grand détaché* — `the-detache-family`). Practise
the same passage at the frog, then the middle, then the tip, then whole bows: each teaches a
different part of the control.

## The usual mistake

Two things go wrong, and they're linked. First, the **up-bow comes out weaker** than the
down-bow — gravity helps the down-bow and you have to consciously match the up-bow to it.
Second, players **slow the bow down at the change of direction**, which makes a little dip in
the sound at every join. Keep the bow speed constant straight through the turn, and keep the
same weight into the string from the arm. The direction change should be invisible in the
sound.

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, play four slow détaché strokes — down, up, down, up — listening only
  for whether the up-bows match the down-bows. When they do, move to the D-string scale above.
  Stop the moment your bow hand or shoulder tightens; a tense détaché only teaches tension.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="Ivan Galamian, Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching (1962)">
  Galamian describes tone as the product of three things you control at once — bow speed,
  weight into the string, and the sounding point (`speed-weight-and-sounding-point`). Détaché
  is where you first learn to hold all three steady while the bow keeps reversing.
</Cite>

**Read** this stroke now — recognise plain noteheads as "separate bows." **Playing** an even
détaché takes months of long tones and slow scales, and that's normal.
