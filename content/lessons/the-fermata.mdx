A dot with a curved line arching over it — a **fermata**, sometimes called a "bird's eye" or
a "pause". It means: stop counting, and hold this longer than it's written. How much longer is
a judgment call, and the era it was written in gives you the answer.

## Where it sits

| Over a… | Means |
|---|---|
| **note** | sustain the note past its value |
| **rest** | hold the silence past its value — still *felt*, not just waited out |
| **barline** | a pause *between* phrases — a breath, no note held |

The default, when nothing else guides you: **about twice the written length**.

## It depends on the era

| Era | What a fermata usually means |
|---|---|
| **Baroque** | roughly double. In Bach's chorales it marks the end of a line of the hymn — a **breath**, not a grand halt |
| **Classical** | a **structural hinge** — it sits at a big cadence, or just before a cadenza. It says "a section is ending" |
| **Romantic** | a **dramatic, elastic stretch** — held as long as the moment can bear |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play D–C–B–A into a held G (a half note). Count the G's two beats — then keep the bow moving,
  full tone, for two more. Four beats on one note. Listen for the sound staying alive the
  whole time, not sagging. Lift, find the pulse again in your foot, and you're ready to go on.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Barely holding it — a fermata that lasts a hair longer than written isn't a fermata. And the
opposite: holding so long the pulse evaporates and you can't find the next entry. Keep a slow
count running underneath. Over a rest, *feel* the silence in time rather than waiting until it
"seems right."

  what performers did with it grew.
</Cite>
