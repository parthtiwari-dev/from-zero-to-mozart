Stage 3 already taught you to *read* a trill — which note, whether it starts on the principal
note or the one above, whether it needs an accidental. This is the physical motion itself: what
the finger actually does, and how it gets fast without getting tense.

## A small hammer, not a squeeze

The motion comes from the **base knuckle** — where the finger meets the hand — not the middle
joint. Using the middle joint "gets stuck" and can't sustain speed; the base-knuckle motion is
closer to a small hammer tapping the string, lift and drop, over and over.

## Relaxation is the speed secret, not force

Tension anywhere — hand, wrist, arm — caps how fast a trill can go, more than raw finger
strength ever will. **Build speed with rhythmic cells**: start at a slow, even tempo (around 50
BPM per note is a common starting point), then move the same alternation to eighths, then
sixteenths, always with a metronome, always staying *even* rather than rushing into a blur.
**Practise without the bow first** — single taps, then groups of two to four, then a sustained
trill — so the finger motion is secure before the bow's timing joins in.

## Every adjacent pair, every string

A real trill vocabulary covers every neighbouring finger pair a piece might ask for — 0-1, 1-2,
2-3, 3-4 — half-step or whole-step, on all four strings, not just the one combination you
happen to practise most.

## The usual mistake

Chasing speed before evenness. A fast, uneven trill sounds worse than a slow, perfectly even
one — and forcing speed early almost always means forcing tension into the hand, which caps
your real ceiling later.
