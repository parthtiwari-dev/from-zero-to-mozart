A vibrato learned on one finger doesn't come free on the others. Each **finger** has a
different length and strength; each **string** sits at a different angle to your hand. So the
ladder gets run on all of them — and two combinations are noticeably harder and get extra
time.

## Start where it's easiest

**1st and 2nd fingers, on the A string.** They're the strongest, the A string is comfortable
to reach, and results come quickest there. Get a loose, even vibrato on 1 and 2 first, on A,
before you spread out.

## The two hard ones

**The 4th finger.** Shortest, weakest, and it has the least natural give. Use **arm** vibrato
for it rather than wrist, and let the 1st, 2nd and 3rd fingers rest lightly on the string
behind it — they brace the hand so the little finger doesn't have to stiffen to stay put. The
4th-finger vibrato is the one that, left unpractised, quietly ruins slow movements later.

**The G string, and the E string.** On the G string your hand and arm are rotated further
under the violin, which changes the direction of the motion; on the E string there's less room
and the hand tends to creep over the fingerboard. Both need their own reps — don't assume the
A-string vibrato transfers.

<TryThis>
  The four-finger climb above, on the A string. Then the same four fingers on the D string.
  Then — a bit later in your practice week — on the G string and the E string. Two or three

  rushed.
</TryThis>

## Hear it

The engine can't play any of this. But listen to a slow movement — the **Adagio of Mozart's
Violin Concerto No. 3, K.216** — and notice that *every* sustained note has vibrato, on
whatever finger and string it falls, evenly. That evenness across the whole hand is what these
reps are for.

## The usual mistake

Only ever practising vibrato on the 1st and 2nd fingers, because they work — so the 3rd and
especially the 4th finger never catch up, and the first time a slow piece asks for a vibrated
high note on the 4th finger it comes out stiff and flat. Give the weak fingers *more* time, not
less.
