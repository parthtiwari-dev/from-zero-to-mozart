Most notes, **you** choose which string to play them on. Sometimes the part chooses for you —
with a word, or with a Roman numeral. And the Roman numerals are a genuine trap.

## Two notations, one instruction

| On the page | Means |
|---|---|
| **sul G**, **sul D**, **sul A**, **sul E** | play this passage on the named string |
| **Roman numeral I – IV** near the notes | the string, **counted from the top** |

**Counted from the top:**

| Numeral | String |
|---|---|
| **I** | E — the **highest** string |
| **II** | A |
| **III** | D |
| **IV** | G — the lowest |

So **"sul G"** and **"IV"** are the same instruction. A dashed line after the marking shows
how long to stay there.

## Why a composer marks it

Two reasons only: **colour** (a melody on the G string is dark and rich — Bach's *Air on the G
String* is the famous case), or **practicality** (keeping a fast run on one string so you're
not crossing back and forth). If neither applies, the string isn't marked and you pick.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play G–A–G–F♯ with the A as the **open** string — feel the bow hop over and back. Now play
  it again *sul D*, the A taken with your 4th finger, the bow staying on one string the whole
  time. Listen to the second version being smoother and darker. That's what a string marking
  buys.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading **III** as "the third string from the bottom." It's the **D** string — third from the
**top**. Count down from E every time until it's automatic. And ignoring a *sul G* on a
passage that's easy to play up high on a bright string — you'd be throwing away the exact dark
colour the composer asked for.

<Cite source="standard string-family notation">
  Every instrument in the violin family numbers its strings from the highest. "Sul G" on the
  violin, like "sul C" on the cello, means: leave the easy, bright option alone and play it on
  the deep string, for the sound.
</Cite>
