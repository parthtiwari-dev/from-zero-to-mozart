Two more things you'll meet on the page. A **slide** — small notes running up into the main
note from below. And an **arpeggiated chord** — a wavy line that says "roll this, don't strike
it all at once."

## The slide

Also called *Schleifer* (German) or *coulé* (French): **two small notes, usually a third
below, running up into the main note** — L2, L1, then P. It fills a leap in from underneath,
with a little scoop.

## Groups of small notes

Sometimes you'll see three, four or five little notes crowded before a beat. That's just an
**ornament written out** — play them fast, leading into the main note, which lands on the
beat. Don't count them as strict subdivisions; they're a run-up.

## The rolled chord

A **wavy vertical line** to the left of a stacked chord: play the notes fast, one after
another, **bottom to top**.

  caption="A rolled G-major chord written out: bottom string to top, very fast — open G, open D, then B on the A string — and you'd sustain the top note. On the page it's a stack of noteheads with a wavy line beside it (Movement G covers the stack itself)."
  defaultBpm={72}
/>

- An **arrowhead at the bottom** of the wavy line = roll it **top to bottom** instead.
- A **square bracket** in place of the wavy line = play the notes **exactly together**, do
  **not** roll.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play a slide into the D: quick B, quick C, then land on D and let it ring. Hear the scoop.
  Then roll a G-major chord — bow across open G, open D and the B, fast, from the bottom — and
  hold the top. It should sound like one thick event, not a slow arpeggio.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing a slide as three even notes — the first two are quick, the target is the arrival.
Rolling a chord that's bracketed (the bracket means *don't*). And rolling too slowly, so a
chord meant as one thickened attack comes out as a separate little arpeggio.

<Cite source="standard ornament practice; Bach, Kuhnau and Walther all used the slide">
  The slide is one of the older ornaments — filling a gap by stepping up into the note. Rolled
  chords come from keyboard and harp technique, and string players roll three- and four-note
  chords because the curved bridge won't let them all sound at once — Movement G comes back to
  that.
</Cite>
