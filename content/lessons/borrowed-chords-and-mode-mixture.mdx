A major key and its parallel minor share a tonic (Movement D). **Mode mixture** — also called
*borrowing* — is when a piece in the major reaches over and takes a chord from the minor (or
the other way round) for a moment of colour, without changing key.

## The common borrowings

In a **major** key, the chords most often borrowed from the parallel minor are:

| Borrowed chord | Built on | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **iv** (minor subdominant) | ^4 with a ♭^6 | wistful, "the light dims" — the classic sad borrowing |
| **♭VI** | ♭^6 | grand, cinematic, a sudden warmth-from-below |
| **♭VII** | ♭^7 | rock and folk flavour; leads powerfully to I |
| **♭III** | ♭^3 | dark, unexpected |

## The Picardy third is the reverse

Going the other way — a **minor**-key piece borrowing a **major** chord — you've already met:
the **Picardy third** (Movement I), where a minor piece ends on a major tonic. Same idea,
opposite direction.

## The tell

A borrowed chord shows up as an accidental that **flattens** a note (♭^6, ♭^7, ♭^3) rather
than sharpening it — the opposite of a secondary dominant, which sharpens. And it usually
*doesn't* resolve up by a half step; it just colours a chord and moves on.

## The usual mistake

Hearing a borrowed chord and thinking the piece has modulated to the minor key. It hasn't —
the tonic never moved. Mode mixture is a *colour* borrowed for a bar or two; the home key is
still the home key. If you find yourself in the parallel minor for a whole section with its
own cadences, *then* it's a modulation.
