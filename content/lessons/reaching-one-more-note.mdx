You already know a small version of this. Stage 1's low/high fingers (`low-and-high-fingers`)
taught you to nudge a finger a half step off its "default" spot, without moving your hand — a
2nd finger sliding forward to touch the 3rd, say. Stage 6 generalises that same move to a
**whole step or more, on any finger, in any position.**

## Extension: reaching forward without shifting

An **extension** stretches a single finger past the interval it would normally cover in your
current hand-frame, to reach one extra note — **without moving the hand or the thumb.**

A shift moves the *whole hand's* reference point; an extension reaches *one* note past it and
springs back. For a single passing note, that's often faster, quieter, and less disruptive than
shifting there and immediately shifting back.

## Contraction: reaching backward without shifting

A **contraction** is the mirror image — pulling a finger back *inward*, toward the hand, to
reach a note that would otherwise need a shift *down*.

## The physical feel: release, don't stretch

The hand has to **open**, not tense up — the same "no squeeze" principle Stage 0's
`the-left-hand-frame` already taught for the static hand. A common image: think of the palm
opening the way a hand slowly opens around an orange, rather than a rigid, forced reach.
Imagine the target pitch first, then feel for it — the same audiate-then-place order Stage 8's
`hearing-it-first` already teaches, now aimed at a finger stretch instead of a whole phrase.

## Pivoting: a named refinement (REFERENCE)

Galamian calls it a "retarded shift" or "half-shift"; Ruggiero Ricci called it "pivoting or
crawling." Either name means the same thing: **combining** a small shift with an extension or
contraction — the hand moves only part of the way toward a new position while one finger
(often the 1st) stays anchored where it was, letting the hand "creep" up gradually instead of
jumping the full interval in one shift. It's a genuine technique, useful for smoothing a fast
passage that would otherwise need several separate small shifts in a row — but it's a
refinement on shifting fluency, not something this stage asks you to drill. Know it exists;
you'll meet it again if you ever tackle a passage that calls for it.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the G string, play the normal 4th-finger D, then stretch that same finger to E without
  moving your hand. Feel the palm open, not clench. Then in 3rd position, play the normal
  1st-finger C, and pull it back to B the same way. Both times, check: did your thumb move?
  If it did, that was a shift, not an extension.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Turning every extension into a small shift — moving the whole hand a little instead of just
stretching the one finger. That defeats the purpose: an extension is supposed to be faster and
quieter than a shift precisely because the hand's reference point doesn't move at all.
