The four strings are **G, D, A, E**, low to high — and each is a **fifth** above the one below
it. The violin has no frets and no way to hold those pitches in place, so you set them by ear
or against a tuner **every time you play**.

## The reference

The **A** string is tuned to **A = 440 Hz** — the pitch an oboe gives an orchestra, and the
modern international standard. Get it from a tuner app, a piano, or the **drone** in the
practice bar below, and tune the A **first**. Then tune the rest in order: **A → D → G → E**.
That order keeps the tension across the four strings balanced.

## Pegs and fine tuners

The **fine tuners** — the little screws on the tailpiece — make small adjustments; use these
day to day (turn *in* to raise the pitch). The **pegs** make big ones.

Pegs hold by friction alone, so:

- turn **slowly**, and **push the peg gently in** toward the pegbox as you turn, or it slips
  straight back the moment you let go;
- always come *up* to the note from just below, and **stop the instant you reach it**;
- if the pitch is going *down* as you turn, you're going the wrong way.

## Hearing "in tune"

Two strings a fifth apart, bowed together, lock into **one clean, ringing sound** when they're
in tune. Slightly out, you hear a **wobble** — a pulsing beat — that slows down and vanishes as
you close in. Learning to hear that beat disappear is a real ear skill, and the drone is the
best place to practise it.

<TryThis>
  Open the practice bar, set the drone to **A**, and bow your open A against it. Nudge the A
  fine tuner one way, then the other — hear the beating speed up and slow down — and settle it
  where the two sounds merge into one. Then tune your D the same way against the A.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

- **Yanking a peg past the note** and snapping the string — turn in tiny amounts;
- **forcing a fine tuner** that's already screwed all the way in (unscrew it most of the way
  and take up the slack with the peg);
- tuning **E first**, or out of order;
- **not pushing the peg in**, so it won't hold.

New strings also stretch for a week or two, and temperature and humidity move everything —
which is why you tune before you play, and again a few minutes in.
