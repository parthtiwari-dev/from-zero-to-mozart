Not every dynamic changes gradually. Some marks hit a **single note**, and some **flip the
level in an instant** with no ramp at all.

## The marks

| Mark | Name | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **sf**, **sfz**, **fz** | sforzando / forzando | a sharp, forced push on **that one note** — then back to the prevailing level |
| **rf**, **rfz** | rinforzando | a reinforcement across a **short group** or until the next dynamic — broader than sf |
| **fp** | forte-piano | attack the note **loud**, drop to **soft** immediately, on the same note |
| **sfp** | sforzando-piano | a forced attack, then instantly soft |
| **subito p** / **subito f** | suddenly | the new level starts **now**, with no crescendo or diminuendo leading in |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open A string, play one bow that is *forte-piano*: a fast, full start — the first
  centimetre of bow moving quickly with weight — then release almost immediately to slow,
  light travel and hold that soft sound to the end of the bow. The ear should hear "bam …
  hmmm."
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing `sf` as "loud from here on." It marks **one note** — the note after it returns to
whatever level you were at. Second: forgetting the `p` in `fp` — the loud attack is easy to
remember, the instant drop is the hard, characterful part. Third: confusing `sf` with a plain
accent `>`. An accent leans on the note within the current dynamic; `sf` is the more violent
gesture — a jab, not a lean — but both are still measured against what's around them.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756), Ch. XII">
  He describes the forte-piano as a paired stress on a single note — a strong start that
  immediately gives way — used for emphasis, the way a speaker hits a word and then drops
  their voice.
</Cite>
