Today, a note with **no** articulation mark is played smooth and connected — legato is the
default, and you'd only detach a note if a dot told you to. In Mozart's time it was the other
way around. An unmarked note was played **slightly short, with a little air after it** — a
light, speaking separation. Legato was the marked exception.

This is a reading lesson: how to read an unmarked Classical line correctly. The full style
work is Stage 7 and the Mozart module — but the habit starts here.

## So the marks read as departures

An unmarked Classical passage is already lightly separated. That means the **slurs** in it are
where the line genuinely joins up, and the **dots** are where it gets even shorter than the
speaking default. You read the marks *against* a detached background, not a legato one.

## Mozart's dots and strokes

Look closely at a Mozart autograph and you'll see short marks over notes that could be **dots**
or short vertical **strokes** (wedges) — and it's often impossible to tell which he meant. The
copyists disagreed. **Two modern Urtext editions of the very same concerto print them
differently.** The rough consensus:

| Mark | Roughly means |
|---|---|
| **stroke / wedge** | a little longer and heavier, a slight accent, then a clean lift |
| **dot** | lighter and shorter |

The K.216 solo part has both. When you can't tell them apart, the difference is small — don't
agonise. (Movement H — editions — comes back to why the editions disagree.)

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the G major scale above with your normal touch. Now play it again and take a hair off
  the **end** of every note — the bow easing to a stop rather than a hard clip, a breath of
  silence, then the next note. Keep it gentle. That small, consistent space is the sound of
  Classical phrasing.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing Mozart like Brahms — long, seamless, weighty. Classical music should **speak**: clear,
articulate, light, with air. The over-correction is just as wrong: chopping it into hard
staccato. The separation is small — a comma's worth, not a full stop.
