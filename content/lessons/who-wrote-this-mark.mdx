Not everything printed on your page was written by the composer. An **editor** sits between
the composer's manuscript and the print shop — adding fingerings, filling gaps, making
judgement calls where the sources are unclear. A good edition uses visual signals to tell you
which marks are the editor's.

## The signals

| On the page | Means |
|---|---|
| **square brackets `[ ]`** around an accidental, dynamic, articulation or note | the **editor added it** |
| **parentheses `( )`** | **optional or doubtful** — a courtesy accidental, a questionable reading |
| a **dashed / dotted slur or tie** | an **editorial** slur — a solid curve is the composer's |
| **small-print** notes, accidentals or fingerings | an editor's **suggestion** |
| the **preface** ("editorial method") | the key to that edition's system — **read it first** |

## The ossia stave

A small extra stave above or below the main one, labelled **ossia** ("or it may be"). It
offers an **alternative** to the bar beneath it — an easier version, an ornament written out,
a suggested cadenza, or a variant reading from another source. You pick which one to play.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Open any edition you have. Find the **preface** or "editorial method" note near the front,
  and read the sentence that says how editorial additions are marked. Then turn to any page
  and spot two of them. Every serious edition tells you its rules — and almost nobody reads
  that page.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing a **bracketed** dynamic with the same weight as an unbracketed one — it's a
suggestion, not the text. And the reverse: ignoring a helpful editorial fingering because
it's "not the composer's." Read both layers, and always know which mark belongs to which
hand.

<Cite source="G. Henle Verlag, 'What is Urtext?'; standard editorial convention">
  Square brackets for editorial additions are borrowed straight from text editing, where an
  editor's insertion into an author's sentence goes in brackets too. The principle is the
  same: show the reader exactly where you stepped in.
</Cite>
