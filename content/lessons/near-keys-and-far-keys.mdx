The circle of fifths isn't just a filing system for key signatures. It's a map of **how
related two keys are** — and that matters, because pieces don't stay in one key. They travel,
and they almost always travel to a **neighbour**.

## Neighbours share almost everything

Keys next to each other on the circle differ by exactly **one note**. C major and G major:

Keys on **opposite sides** of the circle — a tritone apart, like C and F♯ — share almost
nothing, and a jump between them sounds jarring and remote. Composers use that on purpose, but
rarely.

## The dominant is the nearest neighbour — and where pieces go

A key's closest relative, one step clockwise, is its **dominant** (G is the dominant of C). So
when a piece leaves home, the dominant is the natural first stop — one note changes, and the
ear follows easily.

This is the missing "why" behind something from Stage 3. A minuet's first half often ends with
a repeat sign, on a chord that isn't home. That's because the first half has **travelled one
step clockwise, to the dominant** — and the second half brings it back. The double bar isn't
the end of a thought; it's the far point of a round trip.

## How you spot a key change

Two tells, and you use both:

1. **A new accidental keeps appearing.** A tune in G major that suddenly has a C♯ in bar after
   bar hasn't borrowed one note — it's moved to D major (where C♯ belongs).
2. **A cadence lands in the new key.** The phrase comes to rest on a *different* home note.
   (Cadences get their own movement.)

  lift.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a piece note by note and never noticing it has changed key — then being surprised when
a note "sounds wrong" that's actually right in the new key. Watch for a new accidental that
stops being an accident and starts being the rule. That's the music telling you it has moved
house, usually to the key next door.
