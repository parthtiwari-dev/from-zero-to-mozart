Wave 2. The other four degrees — ^2, ^4, ^6, ^7 — are the **restless** ones. You don't learn
them as pitches. You learn them by **where each one wants to go**. Play one over the drone,
feel the pull, sing the resolution — *that's* how you name it.

## The four pulls

| Degree | Pulls to | Feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **^7** (*ti* · *Ni*) | **^1**, up a half step | the strongest pull of all — it *aches* upward, almost unbearable to leave hanging |
| **^4** (*fa* · *Ma*) | **^3**, down a half step | sags, gives way, wants to drop |
| **^2** (*re* · *Re*) | **^1**, down a whole step | leans home, mild — a gentle slope |
| **^6** (*la* · *Dha*) | **^5**, down a whole step | melancholy; falls into ^5 |

Two of them (^7, ^4) move by a **half step** — those pulls are sharp and urgent. Two (^2, ^6)
move by a **whole step** — gentler. That half/whole difference is a big clue to which is
which.

## The drill

1. **Resolve it.** Play a random tendency tone. Don't name it yet — *sing its resolution*
   first. Then name the tone from where it went.
2. **^7 vs ^4.** Both half-step pulls. ^7 goes *up* to home; ^4 goes *down* to the colour
   note. Play one, sing the resolution, say which.
3. **^2 vs ^6.** Both whole-step pulls, both downward. ^2 lands on home (done); ^6 lands on
   ^5 (bright, not done). Tell them apart by where they land.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Drone on G. Play F♯ (1st finger, E string) and just hold it against the drone — feel how
  badly it wants to move. Let your voice give in and slide up to G. That unbearable-to-hold
  quality *is* ^7; you'll know it anywhere now. Then do the same with C (^4) — hold it, feel
  it sag, let it fall to B.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to identify a tendency tone by its pitch or its interval from ^1, the way you might a
stable tone. They're too easy to confuse that way. Identify them by **motion** — hold the
note, feel the pull, sing where it resolves, and name it from that. A tendency tone that
isn't allowed to move barely has an identity.

<Cite source="functional ear-training (the tendency-tone / active-tone progression); Stage 4 'the-scale-degrees-and-their-pull'">
  Tonal theory calls ^2 ^4 ^6 ^7 the *active* or *tendency* tones precisely because they're
  defined by their instability — each is a dissonance against the tonic triad that resolves
  to a specific stable tone. Hearing that resolution is hearing the tone.
</Cite>
