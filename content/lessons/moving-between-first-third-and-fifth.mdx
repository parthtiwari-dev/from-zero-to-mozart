Three positions, two real landmarks (the 3rd-position frame copy, the 5th-position thumb
touch), and now the job is moving cleanly between all three — up, down, and past each other,
not just arriving once and stopping.

Every shift here is **same-finger** (1st to 1st) — the simplest case from `the-four-kinds-of-
shift`. A **different-finger** shift is harder: the hand still has to travel the same distance,
but a new finger has to take over the stopping job the instant it lands, with no chance to
"feel" the old finger's grip carry over. You'll meet that properly once double stops (Movement
E) put two fingers down at once — for now, focus on making the same-finger case reliable in
both directions, on every string.

## The usual mistake

Only ever practising shifts in one direction (up). Downward shifts are genuinely different —
the thumb has to lead — and skipping them in practice means the first time you meet one in
real music is also the first time you've ever had to control it.

<Cite source="Synthesis of this stage's shifting-mechanics research (Passes 3–8); standard triangulation drills used across shifting method books">
  Triangulating between two or three fixed positions — rather than only ever shifting to a
  brand-new spot — is a standard early-shifting exercise precisely because it builds confidence
  through repetition between known, checkable points.
</Cite>
