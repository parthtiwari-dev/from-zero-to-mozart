A major scale is not a set of notes you memorise — it's a **fixed pattern of steps**, the same
shape wherever you start it. Start that shape on G and exactly one note has to be bent to
fit. That note is F♯, and that's the entire reason G major's key signature has one sharp and
nothing else.

## Walk the pattern from G

The major-scale pattern is **whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half** (W-W-H-W-W-W-H). Lay it
over the natural notes starting on G and check each step against what the letters give you
naturally:

| Step | Needs | Natural notes give | Fix? |
|---|---|---|---|
| G → A | whole | whole | — |
| A → B | whole | whole | — |
| B → C | half | half | — |
| C → D | whole | whole | — |
| D → E | whole | whole | — |
| E → F | whole | **half** | raise F → **F♯** |
| F♯ → G | half | half | — |

Every step already fits except **E → F**. Naturally E to F is a half step, but the pattern
needs a whole step there — so F is raised to F♯, which also makes the last step (F♯ → G) a
half step, exactly as the pattern wants. One adjustment, and the shape is satisfied.

  teaches keys this way — pattern first, key signature as a consequence — from the first grade.
  It's the standard because it scales: learn one rule, get all twelve keys.
</Cite>

## Hear why it has to be F♯

Play the scale as written. Now play this — the same scale, but with an **F natural** (a *low*
2nd finger) instead of the F♯:

Listen to that F♮: it sits a whole step below the top G, so the climb to the tonic sags
instead of leaning in. The F♯ is what gives a major scale its pull home. Your ear will tell
you the F♮ is wrong before your theory does.

<Margin>
  The seventh note of a major scale, a half step below the tonic, is called the **leading
  note** — it "leads" your ear up to the tonic. Flatten it and the pull vanishes.
</Margin>

## Where this sits

This is the derivation behind the key signature you met in Stage 1. It isn't arbitrary and
it isn't worth memorising as a fact — it falls straight out of the pattern. Do the same walk
from D and you'll find you need F♯ *and* C♯; from A, three sharps; and so on. That growing
stack of sharps, always added in the order F–C–G–D–A–E–B, is the **circle of fifths**, which
gets its own lesson.

There's a reason a beginner meets G major first on the violin and not, say, on the piano,
where C major is the obvious start. Your open strings — **G, D, A, E** — are themselves a
stack of perfect fifths, and G major lies right under the first-position hand. That's why so
much early violin repertoire is written in G, D and A.

One thing major does **not** mean: "happy." It names this interval structure, nothing else.
Whether a piece in G major sounds bright, grieving, tense or calm is decided by its melody,
harmony, tempo and touch — the key alone decides none of it.
