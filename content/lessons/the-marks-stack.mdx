Articulation marks **combine**. A single note can carry two at once — a dot *and* a slur, a
dot *and* a line, an accent *and* a dot. You read them together: each mark keeps its own
meaning, and what you play is the sum.

## The common stacks

| Stack | Name | What each mark contributes | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **dots under a slur** | portato (also *louré*, mezzo-staccato) | slur = one bow · dots = re-articulate each note | one bow, but each note gently re-pressed — a soft pulse, about ¾ length |
| **dot + tenuto line** | portato / "held-then-lifted" | line = full weight and length · dot = a clean end | a full, weighted note with a deliberate lift off it |
| **accent + staccato** | — | accent = bite · staccato = short | a sharp, clipped stab |
| **staccato under a slur, many notes** | slurred staccato | one bow, a row of short notes inside it | a controlled string of clicks on a single bow |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  One long down-bow, slow, on the A string. Over that single bow play D–C–B–A as *portato*:
  lean the bow into each note and release, lean and release — four soft swells on one
  unbroken stroke. Then play the same four as plain legato (no swells) and hear exactly what
  the dots added.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a stack as **one** thing and dropping half of it — playing dots-under-a-slur as plain
legato (the dots ignored) or as four separate bows (the slur ignored). Take the marks one at a
time: *what does the slur ask? what do the dots ask?* Then do both.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756)">
  He describes the slurred, gently separated stroke — several notes in one bow, each given a
  soft pressure and release. It became a Classical staple: a way to shape a line without ever
  leaving the string.
</Cite>
