## Ode to Joy is made of cadences

The first eight bars are a **period** — a question and an answer — and the two cadences are
what make it a question and an answer.

## The Minuet in G's double bar

The *Minuet in G* you played (in D major, in our version) ends its first section on a **half
cadence** — it comes to rest on the dominant, A, not the home D. That's why the double bar
doesn't sound like an ending: the music has travelled to the dominant and paused. The second
section brings it home with a perfect cadence.

## Amazing Grace

"How sweet the **sound**" leans on the **IV** chord (a subdominant colour), and the final
"…**me**" is a perfect cadence, V → I. The whole tune is the classic hymn frame: I – IV – I – V
– I.

## Spotting a modulation by its cadence

If a tune in G major reaches a firm cadence that lands on **D** — with a C♯ leading tone
climbing to D — it hasn't just ended a phrase; it's **modulated to D major** (the dominant
key). A cadence *in a new key* is the clearest sign a piece has moved house.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the two Ode to Joy phrases above one after the other, slowly. On the last note of the
  first phrase (A), stop and wait — feel the question hanging. Then play the second phrase and
  let its final G ring. That contrast — comma, then full stop — is the entire shape of the
  melody, and it's built purely from two different cadences.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing every phrase-end the same way. A half cadence should sound *open* — don't slow down
too much, keep a little forward lean. A perfect cadence should *arrive* — ease the bow, give
the last note room. If you play both flat, the music loses its punctuation and turns into one
long run-on sentence.
