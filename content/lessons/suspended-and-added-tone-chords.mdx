A triad is root + 3rd + 5th. Swap the 3rd for a neighbour, or add an extra note, and you get
chords that aren't quite triads — the open, floating, un-major-or-minor sounds all over folk
and pop.

## Suspended chords — the 3rd is missing

The 3rd is the note that makes a chord major or minor. Take it out and replace it with the
note next door:

- **sus4** — replace the 3rd with the **4th** (C–F–G instead of C–E–G). Tense, wants to
  resolve down to the major chord.
- **sus2** — replace the 3rd with the **2nd** (C–D–G). Open, airy, ambiguous.

## Added-tone chords — a triad plus one

Keep the whole triad and **add** a note, without stacking a full 7th:

- **6 chord** — add the **6th** (C–E–G–A). Sweet, nostalgic, a "soft landing" (old jazz
  standards end on these).
- **add9** — add the **9th** (the 2nd, an octave up: C–E–G–D). Bright, modern, a bit
  cinematic.
- **6/9** — add both.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the G and D strings, play a Gsus4: open G, 3rd finger C (G string), open D — rolled. Hold
  it, hear the tension. Now move the C back to B (high 2): G major. The suspension has
  resolved. That's the whole trick — a sus chord is a triad with one note "hung up" on the way
  to its proper place.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Hearing a sus or add chord as a "wrong" triad with a bad note in it. They're deliberate: the
sus chord withholds the major/minor decision for a beat of suspense; the add chord tints a
plain triad without turning it into a full 7th chord. Both are about *colour without
commitment*.

  A "suspension" originally meant a single voice holding a note over from the previous chord
  and then stepping down. A sus chord freezes that moment into a chord you can name.
