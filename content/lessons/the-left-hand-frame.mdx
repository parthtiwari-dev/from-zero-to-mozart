The left hand has one job at this stage: hold a loose, consistent shape, and drop fingers onto
the string from above. That shape is the **frame** — the same on every string, so the hand
never has to re-form itself for the next note.

## Where the hand goes

- The neck rests in the soft web between your **thumb** and first finger — *lightly*. Your
  **palm does not touch the neck**; there's a gap.
- The thumb sits on the **side** of the neck, roughly opposite your first or second finger,
  gently curved or straight — never pressing sideways.
- The **base knuckles** stay roughly parallel to the neck, not collapsed flat.
- The **fingers** arch *over* the string and come down onto it from the knuckles, like small
  hammers, landing on their **tips** near the nail. They stay curved and hovering close even
  when they're not playing — a finger that flies away has to travel all the way back.
- The **wrist** is straight, or turned gently in toward the neck. Never collapsed — bulging
  toward you — which jams the fingers and invites strain.

<Margin>
  The elbow swings *under* the violin to carry the fingers over the lower strings, and back out
  for the E string. The hand shape itself doesn't change.
</Margin>

## The squeeze test

A relaxed hand can lift and drop fingers quickly and land in tune. A gripping hand can't do
either. Two checks:

- With a finger pressed down, can you **wiggle your thumb** freely? You should be able to.
- Can you **tap the other fingers** lightly on the string while one is playing? If they're
  rigid, you're clamping.

## The usual mistake

- **Squeezing** the neck between thumb and first finger — the single biggest fault. It plays
  everything sharp and kills speed.
- **Collapsing the wrist** toward you.
- **Flat fingers** instead of landing on the tips.
- The **thumb creeping too far under** the neck, so the palm grips.

<TryThis>
  No bow. Form the frame on the A string. Tap fingers 1–2–3–4 lightly onto the string in
  order, leaving the earlier ones down. Then release the *whole* hand at once and shake it
  out. Repeat slowly. You're training the shape and the letting-go, not any notes yet.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="Simon Fischer, Basics — and Klara Berkovich, who taught Hilary Hahn">
  puts it plainly: *the single most important factor in the left hand is which part of the
  fingertip touches the string*, and the thumb stays as light as possible. Berkovich drilled a
  light, organised left hand into Hahn from age five — it's what lets speed and vibrato happen
  later.
</Cite>
