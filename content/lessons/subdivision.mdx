Subdivision is feeling the **smaller** notes even when you're not playing them. On a held
half note, "& 2 &" is still ticking inside your head. Just before a run of sixteenths, you're
already feeling "e & a". The beat stops being a single point and becomes a little grid you
drop the notes onto.

It is the single most useful anti-rushing habit there is, and it costs nothing but attention.

## Feel it before it arrives

L:1/4

  labels={[

## In a real phrase

The end of *Ode to Joy*'s first line: three even notes, then a longer one. The long note is
where players rush the next entry — so subdivide it.

## The usual mistake

Rushing the instant the notes get shorter — a fast run, a string of sixteenths — because the
pulse was being felt in big empty whole-beats with nothing inside them. And the mirror
image: **clipping** a long note short because nothing was being counted through it. Subdivide,
and both problems disappear.

<TryThis>
  Clap a slow steady four. Whisper "e & a" on every beat while you clap only the numbers.
  Now play any Stage 1 tune and keep that whisper going underneath — you'll feel where you
  were rushing before.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="Essential Elements for Strings; Simon Fischer, Basics">
  Essential Elements drills subdivision "from page one"; Fischer's fix for a rushed passage is
  almost always to subdivide the beat before it. "Rushing when the notes get short" is the
  single most-cited beginner rhythm fault.
</Cite>
