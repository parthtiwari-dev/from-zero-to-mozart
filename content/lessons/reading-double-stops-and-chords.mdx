When two or more noteheads share **one stem**, they sound **together**. On a violin that's a
**double stop** — two strings at once — or a **chord** of three or four. Stage 2 (*two in a
row, two at once, or a wobble*) taught you to spot them; this lesson is about reading what
they ask for. **Playing** them is Stage 6.

## Three or four noteheads — a chord you roll

The bridge is **curved** — the bow can only truly catch **two** strings at a time. So a
printed three- or four-note chord is **rolled**: fast, **bottom to top**, the lower pair then
the upper pair, on one bow. You usually sustain only the top note or two.

<NotatedExample
  abc={`X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:G clef=treble
[G,DB]4 |]`}

/>

## One more, from the orchestra

In an ensemble part, **div.** ("divisi") means the section **splits** — half the players take
the top note, half the bottom, and nobody double-stops. **No div.** on a stack means
**everyone** plays the double stop. **unis.** or **non div.** puts them back on one line. As a
soloist you don't act on it, but you'll see it in a score.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Rest the bow on the open D and open A strings at once — the arm at the height that touches
  both — and draw one slow, flat bow. Aim for both strings speaking equally, no wobble between
  them. That balanced double stop is the foundation every chord is built on. Leave the
  three- and four-note chords for Stage 6.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a stack of noteheads as a **fast arpeggio** — one after another — instead of a
simultaneous event. (The tell is the beam: side-by-side and beamed = sequential; stacked on
one stem = together. Stage 2.) Trying to **sustain** all the strings of a big chord — you
can't; roll it. And playing **div.** as a double stop.

<Cite source="Andrew Hugill, The Orchestra: A User's Manual; standard string writing">
  The curved bridge is a trade: it lets you play one string cleanly, which is what you do
  almost all the time. The price is that Bach's *Chaconne* chords and Mozart's cadential
  double stops have to be broken or rolled — the instrument can't hold them.
</Cite>
