For the two centuries before the metronome, the **time signature itself** was half the tempo
instruction. A player read the metre, the smallest note values, and the word together, and
arrived at a speed. The habit still works, and it still matters — because most of the music
this course is heading toward was written that way.

## The metre is part of the speed

**Cut time (₵) against common time (C).** Same notes on the page, but ₵ is counted in **two**
half-note beats, not four quarters — so the bar goes past about twice as fast.

## The compound bar has a longer stride

An **Allegro in 6/8** is quicker than an **Allegro in 3/4**. The 6/8 bar is felt in two big
beats, each carrying three eighths — so "one bar" covers more ground, and the piece pushes
forward. The finale of the Mozart concerto you're aiming at is a 6/8-feel *Rondeau: Allegro*,
and it bowls along for exactly this reason.

## Small notes, slower tempo

A piece packed with sixteenth notes is played more slowly than one built from quarters — even
under the same word. The busy values fill the time; pushing the tempo would turn them to mush.
This implied "proper speed" has an old name: **tempo giusto**, the tempo the notation itself
asks for. Before you set a number, look at the fastest notes on the page.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Pick two pieces you know. For each, note the time signature and the smallest note value on
  the page, then guess the tempo *before* playing. A 6/8 with long dotted values wants a
  flowing two-beat pulse; a 4/4 full of sixteenths wants patience. Check your guess against a
  recording.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading only the word and ignoring the metre — so a cut-time march gets played as slowly as a
common-time one, or a 6/8 *Allegro* gets raced because "Allegro means fast." And the
across-the-board one: a **Classical Allegro is contained** — brisk, articulate, unhurried —
not the headlong tempo a modern ear expects. When the metre and the small notes say "steady,"
believe them over the word.

<Cite source="Johann Joachim Quantz, On Playing the Flute (1752); Jean-Pierre Marty, The Tempo Indications of Mozart (Yale, 1988)">
  Quantz and his contemporaries taught tempo from the metre and the note values as much as
  from the word. Marty's survey concludes that Mozart's Allegros sit well below modern habit —
  the notation, read properly, holds them back.
</Cite>
