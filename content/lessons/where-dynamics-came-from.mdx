How you shape the loudness of a phrase depends on **when it was written**. Three eras, three
habits — and reading a piece in the wrong era's habit is one of the commonest ways to make old
music sound wrong.

## Baroque — terraced

The harpsichord and the organ physically **cannot** get gradually louder. So Baroque
composers — Bach, Handel, Vivaldi — wrote in **blocks**: a whole passage at one level, then a
step to another. `f` … then `p` … then `f` again. When a phrase is immediately repeated with
no marking, it's very often meant as an **echo** — the same notes, quieter.

## Classical — graded, and speaking

By Mozart's time the orchestra *could* swell — the **Mannheim** court orchestra built its
fame on a long, unanimous crescendo (more on that below). But Classical composers used it
**rhetorically**, like punctuation: a sudden `f` answered by a `p`, an echo, a `subito p`
surprise. The dynamics *talk* — they follow the rise and fall of a spoken sentence.

## Romantic — continuous

From Beethoven onward the whole dynamic surface is in motion — hairpins everywhere, long
crescendos over dozens of bars, the volume never quite settling. Here is the Baroque phrase
from the top, played the Romantic way:

## The usual mistake

Pouring Romantic hairpins over Bach and Handel — those phrases mostly hold one level and
**step** to the next. And swelling a Classical crescendo into a big Romantic surge: in Mozart
it's a short, speech-like inflection, over almost before you've noticed.

<Cite source="Grove, on the Mannheim school; Leopold Mozart, Violinschule (1756)">
  The Mannheim orchestra under Johann Stamitz (1740s–60s) drilled the graduated crescendo and
  diminuendo until a whole ensemble could swell as one — the "Mannheim roller." Leopold Mozart
  taught that dynamic shading should imitate the inflections of a speaking voice.
</Cite>
