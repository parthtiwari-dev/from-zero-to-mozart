This is the lesson the whole course has been building toward: **you hear a tune, and you play
it back on the violin without anyone writing it down for you.** You now have every piece you
need for it — a hand that knows first position, an ear that knows scale degrees (Stage 4), and
a bow that can make a note speak. This lesson puts them together.

## The method

### 1 · Find home

Hum the tune to yourself. Somewhere in it there's a note that feels like **rest** — the
tonic. Find it on the violin: try the open strings and the notes near them until one *feels*
like the tune's home. That tells you the **key**, and the key tells you your **finger
pattern** (Stage 4, `whole-steps-and-half-steps`).

### 2 · Sing, then find, one note at a time

For each note of the tune, sing it, and ask your ear: **how far is it from home, or from the
note before it?** Is it *do*? the fifth? a step up? a leap? Use the anchors from Stage 4
(`hearing-the-scale-degrees`):

- a leap up that sounds like the start of **Twinkle** → a fifth (^1 → ^5)
- a leap that sounds like **"Oh, when the Saints"** → a third
- a step up → the next finger (or a string crossing)

Then put your finger where your ear says, and play it.

### 3 · Play a guess, hear the error, fix it

This is the same skill as playing in tune. You play a note; your ear says "too high" or "too
low" or "yes"; you adjust. Every wrong guess teaches your hand where the right note lives.
**Being wrong and correcting is the method, not a failure of it.**

### 4 · Rhythm is already yours

You don't have to *work out* the rhythm — you feel it. That's the half of ear-playing you
came in already knowing. Lean on it: get the rhythm solid first, hum it in rhythm, then hunt
the pitches.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Pick a tune you know cold but have never played on the violin — a nursery rhyme, a pop hook,
  a bit of *Frère Jacques*. Find its home note. Work out the first phrase by ear, one note at a
  time, checking each against your ear. It'll be slow and full of wrong guesses the first few
  times. Do it every day with a new short tune and it gets fast.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to find the pitches before the rhythm and the shape are solid in your head — you can't
hunt a note you can't clearly hear. Sing the whole phrase first. The other mistake: giving up
after a few wrong guesses. The wrong guesses *are* the practice — each one maps a bit more of
the fingerboard to your ear.

<Cite source="the 'mother-tongue' method — Shinichi Suzuki; functional ear-training research; and the sargam tradition you already carry">
  Suzuki built his whole method on children learning tunes by ear before ever reading them —
  the way they learn to speak. Your sargam training is the same instinct: hearing a note as a
  *distance from Sa*, not as a letter. Point that instinct at Western tunes and this gets
  easier fast.
</Cite>
