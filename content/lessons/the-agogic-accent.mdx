You can stress a note **without playing it louder** — by holding it a hair longer than
written. It's called an **agogic accent** (*agogic* = to do with timing, as opposed to
*dynamic* = to do with loudness), and it is probably the most-used expressive device in all of
music. It's also completely invisible on the page.

## How it works

You lengthen the important note slightly, and you **borrow that length from the note right
after it** — which gets a touch shorter. A tiny stretch here, a tiny squeeze there, and the
bar still adds up. The listener doesn't hear "wrong rhythm"; they hear *emphasis*.

## It's how speech works

You stress a word by lingering on it — "*that's* the one" — not only by saying it louder.
Leopold Mozart said dynamic shading on the violin "imitates the inflections of speech"; the
same is true of timing. A phrase read with no agogic shaping sounds like a robot reading a
sentence with every word the same length.

## Where to use it

- The **first note of a group** or a beat — a gentle lean marks where the pattern starts.
- A **dissonance resolving** to its consonance — linger on the tension, then release.
- The **peak of a phrase** — it often wants both an agogic *and* a dynamic lean together.
- A **surprise** — an unexpected note or harmony gets a moment of extra time to register.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play a one-octave G major scale, and lean (lengthen) every third note — G, *B*, D, *F♯*, A…
  — repaying each stretch from the note after. Then play a phrase you know and find the one or
  two notes that most want the lean. Usually you'll feel it before you can explain it.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Overdoing the stretch until the listener hears an actual change of rhythm — a lean becomes a
limp. It should be felt, not identified. The other mistake is stacking a hard dynamic accent on
top of every agogic one, so nothing is subtle — sometimes the *only* stress a note needs is a
little extra time.

<Cite source="Hugo Riemann coined the term 'agogic' (Musikalische Dynamik und Agogik, 1884); Leopold Mozart, Violinschule (1756), on dynamic shading imitating speech">
  Riemann named it, but players have always done it — a marked note in a Baroque or Classical
  score is often meant to be leaned on *in time* as much as in weight. The page rarely tells
  you; the phrase does.
</Cite>
