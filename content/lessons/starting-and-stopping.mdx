A vibrato you can only do "on" is not under control yet. The next skill is **starting and
stopping it on purpose** — playing a note dead straight, then letting the shimmer bloom, then
taking it away again, all while the bow keeps moving.

## Why this matters — the "late" vibrato

Starting a note straight and letting vibrato **bloom** on it a moment later is a real
expressive device, not just an exercise. It's how a Baroque or Classical player often
approached a long note — the note speaks cleanly first, then warms. It's the opposite of the
modern habit where the vibrato is running before the note even sounds. Being able to do it
means being able to *choose* when the shimmer arrives — which is the whole point of Movements
E and F.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On one long note: four slow bows. Bow 1, all straight. Bow 2, all vibrato. Bow 3, straight
  then blooming to vibrato. Bow 4, vibrato then settling to straight. If bows 3 and 4 are hard
  — if the vibrato can only be all-on or all-off — spend a week just there. That switch is the
  control.
</TryThis>

## Hear it

Listen to almost any phrase in a **period-instrument recording of Bach** (Rachel Podger, say):
notes often speak straight and then a little vibrato arrives on the longer ones. Then a
**modern Romantic concerto** for contrast — the vibrato is simply always there. You're
learning to do either on purpose.

## The usual mistake

A vibrato that starts the instant the bow moves and never stops — you've got a switch stuck in
one position. Or the reverse: a vibrato that dies the moment you think about anything else,
especially the bow. Both mean the same fix: practise the deliberate start and the deliberate
stop, slowly, until "straight" and "shimmering" are two settings you move between at will.
