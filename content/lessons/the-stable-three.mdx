Wave 1 of the scale-degree gym. Before all seven degrees, you learn the **three that
rest** — ^1, ^3, ^5 — the notes of the tonic triad. They're the easiest to hear because
none of them pull anywhere; they just sit.

## ^1, ^3, ^5 — the handbells

Set a drone on the tonic and play these three, slowly, many times:

- **^1** (*do* · *Sa*) — dead home. The tension is fully slack. Arrival.
- **^5** (*sol* · *Pa*) — also stable, but *lit* — bright, open, standing tall. It rests, but
  you can feel it's not the last word.
- **^3** (*mi* · *Ga*) — the colour note. It tells you major (bright) or minor (shaded) all
  by itself. Softer than ^1 and ^5, but still settled.

Think of them as three tuned handbells. The goal is that any one of them, struck alone over
the drone, is **instantly** recognisable — no counting up from ^1, just "that's ^5."

## Build it up

1. **One bell.** Drone on, play a single random one of ^1 / ^3 / ^5. Name it. Check by
   singing down to ^1.
2. **Two in a row.** "^5 then ^1." "^1 then ^3." Name both, in order.
3. **A three-note fragment.** Play all three in some order (^1 ^5 ^3, ^3 ^1 ^5…). Sing it
   back on numbers, then name the order.

Do this in more than one key — G, then D, then C — so you're hearing the *function*, not
memorising three specific pitches.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Drone on G. Close your eyes. Play open D (^5), then 3rd finger on the D string (G, ^1),
  then 1st finger on the A string (B, ^3). Sing the three back on "five, one, three." Now
  shuffle the order and do it again. When ^5 and ^1 never get mixed up, add Wave 2.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Naming ^3 and ^5 by *guessing the interval from ^1* every time — playing ^1 in your head
first, then measuring. That's slow and it breaks the moment there's no ^1 to refer to. The
point of Wave 1 is to hear each bell's *own* character against the drone directly. ^5 is
bright and standing; ^3 is soft and coloured; ^1 is just done.
