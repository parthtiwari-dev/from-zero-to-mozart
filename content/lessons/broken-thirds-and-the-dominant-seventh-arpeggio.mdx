Two more patterns that sit between a scale and a plain arpeggio. They turn up constantly in
real music and they're standard practice material.

## Broken thirds

Play the scale, but in **3rds** instead of steps: ^1–^3, ^2–^4, ^3–^5, and so on up. It's the
halfway house — bigger than a step, smaller than an arpeggio's leaps.

## The dominant-seventh arpeggio

The plain V arpeggio of G major is D–F♯–A. Add the **7th** (C, which is ^4 of the key) and you
have the **V7 arpeggio** — the broken form of the chord that drives every cadence.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the V7 arpeggio (D–F♯–A–C) slowly, then play a G. Do it several times. Then play just
  the two tension notes — F♯ and C — as a rolled double stop, and resolve them: F♯ up to G, C
  down to B. You're isolating the engine of a cadence.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Skipping broken thirds because they feel fussy, or never practising the V7 arpeggio because
it's "just a chord." Broken thirds are the single best drill for even, in-tune 2nd-finger
placement, and the V7 arpeggio is the shape your hand needs to make at nearly every cadence in
the repertoire ahead. Both earn their place in the routine.
