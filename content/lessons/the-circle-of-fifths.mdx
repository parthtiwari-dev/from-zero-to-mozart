You now know the order of sharps (F C G D A E B) and that each new key going "sharpwards" is a
fifth above the last. The **circle of fifths** is that idea drawn as a wheel — every key, in
fifth order, so the keys that sit next to each other are the ones most closely related.

<CircleOfFifths highlight="G" />

- **C** sits at the top, with a clean signature — no sharps, no flats.
- Each step **clockwise** goes **up a perfect fifth** and **adds one sharp**: C → G (1♯) → D
  (2♯) → A (3♯) → …
- Each step **anticlockwise** goes down a fifth and **adds one flat**: C → F (1♭) → B♭ (2♭) → …
- The inner ring is each major key's **relative minor** — same signature, different home note.
  That pairing is the next movement's job; for now just see that it's there.

## The wheel is already in your hands

Look at four neighbouring keys near the top: **… F – C – G – D – A – E …**

Now play your four open strings, low to high: **G, D, A, E.** Those are **four consecutive
spokes of the circle** — the violin is tuned in perfect fifths, so the instrument itself *is* a
slice of this diagram. That's also why **G, D and A major fall so naturally under the
first-position hand**, and why so much beginner violin music lives in those keys. It isn't a
coincidence; it's the circle.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Find G on the circle. One step clockwise is D — G major's **dominant**. One step
  anticlockwise is C — G major's **subdominant**. So a key's two closest neighbours are its IV
  and V, the two chords you'll use most to harmonise anything in that key. Say it for D major
  too: neighbours A and G.
</TryThis>

## The bottom of the wheel

Where the sharp side and the flat side meet — around 6 o'clock — the same keys get two names:
**B = C♭**, **F♯ = G♭**, **C♯ = D♭**. Same piano keys, same violin fingers, spelled
differently. A first-position player never meets these as home keys; they're covered in *The
twelve keys*.

## The usual mistake

Trying to memorise the circle like a poster you'll be tested on. It's a **map** — you come
back to it. Every time you meet a new key signature, find it on the wheel and you can see at a
glance how close it is to keys you already know, which of your open strings will ring in it,
and what its dominant and subdominant are.

  Heinichen drew the keys as a closed circle to show composers how to move smoothly from one

</Cite>
