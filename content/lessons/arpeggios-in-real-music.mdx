Arpeggios aren't just practice material — composers write them into melodies and
accompaniments constantly. Here's where you'll meet them, including the one this whole course
has been pointing toward.

## Alberti bass

The stock Classical keyboard accompaniment: a broken chord in the pattern **low – high –
middle – high**, over and over, under a tune. Mozart's piano sonatas are full of it. You won't
play it, but you'll see it in scores and hear it under everything.

## The Mannheim rocket

A fast **rising arpeggio** that gets louder as it climbs — a dramatic curtain-up gesture the
Mannheim orchestra made famous in the 1740s. You'll hear one at the very start of *Eine kleine
Nachtmusik*.

## The first note of Mozart

Here it is. The Mozart Violin Concerto No. 3, K.216 — the summit this course points toward —
opens its solo violin entrance with a **G major chord, and then a G major arpeggio.** The
violin comes in high and falls **D → B → G**: that's scale degrees **^5 → ^3 → ^1**, the tonic
triad, broken downward.

<NotatedExample

/>

You'll decode the real thing — the exact rhythm, the little turn, the finger numbers on the
part — in *The first note of Mozart*, a few lessons from now. The point here: **that opening is
an arpeggio you can already play.**

## The three (four) kinds of number on a violin part

While we're looking at a real part, one thing that trips everyone up. A note on a violin part
can have:

- an **Arabic number 1–4** next to it → a **finger** (0 = open string);
- a **Roman numeral I–IV**, or "sul G / D / A / E" → a **string** (I is the E string);
- a **Roman numeral at the start of a passage**, like "III." → a **position**;
- and in an *analysis*, **^1–^7** → a **scale degree**.

Four different numbering systems. The "2" by the first note of K.216 means **second finger** —
not second position, not the second string. Keep them straight and a dense part stops looking
like code.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the simplified K.216 entrance above: roll the chord bottom to top — open D, B (1st
  finger, A string), G (low 2, E string) — landing and holding on that high G. Then let the
  line fall: D (3rd finger, A string), B (1st finger, A string), G (3rd finger, D string).
  Slowly. You're playing the first gesture of a Mozart concerto. Reading it was the hard part,
  and you just did it.
</TryThis>
