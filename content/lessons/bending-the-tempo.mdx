The word at the front sets the tempo. Other words, printed above the staff **partway through**,
bend it — ease forward, hold back, stretch one moment, then snap the original speed back.

## The marks

They sit above the staff exactly where the change begins. A gradual one often trails a dotted
line — *rit. . . . . .* — to show how far it reaches.

| Mark | Means | Gradual or at once |
|---|---|---|
| **rit.** (ritardando), **rall.** (rallentando) | slowing down | gradual |
| **ritenuto** (also abbreviated *rit.*) | held back — a lower speed, straight away | at once |
| **accel.** (accelerando), **stringendo** | speeding up | gradual |
| **allargando** | broadening — slower *and* weightier | gradual |
| **a tempo** | back to the tempo you just left | at once |
| **Tempo I** (*Tempo primo*) | back to the very first tempo of the piece | at once |
| **rubato** | flexible time — borrow from one note, pay it back inside the phrase | throughout |

**The `rit.` trap:** it abbreviates both *ritardando* (slow down **gradually**) and *ritenuto*
(drop to a slower speed **immediately**). Usually context tells you: a `rit.` with a dotted
line over several notes is the gradual one; a bare `rit.` on a single beat is often the sudden
one. When it's genuinely unclear, the gradual reading is the safer default.

## The little words that lean on a marking

Rarely does a tempo word travel alone. These modifiers tune it:

| Word | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| molto | very | *molto rit.* — slow down a lot |
| assai | very / quite | *Allegro assai* — quite fast |
| poco | a little | *poco rit.* — ease off slightly |
| poco a poco | bit by bit | *cresc. poco a poco* — over many bars |
| meno | less | *meno mosso* — less motion |
| più | more | *più mosso* — more motion |
| ma non troppo | but not too much | *Allegro ma non troppo* |
| sempre | always | *sempre staccato* |
| quasi | almost, as if | *quasi recitativo* |

**`meno mosso` and `più mosso` are not gradual.** *Mosso* means "moved" — *più mosso* is a
new, faster steady tempo that starts now and holds; *meno mosso* a new slower one. They change
the setting; `accel.` and `rit.` change it while you play.

## The usual mistake

Thinking a `rit.` slows the *rest of the piece*. It lasts until an `a tempo` or `Tempo I`
brings the speed back — often just a bar or two later. The other one: making a `rit.` a sudden
lurch. Ease into it. And in **rubato**, players stretch the melody but let the *pulse*
underneath sag too — keep an honest beat in your foot and only the tune flexes over it.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756), Ch. XII">
  His picture of rubato: the accompaniment keeps strict time while the melody presses ahead
  and falls back over it — the freedom is in one voice, not the whole texture.
</Cite>
