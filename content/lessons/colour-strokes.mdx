Not strokes so much as **sound colours** — ways of using the bow to change the *timbre*,
almost always marked with a word. Most of them are just the **sounding point** (`the-five-lanes`)
pushed off the ends of the ruler, plus two "use the wood, not the hair" oddities. You met all
of these as *words to read* in Stage 3; here's what they actually do.

<Margin>
  REFERENCE. Recognise them on the page. sul ponticello and sul tasto are safe to try any time
  — you just move the bow. The rest are ten-second curiosities.
</Margin>

| Term | What you do | Sound | How it's cancelled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sul ponticello** (*sul pont.*) | bow right by the **bridge**, light and fast | glassy, metallic, thin, eerie — almost all high overtones | `ord.` or `nat.` |
| **sul tasto** (*sul tasto*, *flautato*) | bow **over the fingerboard**, light and fast | veiled, hollow, soft | `ord.` |
| **flautando** | extreme sul tasto, often with less hair | flute-like, breathy, almost no core | `ord.` |
| **col legno battuto** | **tap** the string with the **wood** of the stick | a dry percussive "tick", barely any pitch | `arco` |
| **col legno tratto** | **draw** the wood across the string | a ghostly, scratchy pitch | `arco` |
| **con sordino** | fit the **mute** onto the bridge | soft and dark — the mute mechanically removes upper partials, like playing sul tasto everywhere | `senza sord.` |
| **au talon** / **à la pointe** | play **at the frog** / **at the tip** | heavier, more bite / lighter, less | — (it's a bow-*place* instruction) |

## It's all the five lanes

Playing *sul ponticello* is playing in lane 1 and beyond; *sul tasto* is lane 5 and beyond.
The strip of string between bridge and fingerboard is a continuous dial of tone colour, and
these words just name its extremes. If you can sweep the sounding point (you practised that in
`the-five-lanes`), you can already do the two most common colours in music.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Open D, three whole bows: ordinary, then sul ponticello, then sul tasto. Then, carefully,
  turn the bow over and tap the string once with the wood (col legno) — a tiny dry click. That
  little tour covers most of the colour vocabulary you'll ever meet in first-position music.

## The usual mistake

Treating these colours as difficult: sul pont and sul tasto are among the *easiest* things you
can do to the sound — move the bow, and the colour changes. (One more colour, **tremolo**, has
its own lesson back in Stage 3, `tremolo` — you learned both to read it and to do it there,
bowed and fingered. The trap there is confusing its slashes with rhythmic beams.)

<Cite source="the orchestral bowing vocabulary — codified through the 19th century (Berlioz's Treatise on Instrumentation, 1844); Simon Fischer on the mute and upper partials">
  Berlioz was one of the first to write these colours into scores as deliberate effects.
  Fischer's point about the mute is the useful one: *con sordino* isn't just "quieter" — it
  strips the bright overtones out, the same way bowing over the fingerboard does.
</Cite>
