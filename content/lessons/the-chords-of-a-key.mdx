Build a triad on **every degree** of a scale, using **only that key's notes**, and you get
seven chords — the harmonic vocabulary of the key. And the pattern of *which* chords are major,
minor or diminished is **the same in every major key**, so you learn it once.

## The seven triads of a major key

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman numeral | **I** | **ii** | **iii** | **IV** | **V** | **vi** | **vii°** |
| Quality | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor | diminished |
| in **G major** | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | F♯° |

**UPPER-CASE numeral = major triad. Lower-case = minor. A little ° = diminished.** You can read
a whole harmonic analysis — I, IV, V, vi — without naming a single letter.

In a **minor key**, using the harmonic-minor raised 7th (which gives a strong V), the pattern
is **i – ii° – III⁺ – iv – V – VI – vii°**. In E minor: Em – F♯° – G⁺ – Am – **B** – C – D♯°.
The three you'll actually use are **i, iv and V**.

## What the chords are *for* — three jobs

Every chord belongs to a **function family**:

| Function | Chords | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonic (T)** | **I**, vi | home, rest |
| **Predominant (PD)** | **IV**, ii | stepping away from home, building |
| **Dominant (D)** | **V**, vii° | maximum tension — *must* resolve to I |

The standard shape of a phrase is **T → PD → D → T**: home, step away, tension, home. Once you
hear that arc, you hear the skeleton of almost every tune you've played.

## The usual mistake

Learning the chords of each key as a separate list. They follow **one pattern** — major, minor,
minor, major, major, minor, diminished — in every major key. Learn the pattern and the roman
numerals, and you can name the chords of any key on sight and know what each one is likely to
do next.

<Cite source="diatonic triads and Roman-numeral analysis — systematised by Gottfried Weber (1817–21); harmonic function from Hugo Riemann (1893)">
  Weber's Roman numerals let you describe harmony independently of key. Riemann grouped the
  seven chords into three functions — tonic, subdominant, dominant — and showed that a phrase
  is a journey through them.
