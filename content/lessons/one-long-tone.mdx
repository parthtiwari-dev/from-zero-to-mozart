The first real skill of this stage is also the plainest: **one sustained, even sound from frog
to tip, and back.** It has a name — *son filé*, a "spun" tone — and it is the exercise every
serious player keeps doing for life. It's hard for one reason: the bow is not evenly balanced,
so an even sound is something you *make*, not something that happens.

## Why it isn't automatic

Near the **frog** the bow is heavy — its weight plus your hand's weight sit close to the
string, and it wants to dig in. Near the **tip** the bow is light — the tip is the far end of
a lever with the pivot in your hand — and the sound wants to thin out and fade.

So to keep the sound the same the whole way, you **add a little arm weight as you travel
toward the tip and release it as you return to the frog.** It's a constant, quiet adjustment.
Galamian put it exactly: *equal pressure throughout the bow gives unequal pressure on the
string.* Levelling that out is the whole exercise.

## Keep it in one lane — "the highway"

The bow travels **parallel to the bridge**, in one straight line, the whole stroke. Your arm
opens and closes on a hinge while the bow has to go straight — which is genuinely awkward at
first, and worth a mirror. If the bow drifts toward the fingerboard or the bridge mid-stroke,
the tone changes underneath you (you'll meet exactly why in `the-five-lanes`). One lane, start
to finish.

## How much hair

Tilt the stick slightly away from you and **less hair** touches the string; hold the stick
flat and **all** the hair is on. The working rule: **flat hair for a full, strong sound;
tilted for something soft and sweet.** Start flat — it gives the fullest, most secure tone and
one less thing to think about. Near the frog a slight tilt also helps keep the bow straight
and the wrist loose.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Four slow whole bows on open D at the very start of every practice session — down, up, down,
  up — listening only for evenness. Then move to open A, then open G, then open E; each string
  feels different under the bow. When you can hold one string steady for a slow count of ten
  each way, you've done a real day's work on tone.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Rushing the middle and starving the ends — a bulge of sound in the easy centre of the bow,
then a thin, hesitant tip and a scrapey frog. The fix is a **constant speed**: the bow covers
the last inch near the tip at the same rate as the first inch near the frog. The second
mistake is watching the string instead of listening — do this one with your ears.
