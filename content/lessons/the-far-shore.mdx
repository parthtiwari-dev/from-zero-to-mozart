A handful of strokes you will meet in scores and recordings and should be able to **name and
recognise** — nothing here is remotely a task for now. Most are built on spiccato and
sautillé: thrown, flung, or bounced in one direction. One of them carries an idea worth
keeping even though the stroke itself is years away.

</Margin>

| Stroke | What it is | Built on | Where you hear it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ricochet / jeté** ("thrown") | throw the bow onto the string and let it **bounce 2–6 times** in one direction — gravity and the stick do the work; almost always **down-bow** | spiccato | the gallop in Rossini's *William Tell*; Paganini; Mendelssohn's concerto finale |
| **Flying staccato** | a row of short notes in **one** bow direction that lift off the string — a controlled arm-and-finger bounce, not gravity | up-bow staccato + spiccato | Wieniawski; virtuoso showpieces |
| **Fouetté** ("whipped") | the bow is lifted and **flung at the string** for a single sharp accented note, usually up-bow near the tip | between spiccato and accented détaché | Mendelssohn concerto, 1st movement |
| **Viotti stroke** | *not a bounce* — a bowing **character**: a **short weak note → a long strong accented note** on one bow, on the principle of **energy and release** | martelé / détaché | named for G. B. Viotti; all through the Kreutzer studies; a Classical-era signature |
| **Chopping** | a **percussive** vertical strike of the bow near the frog, usually on two strings — a drum "chk", combined with pitched notes for a rhythm-guitar effect | its own thing | invented by fiddler **Richard Greene** in the 1960s, spread by the **Turtle Island Quartet**; jazz, pop, modern fiddle |

## The one idea to take: energy and release

The **Viotti stroke** is worth more than its place on this card, because of the principle
under it. You put tension *into* a short note — work it, bite it — and the **long note that
follows comes out *released*, ringing, singing**. It's "prepare the bite, then let go"
(`martele`) turned into a musical instinct.

You'll feel it long before you can name the stroke: in almost any Classical phrase, a little
upbeat note leading to a strong downbeat wants exactly this — a small effort, then a release.
Keep the idea; the stroke can wait.

## Chopping — a door, not a step

Chopping is genuinely a different idiom from everything else in this stage — closer to
drumming than to bowing. It's all over modern fiddle and pop-string playing, and it's a good
reminder that technique gets *invented*, not just handed down: Richard Greene worked it out
while resting a sore wrist. If it's a sound you want, it's a whole separate study — a door you
can open later, not a rung on this ladder.

## The usual mistake

Trying any of these because a video made it look fun. Without a secure spiccato and sautillé
underneath (which need secure on-string strokes underneath *those*), they just produce noise
and tension. Recognise them, enjoy them in recordings, and build the foundation that makes
them possible.
