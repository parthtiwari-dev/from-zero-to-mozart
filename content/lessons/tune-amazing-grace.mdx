A hymn from 1779, sung to the tune "New Britain." It moves almost entirely by **step and by
skips within the chord**, and it never rushes — which makes it the perfect tune for reading a
line of expression marks: a slow melody where every hairpin has room to breathe.

In G major, first position. **Andante, ♩ = 95**, 3/4. Start `mp`.

## The one thing this tune is for

**Reading a line of expression marks on a slow melody** — `mp`, `cresc.`, `mf`, `dim.`,
`rit.`, a fermata — and playing each one as part of a single arc, not as separate events. The
tune barely moves; the shaping is the whole performance.

## The usual mistake

Playing it all at one dynamic and one speed because the notes are easy — the marks *are* the
music here. And clipping the final fermata: it should outlast its written length by a good
margin, fading rather than ending.

  The words and the tune met in print only in 1835, in William Walker's *Southern Harmony*.
  The melody is a pentatonic Appalachian folk tune — which is why it sits so naturally under
  the hand, and why the D string alone nearly carries it.
