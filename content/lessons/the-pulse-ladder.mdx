Now the pulse goes onto a real note, and you build it with a metronome — the same **ladder**
every method uses: one rock per beat, then two, then three, then four, then continuous. Slow,
even, and — at first — **without the bow.**

## The ladder

Set the metronome to 60. Put your 1st finger on B, A string, in tune. No bow yet. Then:

## The honest arithmetic

Rocks per beat, times the tempo, divided by 60, gives you the speed in wobbles per second (Hz):

- **4 rocks per beat at 60 = 4 Hz.**
- A real expressive vibrato is about **5–7 Hz** — call it 6.

So even the top rung of this ladder, at this tempo, is still a **practice speed**, not a
performance speed. That's fine and it's deliberate. You get to 6 Hz over **months**, and you
get there by the motion staying loose while it gradually quickens — *not* by cranking the
metronome up and forcing it. A fast, tight vibrato is worse than a slow, loose one.

## Add the bow, then take it away again

Once a rung is even without the bow, play a slow bow through it and listen: is the pitch
actually shimmering, evenly, around the centre of B? If the rhythm falls apart the moment the
bow moves, drop the bow and go back to silent practice — the two hands are learning to
cooperate and that takes its own time.

## The usual mistake

Chasing the number — tightening the hand to force 4 even rocks per beat when 3 is still the
honest limit. The metronome is there to keep you *even*, not to push you faster. The other
mistake is jumping straight to "continuous" and getting a nervous, uneven flutter — the ladder
exists so each speed is built on an even one below it.
