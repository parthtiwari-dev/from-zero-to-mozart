Back in Stage 1 (*Sa, and the letter names*) you lined up **sargam** — Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni —
with Western **movable-do** solfège — Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti — and saw they match almost
one-to-one, with **Sa = Do = the tonic**. This lesson is about *why that matters* for
everything ahead.

## The syllable carries a job, not a pitch

The point of naming notes by their **degree** instead of their letter is that the name stays
put when the key moves — and each name has a *character* your ear can learn to recognise:

- **Ti / Ni** (^7) always leans **up** to Do.
- **Fa / Ma** (^4) always sags **down** to Mi.
- **Sol / Pa** (^5) always sounds like it's **pointing home** but hasn't arrived.
- **Do / Sa** (^1) is rest.

Sing a tune you know — *Twinkle* — on solfège in G major, then in D major. The letters all
change; the syllables don't; and the *feeling* of each syllable doesn't either.

## Sargam has fixed notes and movable notes

One detail worth knowing, because it's the same idea Western tuning rests on. In Hindustani
practice, once you choose **Sa**, two notes are **achala** — "immovable": **Sa itself, and Pa**
(a pure fifth above). The other five — **Re, Ga, Ma, Dha, Ni** — are **vikrut**, "movable":
each can take its natural (*shuddha*) pitch or an altered one a half step away. Western music
does the same thing when it sharpens or flattens ^2, ^3, ^6, ^7 to change between major and
minor — but ^1 and ^5 hold still.

## The mindset: distances, not letters

Sargam is practised **against a drone** — a held Sa (and Pa) underneath — and the whole point
is to hear each swara as *a distance and a tension from Sa*, tuned by ear until it sits right.
That is exactly the ear this stage is building. As one description of the system puts it, "the
emphasis is not on the scale but on the intervals."

<TryThis>
  Turn on the drone bar and hold a D drone. Play the D major scale slowly and *say the
  solfège* — Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti Do. Don't rush. On each note, listen to how it sits against
  the drone: Do and Sol settle onto it; Ti grinds and wants to move; Fa leans down. You're not
  learning new syllables — you're noticing what you already hear.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Thinking of this as "a second, foreign system to memorise". It isn't. If you grew up with Sa
Re Ga, you already hear **function** — you just called it something else. This course teaches
the fixed letters to sit *alongside* that ear, not to replace it.

One caution, though, for later: a **raga is not a scale**. Sargam gives you the notes; a raga
is a whole grammar built on top of them — an ascending line, a descending line, favourite
notes, characteristic phrases, a mood. More on that in *A raga is not a scale*.
