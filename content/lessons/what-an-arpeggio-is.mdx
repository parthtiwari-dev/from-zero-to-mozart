An **arpeggio** is a **broken chord** — the notes of a chord played one at a time instead of
together. The word is Italian, from *arpeggiare*, "to play like a harp."

## Step or skip — the two ways a melody moves

- A **scale** moves by **step** — neighbour to neighbour.
- An **arpeggio** moves by **skip** — jumping through the notes of a chord (^1, ^3, ^5, ^8).

Every tune you know is some mix of the two. Practise both and you've drilled the two motions
music is made of.

## It outlines the harmony

Because an arpeggio *is* a chord, playing one tells the listener the harmony without any
accompaniment. A solo violin line that arpeggiates G–B–D has stated "G major" all by itself.
That's exactly what happens at the start of the Mozart concerto this course points toward —
which you'll decode later this stage.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the G major arpeggio slowly: open G, then B (high 2 on the G string), then open D, then
  G (3rd finger on the D string). Come back down. Notice each move is a skip, and notice your
  bow has to cross from the G string to the D string cleanly in the middle. Arpeggios drill
  string crossing as much as they drill the notes.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing an arpeggio as if the skips were hard and scary. They're not — they're just a chord
you already know, played in a line. If you can roll a G major chord, you can play a G major
arpeggio; it's the same three notes with time between them.
