**Vibrato** is a small, regular wobble in the pitch of a **held, stopped note** — the shimmer
you hear in a singer's long tone, or under a violinist's sustained note. It's made by rocking
the fingertip on the string. It is expression, not correction, and — this matters — **there is
no symbol for it.** Nothing on an ordinary part tells you to vibrate or not. You hear the
music and you decide.

## Where it lives on the page: nowhere

Look at a single long note. Vibrato would make it shimmer — but on the page it's just a
notehead. No wavy line, no `tr`, no mark. (You met this back in Stage 2: a shimmering finger
never adds noteheads — if you see extra notes, it's rhythm or a double stop.)

Vibrato needs **time** and a **stopped** note. Short notes have no room to wobble; open strings
have no finger on them to rock. So it lands on the long, fingered notes of a phrase:

## Hear it — you'll have to go outside the site

The playback engine can't produce vibrato, so listen for it in a recording. Two clear
examples: the long opening notes of **Massenet's *Méditation* from *Thaïs*** (try Itzhak
Perlman's recording), and the sustained melody of **Saint-Saëns' *The Swan***. In both, every
held note is alive with a slow, even shimmer — that's vibrato doing its job.

## The cue you'll be taught, and what actually happens

These are **two different things**, and it's worth keeping them straight from the start.

**The cue:** start with your finger on the note, exactly in tune, and rock it *backward* —
toward the scroll — which flattens the pitch a little, then return to the note. The in-tune
pitch is the **top** of the wobble. You never reach *above* it. Every teacher says this,
because reaching above an in-tune note just sounds sharp.

**What the string actually does:** once vibrato is fluent, the fingertip rocks *both* sides of
wherever it settles, and your ear takes the pitch as roughly the **average** of the wobble — in
fact a note with vibrato sounds a hair *lower* than a dead-steady note in the same spot. So a
good player's average pitch ends up sitting slightly below the nominal note — which is exactly
what "roll back from the note" produces. The cue and the acoustics agree; they're just
describing different things (what you do, versus what the string does).

## Try it — no bow

<TryThis>
  Put your 1st finger on B on the A string, in tune. No bow. Rock the fingertip slowly back
  toward the scroll and forward to the note — big and slow, a clear dip and return, maybe once
  a second. Just feel the pitch fall away from B and come back to it. That backward-and-return
  is the whole motion; everything else in this stage is making it smaller, faster and looser.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to make the wobble go *above* the note as well as below — it comes out sharp and
nervous. The pitch is a ceiling. The other mistake is starting to vibrate before the note is
in tune in the first place (the next two lessons are about exactly that).
