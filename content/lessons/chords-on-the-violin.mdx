A violin can't easily play a whole chord — the bridge is curved, so the bow catches at most
two strings cleanly at once. So how does a violinist "play harmony"? Two ways: **double
stops** (two notes = a piece of a chord), and **rolled chords** (three or four notes swept
fast). And just as important: **hearing the chord your single line is outlining.**

## Double stops are chord fragments

Any two notes a **3rd, 6th or octave** apart is a slice of a triad:

- **3rds and 6ths** are the sweet, singable double stops — most of the harmony you'll play in
  duets is one of these.
- **Octaves** state the root plainly.
- A **5th** (like your open strings) is hollow — root and 5th, no 3rd, so no major/minor.

## Which two notes to hold from a triad

If a three-note chord is written and you can only sustain two, keep the **3rd and the root**
(or the 3rd and the 5th) — because the **3rd is the note that carries the chord's identity**.
Drop the 5th before you drop the 3rd.

## Rolled chords

For a written 3- or 4-note chord, sweep the bow fast from the lowest string up, landing on and
holding the top note or two. You met the notation in Stage 3; the technique is Stage 6. The
key fact: it's **broken in time**, not sounded all at once.

## Hearing the chord under your line

Even a plain single-note melody is *implying* a chord at every moment. When you play a phrase
that arpeggiates G–B–D, you're playing a G chord — no double stops needed. Reading your own
part for the harmony it outlines is the real skill: it tells you which notes are structural
(chord tones, hold them, tune them carefully) and which are passing (lighter, quicker).

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play a slow C major scale, and after each note name the chord it most likely belongs to (C,
  E, G → the C chord; D, F, A → could be the G7 or the F chord). You're reading the harmony out
  of a one-note line. Then play E and G together as a double stop — the same C chord, stated
  with two notes instead of one.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Thinking harmony is something only the accompanist does. Your line is harmony too — it
outlines chords note by note, and it lands on double stops at cadences. Knowing *which chord*
you're inside at any moment is what lets you tune the important notes to ring and phrase
toward the arrivals.
