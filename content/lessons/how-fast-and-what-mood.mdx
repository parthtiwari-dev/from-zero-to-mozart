Stage 2 was *when* and *how long*. Stage 3 is everything else printed on the page — and it
opens with the words that set the character of the whole piece.

At the top left of almost every piece, just above the first bar, sits a word or two in
italics: *Adagio*, *Allegro moderato*, *Andante cantabile*. It tells you two things at once —
**roughly how fast**, and, just as much, **what mood** to play in.

## The ladder, slow to fast

Most of these words are Italian, and they line up from slowest to quickest. The character
matters more than the number — a metronome speed is a modern guess, and a contested one.

| Word | What it asks for |
|---|---|
| Grave | heavy, solemn, the slowest |
| Largo | broad, dignified |
| Larghetto | a little less broad than Largo |
| Adagio | "at ease" — slow, songful, room to breathe |
| Andante | "walking" — a comfortable moving pace |
| Andantino | a little *more* than Andante — usually quicker, sometimes slower (see below) |
| Moderato | moderate, middle of the road |
| Allegretto | lightly quick, not as far as Allegro |
| Allegro | "cheerful, lively" → and so, quick |
| Vivace | vivid, full of life |
| Presto | very fast |
| Prestissimo | as fast as can be played |

**Allegro does not mean "fast" first.** It means *cheerful, lively*. A cheerful piece happens
to move along, so over time the word came to imply quick as well — but the mood is the
instruction. Play an Allegro like you're in a good mood, not like you're late.

<Margin>
  **Andantino is a trap.** It means "a little Andante" — but composers never agreed whether
  that's a little *faster* or a little *slower*. Mozart used it both ways in different pieces.
  When you meet it, look at the notes: busy sixteenths suggest the slower reading, long calm
  values the quicker one.
</Margin>

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Set the metronome to 60 and play a one-octave G major scale, one note per click — that's
  around *Adagio*. Now 90, two notes per click feel — *Andante*. Now 120 — *Allegro*. Say the
  word out loud before each one and let your bow arm take on that mood before you start.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Treating the word as an exact number and chasing it. The words are bands, not points, and the
*feel* is the real instruction. The other mistake is playing every Allegro flat out: a
Classical **Allegro is more contained** than a modern one — brisk and clear, not a race. When
in doubt, take an Allegro a notch slower than feels exciting and let it settle.

<Cite source="Sébastien de Brossard, Dictionaire de musique (1703); Jean-Pierre Marty, The Tempo Indications of Mozart (Yale, 1988)">
  Brossard's early dictionary glosses Allegro as "gay" — a mood word before a speed word.
  Marty's study of Mozart's own markings argues the Classical Allegro sits well below the
  tempo a modern player reaches for.
</Cite>
