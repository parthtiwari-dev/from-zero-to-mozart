A **mode** is a scale that uses a familiar set of notes but treats a **different one as home.**
You already know two modes without calling them that: the **major scale** is the *Ionian* mode,
and the **natural minor** is the *Aeolian* mode. There are five more.

## Seven homes, one set of notes

Take the notes of C major — C D E F G A B — and start (and end) on each one in turn. Same
seven notes every time; a different note has gravity; a different scale.

| Mode | From | The scale | = major/minor, with… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionian** | C | C D E F G A B | the major scale |
| **Dorian** | D | D E F G A B C | minor, **♯6** |
| **Phrygian** | E | E F G A B C D | minor, **♭2** |
| **Lydian** | F | F G A B C D E | major, **♯4** |
| **Mixolydian** | G | G A B C D E F | major, **♭7** |
| **Aeolian** | A | A B C D E F G | the natural minor |
| **Locrian** | B | B C D E F G A | diminished, **♭2 and ♭5** |

## The characteristic note

Each mode has **one note** that sets it apart from the plain major or minor:

- **Lydian** — the ♯4 (the "floating" note)
- **Mixolydian** — the ♭7
- **Dorian** — the ♯6 (bright, for a minor mode)
- **Phrygian** — the ♭2 (dark, Spanish)
- **Locrian** — the ♭5 (no stable home at all)

Order them by brightness and each step flattens one more note: **Lydian → Ionian → Mixolydian →
Dorian → Aeolian → Phrygian → Locrian.**

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play a one-octave G major scale, then play the exact same fingering but flatten every F to F
  natural (low 2 on the D string, and the top note if you go there). You've turned G major into
  **G Mixolydian**. Play them back to back. One note, and the mode changes from "major" to
  "folk major."
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Thinking a mode changes the *notes*. It doesn't — it changes which note is **home**. D Dorian
and C major have identical notes; they're different music because the ear is anchored
somewhere else. It's the relative-key idea (same notes, different tonic) — except with a mode
you *stay* on the unusual home and build the whole tune around it.

<Cite source="the modes — the medieval church modes, named and systematised by Heinrich Glarean, Dodecachordon (1547)">
  Glarean added Ionian and Aeolian to the older list to account for the "major" and "minor"
  sounds folk music was bringing in. Those two took over; the other five stayed as modes and
  are everywhere in folk, jazz, and film music.
</Cite>
