## Why bother

A scale is a **map of a key laid out under your hand.** Practise the G major scale properly and
every piece in G major is half-learned — you've already found each note, heard how it pulls,
and drilled the string crossings. Scales aren't a punishment tacked onto real music; they're
**vocabulary practice**, and skipping them just means learning the same things the slow way,
one tune at a time.

## The routine — six steps

1. **Read it first.** Name every note out loud, know the key signature, *before the bow
   moves.* (This is the sight-reading pre-read habit, started here.)
2. **Slow, separate bows.** One note per bow, full sound. Listen for the ring on the resonant
   notes (B, D, top G).
3. **Slurred.** Two notes per bow, then four, then the whole octave in one bow — and **divide
   the bow evenly** (four up, four down, for an eight-note slur).
4. **Rhythms.** Play it dotted (long–short–long–short), then reverse-dotted (short–long). This
   exposes any hesitation and evens out the fingers against the bow.
5. **Metronome.** For **evenness**, not speed — every note the same length and weight. Speed is
   the *last* thing to add.
6. **Up and down.** The descent is where intonation quietly slips. Practise coming down as hard
   as going up.

## The evenness goal

When it's really working: every note is the same length, the same amount of bow, the same
weight into the string, and the **bow changes are inaudible**. That's the standard a scale is
held to — not "fast," but *even*.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take five minutes with G major. One minute slow and separate (listen for the rings). One
  minute slurred, two per bow, dividing the bow in half exactly. One minute in a dotted
  rhythm. One minute with the metronome at a slow click, dead even. One minute coming *down*
  only, very slowly, checking each note. Then leave it — five focused minutes beats twenty
  distracted ones.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing scales fast and sloppy to "get them over with." A fast, uneven, out-of-tune scale
teaches your hand a fast, uneven, out-of-tune habit. Slow and exact is the only way it's
worth anything. And **scales are not sight-reading** — drilling one doesn't cover the other;
you need both in a practice session.

<Cite source="scale practice method — Simon Fischer, Scales; Ivan Galamian's rhythm and acceleration approach; the graded syllabi (ABRSM / RCM) treat scales and sight-reading as separate skills">
  Galamian's insight: practise scales in rhythms and in small bursts, not as one long even
  crawl, so the hand learns to move and then rest. And always for evenness first — speed is a
  by-product of evenness, never the goal itself.
</Cite>
