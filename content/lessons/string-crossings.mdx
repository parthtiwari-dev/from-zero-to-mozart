To move the bow from one string to the next, the **whole arm** changes level — like a lift
stopping on a different floor. Get this smooth and quiet and a lot of music opens up; leave it
jerky and every scale and arpeggio fights you.

## Four levels, and the one in between

Each string sits at its own height of the **whole arm** — elbow and upper arm **high for G**,
dropping for D, lower for A, **lowest for E**. Between any two there's a level where the hair
touches *both* strings at once — you pass through it on the way across (and you'll use it for
double stops in Stage 6).

## The arm leads, the forearm rotates

The **impulse comes from the upper arm** moving up and down — that's what changes strings. But
the fine, fast version adds a small **forearm rotation** — the same movement as turning a
doorknob — plus a slight wrist adjustment: **higher string = higher wrist, lower string = lower
wrist.** A little rotation replaces a lot of arm movement, so a fast crossing passage becomes a
small operation instead of a big one.

## The keystone: cross *early*

The single most useful rule: **the bow starts moving toward the new string while it's still
playing the old one.** When you want the note to *sound* is one thing; when the bow has to
*move* is always a little before that.

Cross too late and fast passages feel clumsy, and slow ones get an unwanted accent at every
crossing. The fix, and it suits your ear for timing: **play the note before the crossing and
the note after it as a double stop** — both strings sounding together — then separate them
again. Start slow and deep in the string, speed up toward tempo.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Open D and open A, alternating, sixteen slow crossings. First exaggerate — big arm-level
  moves, watching the elbow. Then shrink it: same crossing, but tiny, from the forearm, elbow
  almost still. Then a one-octave G scale slurred two-per-bow, letting the D→A crossing happen
  smoothly inside the bow.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Two, and they're opposites. **Moving too big and too late** — a lurch right on the beat that
bumps the sound. And **flapping the wrist** to reach a string while the elbow stays frozen,
which never quite gets there and tires the hand. The arm sets the level, early and smoothly;
the wrist and forearm only finish the move.

<Cite source="Simon Fischer, Basics, 'Smooth string crossings' and 'Forearm rotation'">
  Fischer's principle: musical timing is when you want the note to sound; technical timing —
  the bow's move — is always before that. Practising a crossing by deliberately doing it *too
  early*, as a double stop, is one of the most effective fixes there is.
</Cite>
