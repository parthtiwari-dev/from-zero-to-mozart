**Staccato** means short and separated — each note clipped well under its written length, then
silence, with the bow **staying on the string** between notes. On the violin, the plainest
staccato is basically **a small martelé**: the same prepare–bite–stop, just less bow and a
quicker release.

## On the page

A **dot** above or below the notehead. (Remember from Stage 2: a dot *beside* the notehead is a
duration dot — a completely different thing.) The dot doesn't tell you *how* short or *how*
hard — the tempo and the style do that.

## Two kinds

- **Separate-bow staccato** — one bow direction per note, as above. Start here. It's a short
  martelé and it's a real, usable stroke.
- **Slurred (one-bow) staccato** — staccato dots written *under a slur*: a whole string of
  short, bitten, stopped notes all in the **same** bow direction, the bow travelling in tiny
  stop-starts. This is a virtuoso stroke (Wieniawski, Paganini) and even professionals argue
  about how it works. **Recognise it on the page; don't drill it now.**

## Staccato is not spiccato

Both give you short notes, but:

- **Staccato** — the bow **stays on the string**. It's an on-string stroke you can do now.
- **Spiccato** — the bow **leaves the string**, bouncing. That's a later stroke (`spiccato`).

If the dots are in a slow or moderate passage, they mean on-string staccato. In a fast, light
Classical passage they usually mean spiccato. The tempo and era decide — same as Stage 3 said
about every ambiguous mark.

  "clipped".

## The usual mistake

Making staccato as heavy as martelé — a row of hammer blows when the music wants something
light and pointed. Staccato is *crisp*, not *hard*. The other mistake: lifting the bow off the
string to get the note short. That's an off-string stroke, and it's harder to control — keep
the hair on the string and just stop it.

<Cite source="from the archive of right-hand pedagogy — Galamian (1962) on 'solid' vs 'firm' staccato; The Strad, 'Staccato — the most controversial element of right-hand technique'">
  Slow staccato is built on martelé: a prepared bite, released, then a stop. The fast,
  "nervous" one-bow staccato keeps the pressure constant instead — and teachers have argued
  about the right way to do it for a century.
</Cite>
