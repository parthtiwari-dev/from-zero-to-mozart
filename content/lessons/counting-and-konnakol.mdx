You need a spoken syllable for every subdivision, so your mouth and your foot can hold the
pulse while your hands are busy learning the notes. Western method books teach one standard
system; you already own a far more sophisticated one.

## The standard counting

- Quarter notes: **"1, 2, 3, 4."**
- Eighth notes: **"1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &."**
- Sixteenth notes: **"1 e & a 2 e & a."**

## Konnakol works too

If "Ta Ka Di Mi" or a bol pattern is more natural in your mouth than "1 e & a", **use it.** The
job of a counting syllable is just to give a steady spoken anchor to a subdivision — it doesn't
have to be the Western one. Konnakol is, if anything, a more precise system for fast
subdivisions than anything in a beginner violin book.

The one adjustment: Western rhythm is almost always built from **groups of 2 and 3** layered
over a steady quarter-note pulse (simple meter = groups of 2, compound meter = groups of 3),
rather than the long additive cycles (tala) you may be used to counting across a whole phrase.
Same subdivision skill, a narrower canvas.

  Take any short pattern you already clap in konnakol and lay it over a slow, steady four-beat
