The bow hold looks fussy, and it's worth getting roughly right early — a locked bow hand kills
the sound, a floppy one loses control. What you want is a hold that's **springy and
balanced**: secure enough that the bow won't escape, loose enough that your fingers can flex.

## The shape

<BowHoldDiagram />

Build it one finger at a time, on a pencil first, then on the bow at the frog:

- **Thumb** — **bent**, never locked straight. The tip goes in the little nook where the stick
  meets the frog, tucked slightly under. This is the anchor.
- **Middle two fingers (2 and 3)** — draped over the stick, wrapping down the far side, roughly
  opposite the thumb. The thumb and these two form the **ring** that actually holds the bow.
- **First finger** — a little separated from the middle two, contacting the stick nearer its
  side. This is where you lean your arm's weight into the string.
- **Pinky** — **curved**, balanced on **top** of the stick near the screw. It counterbalances
  the heavy tip and lets you lift the bow. A straight, locked pinky is pure tension.

Hold it like a small bird: firm enough that it can't fly off, gentle enough that you won't
hurt it.

<Margin>
  The default here is the **Franco-Belgian** hold, as taught by Galamian and his students. The
  **Russian** hold (more first finger, flatter wrist) and the older **German** hold differ
  mostly at the thumb and index — a teacher may prefer one, none is wrong.
</Margin>

## The usual mistake

Four faults, and they're all tension:

- a **straight, stiff thumb** — the single most common beginner fault;
- a **collapsed or straight pinky** — it should stay rounded;
- **gripping** until the knuckles go white;
- the **first finger pressing down hard** instead of just resting its weight.

If your hand looks tense, it is. Shake it out and rebuild.

<TryThis>
  Away from the violin, hold the bow pointing straight up. Slowly turn your wrist and let the
  fingers stay soft on the stick. Then hold a pencil the same way and write your name — that's
  the finger flexibility you're after. Now set the bow on the open D string at the frog, and
  lift and lower it ten times using only your fingers, keeping the pinky curved.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="Galamian, Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching (1962)">
  taught a modified Franco-Belgian hold — flatter wrist, index a little apart — and it passed
  down his line: Capet taught it to Galamian, Galamian and DeLay taught it to Perlman,
  Zukerman and a generation of soloists.
</Cite>

**Read** this now — recognise the shape. A relaxed, springy bow hold under real bow weight
takes months to own, and that's normal.
