Flags and beams do the same job: they mark notes shorter than a quarter. A note standing on
its own gets a **flag**; a group of them gets a **beam** — a thick bar across the stems —
instead. The number of flags or beams tells you how far the beat is divided.

- **one** flag or beam = an **eighth note** — two per beat. Count "1 **and**".
- **two** = a **sixteenth note** — four per beat. Count "1 e and a".
- **three** = a thirty-second note. Rare; recognise it and move on.

## Beams show you the beat

Notes are beamed **in beat-groups** — a bar of eighths in 4/4 is beamed in pairs, one pair
per beat; sixteenths in fours. So the beaming itself is a map of where each beat starts. When
a run of small notes looks like a wall, find the breaks between beam groups — each break is a
new beat.

## The usual mistake

Reading a beam as "play all these in one bow." A beam is **rhythm only** — it groups notes by
time, and nothing else. Whether beamed notes share a bow is a separate mark: a curved
**slur** line (next lesson). A beam and a slur can sit over the same notes and mean two
different things.

<TryThis>
  Clap a slow, steady four. Fit two claps into each beat (eighths), then four (sixteenths),
  without letting the underlying four speed up. The beam count is just telling you how many
  claps go in each beat.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="standard music engraving (Gould, Behind Bars)">
  The rule that beams follow the beat is what makes a busy bar readable — the engraver's
  handbook treats it as close to inviolable.
</Cite>
