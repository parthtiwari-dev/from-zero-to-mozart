A **scale** is a row of pitches in order — climbing (or descending) from a home note back to
itself an octave later. The home note is the **tonic**, and every other note in the scale is
heard *in relation* to it: a little way from home, or nearly home, or the note that leans hard
back toward home.

That's the important part. A scale isn't a warm-up drill and it isn't the same thing as a key.
It's the **pitch vocabulary** of a key — the notes a piece in that key mostly draws from — laid
out as a ladder so you can see the shape.

Eight notes written, seven really — the eighth is the first one again, higher. The ladder goes
up by **steps** (neighbour to neighbour), never skipping. That's what makes it a scale rather
than a tune.

## Hear it — stop before the top

Play the scale up, but **stop on the seventh note** and hold it. Your ear will try to finish it
for you — it *wants* that last step home. That pull is the whole reason keys exist.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play a one-octave G major scale slowly, up only. Stop dead on the F♯ (high 2 on the D
  string) and count four slow beats before you play the last G. Feel the itch to move. Then do
  it again stopping on the E (the sixth) — less itch. Then on the D (the fifth) — you could
  almost rest there. The scale's notes are not all equal.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Treating "scale" as a synonym for "boring exercise". A scale is a *map* — practise the G major
scale and every piece in G major is half-learned, because you've already found every note under
your hand and heard how each one pulls. The other mistake is thinking the scale *is* the key.
The scale is the ladder of notes; the key is the sense that one of them is home. You need both
words.

<Cite source="the word 'scale' — Latin scala, 'ladder'; Guido of Arezzo, c. 1030">
  Medieval singers learned pitches as a moveable six-note ladder (the hexachord), sung to
  syllables — the ancestor of do-re-mi. The idea has always been the same: not the absolute
  pitches, but the *steps between them* and which one is home.
</Cite>
