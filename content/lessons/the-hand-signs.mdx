**Reference — optional.** A kinaesthetic aid for the scale degrees. Use it if it helps the
sound stick; skip it if it doesn't.

## What they are

The **Curwen hand signs** (adopted by Kodály) give each scale degree a shape your hand makes
in the air, at a height that rises with the pitch. Singing a degree *and* making its sign
adds a physical memory to the aural one — useful for some people, redundant for others.

## The signs

| Degree | Sign |
|---|---|
| **do** (^1) | a closed **fist**, held at about waist height — solid, grounded |
| **re** (^2) | flat hand angled **up** from the wrist, like pointing up a ramp — it's rising |
| **mi** (^3) | flat hand, **level**, palm down — calm, settled |
| **fa** (^4) | thumb points **down**; the hand droops — it wants to fall (to mi) |
| **sol** (^5) | flat hand **vertical**, palm forward — open, strong, "the herald" |
| **la** (^6) | hand hangs limp from the wrist — soft, a little sad |
| **ti** (^7) | index finger points **up and forward** — straining toward do |
| **do** (^8) | fist again, up at eye level — home, an octave higher |

The two that carry the most is the pair with the strongest pulls: **fa** droops toward
**mi**, and **ti** reaches up to **do**. If you only use two signs, use those.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Over a C drone, sing the scale up and down on solfège while making the signs. Then close
  your eyes, have someone play (or play yourself) a single degree, and make its sign before
  you name it. If the shape comes to your hand faster than the number comes to your mouth,
  the signs are working for you.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Treating the signs as the point. They're a scaffold for the *sound* — if you're concentrating
on getting the hand shape right instead of hearing the degree, drop them. They've helped
generations of Kodály-trained children; they are not compulsory for an adult who already
hears the degrees against a drone.

<Cite source="John Curwen (Tonic Sol-fa, 1870s); Zoltán Kodály's music education method">
  Curwen designed the signs so the *gesture* mirrors the *tendency* of the note — a drooping
  hand for the note that sags, a reaching finger for the note that pulls up. Kodály's method
  spread them worldwide as a bridge between hearing a degree and singing it.
</Cite>
