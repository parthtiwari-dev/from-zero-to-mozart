**Penta** = five. A pentatonic scale is a **five-note** scale — and the trick to why it sounds
so easy on the ear is that it has **no half steps.** No half steps means no grinding
dissonances, so almost any combination of the notes sounds fine. Folk music the world over
uses pentatonic scales; so do nursery tunes, hymns, and a lot of pop.

## Major pentatonic

Take a major scale and **drop the 4th and 7th** — the two "restless" notes. What's left is
**^1 ^2 ^3 ^5 ^6**: open, sunny, uncomplicated.

*Amazing Grace* is almost entirely major pentatonic. So is *Auld Lang Syne*, and the black
keys of a piano.

## Minor pentatonic

From the natural minor, **drop the 2nd and 6th**. What's left is **^1 ♭^3 ^4 ^5 ♭^7** — the
sound of blues and rock riffs.

Notice: **G major pentatonic and E minor pentatonic are the same five notes** — G A B D E —
just started from a different home. The relative relationship again.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Turn on a G drone. Play only the notes G, A, B, D, E — in any order, any rhythm, up and down
  the string. Make up a little tune. Every note works against the drone. That "everything
  fits" feeling is why pentatonic scales are where beginners (and improvisers) start.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Thinking pentatonic scales are "incomplete" or "for beginners only." They're a deliberate
choice — leaving out the two notes that create tension gives you a scale that's pure melody,
no friction. Debussy, Dvořák and countless film composers use pentatonic passages on purpose,
for exactly that open, weightless sound.

<Cite source="pentatonic scales — found independently in folk musics worldwide; the major pentatonic is the major scale minus ^4 and ^7, the minor pentatonic is the natural minor minus ^2 and ^6">
  Because it has no half steps, a pentatonic scale can't produce a tritone or a minor 2nd —
  the two most dissonant intervals. That's the whole secret to why it always "works."
</Cite>
