The single most useful thing your ear can do with harmony: hear a chord and know
**major or minor**. It's a fast, binary call, and once it's automatic you can follow the
mood of a piece as it plays.

## The 3rd decides — so listen to the 3rd

Stage 4's `the-four-triad-qualities` showed you that major and minor differ by exactly one
note: the middle one, the **3rd**, a half step apart. Bright versus shaded, hopeful versus
sad — that whole difference is that one note.

So don't try to take in the whole chord. **Find the 3rd and sing it.** Against the root:
a major 3rd is the bright, open one; a minor 3rd is the closer, darker one. With practice you
skip the singing and just *know* — but the 3rd is always where the answer lives.

## Then the other two

**Diminished** (both thirds minor — tense, wants out) and **augmented** (both thirds major —
floating, weightless) are rarer and you'll place them by their *instability* rather than by
bright/dark. Drill those with `the-four-triad-qualities`; for now, the major/minor snap
judgement is the one to make automatic.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play any two open strings a fifth apart — say D and A. That's a root and a 5th, no 3rd, no
  quality. Now add F♯ (high 2 on the D string) with your ear only — major. Slide it down to F
  natural — minor. Flip back and forth. You're hearing the exact half-step move that decides
  every major/minor call.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to judge the chord as a whole blur of sound. It's too much at once. Isolate the 3rd —
that's the diagnostic note — and the call gets easy. The other mistake: confusing a plain
open fifth (no 3rd) with major because it sounds "bright and stable." No 3rd means no quality
yet; wait for the note that colours it.
