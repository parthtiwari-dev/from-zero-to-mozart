The lowest string, and the one that reads *below* the staff — so ledger-line reading arrives
here too (the next lesson, "Ledger lines", is timed with this one). Five natural notes climbing
away from the open string.

## The five notes

| Note | Finger | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| G | 0 — open | hangs below its own short ledger line |
| A | 1 | a whole step above G |
| B | 2, **high** | right up against the 3rd finger — *not* halfway between 1 and 3 |
| C | 3 | a half step above B — this is middle C |
| D | 4 | the same pitch as your open D string |

<Margin>
  Everything on the G string in first position lives below the staff. Open **G** hangs under
  its own short line; **C** is middle C, one ledger line below the staff; **D** sits just under
  the bottom line. The next lesson, "Ledger lines", is about exactly this.
</Margin>

4th-finger **D** should be your open D string exactly — play them back to back; if they don't
lock, the hand has drifted along the neck.

## The B is high

On this string the natural **B** is a *high* 2 — up against the 3rd finger, the two nearly
touching. The gap is between the **1st and 2nd** fingers, not 2 and 3.

## The usual mistake

The 2nd finger in the middle — playing B♭ instead of B. If a G-string tune sounds sour, this
is almost always why. Slide the 2nd finger forward until it touches the 3rd.

<TryThis>
  The ladder up and down, names aloud, 4th-finger D checked against the open D. Then jump
  G–B–D–B–G a few times, keeping the fingers down. Stop the moment the hand starts to squeeze
  the neck — shake it out, start again.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="Suzuki — Allegretto and Etude, the first G-string pieces">
  come *after* the A and D strings are solid. The G string's natural B as a high 2 is the first
  shape that genuinely surprises the hand — worth drilling on its own before a tune needs it.
</Cite>
