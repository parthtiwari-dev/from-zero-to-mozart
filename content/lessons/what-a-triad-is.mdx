A **triad** is three notes stacked in **thirds**: a bottom note (the **root**), a **third**
above it, and a **fifth** above the root. Stack two thirds and you have one. It's the atom of
harmony — nearly every chord in tonal music is a triad, or a triad with something added.

You've met this chord before — Stage 3's *Reading double stops and chords* printed exactly
this stack and called it "a G major chord." Now you know *why* it's a G major chord: it's a
triad built on G, and the third inside it (G up to B) is a major third.

## Block or broken

The same three notes can be a **chord** (all at once, stacked on one stem) or an **arpeggio**
(one after another — you'll get a whole movement on those). Same notes, same triad, different
surface:

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Build the G major triad note by note, low to high: open G, then B on the G string (high 2),
  then D (open D string). Hold each note in your ear before you add the next. Then play them as
  a fast roll, bottom to top, on one bow. That rolled shape is how a violinist plays a chord.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a stack of noteheads as a fast run of separate notes. The tell is on the page: notes
**side by side and beamed** happen one after another; notes **stacked on one stem** happen
together. (Stage 2 and Stage 3 both hammered this — it's the single most common misreading of
string music.)

<Cite source="Jean-Philippe Rameau, Traité de l'harmonie (1722)">
  Rameau's insight was that harmony isn't a soup of intervals — it's built from **triads**, and
  a whole passage can be understood as a succession of them, each with a single "fundamental"
  root. Every chord lesson from here rests on that idea.
</Cite>
