Two little figures use the same two note values — a dotted eighth and a sixteenth — in
opposite orders, and they sound completely different.

- The **dotted rhythm**: dotted-eighth **then** sixteenth. "**Long**–short." The strut of a
  march, a hornpipe, *God Save the King*.
- The **Scotch snap** (or **Lombard rhythm**): sixteenth **then** dotted-eighth.
  "short–**Long**." The kick of a Scottish strathspey, and a favourite of Bach's.

## A note on Baroque style

You'll sometimes hear a written dotted rhythm played **even more** dotted than the page shows
— the long note stretched, the short one squeezed — especially in Baroque marches and French
overtures. That's a performance convention called **overdotting** (its cousin is *notes
inégales*). It's a **Stage 3** topic — for now, play dotted rhythms exactly as written.

## The usual mistake

Flattening the dotted rhythm into even eighths (the march goes limp), or turning the Scotch
snap into a triplet. Count the sixteenth precisely — it's a quarter of a beat, no more.

  the older, statelier of the two.
