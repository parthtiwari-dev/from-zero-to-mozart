Of the five "extra" modes, these are the two a first-position player actually meets — all over
folk, fiddle tunes, and rock. Each is **one note away** from a scale you already know.

## Dorian — minor with a raised 6th

**Dorian is the natural minor scale with its 6th degree lifted a half step.** That one bright
note stops it sounding fully mournful — it's a *hopeful* minor.

## Mixolydian — major with a flat 7th

**Mixolydian is the major scale with its 7th degree lowered a half step.** No leading tone
straining up to the tonic — so it sounds like major with the edges knocked off: folky,
grounded, a bit ancient. Bagpipe tunes, sea shanties, and a huge amount of rock live here.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play *When the Saints Go Marching In* in G major. Now play it again, flattening every F to F
  natural (low 2 on the D string). It shifts from a bright march to something older and
  folkier — that's the ♭7. Then play a G major scale and an E natural minor scale, and put a
  ♯6 into the minor one (C♮ → C♯): now it's Dorian, minor-but-lifted.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing the characteristic note "in tune" the way your hand expects. In Dorian, the 6th wants
to be **high** (against your minor-key habit of a low 6th). In Mixolydian, the 7th wants to be
**low** (against your major-key habit of a high leading tone). Your fingers will fight it —
override them, and listen for the mode.
