Violin parts are full of numbers, and they don't all mean the same thing. A number near a
note might be a **finger**, a **string**, or a **position** — three completely different
instructions. Telling them apart is a genuine reading skill, and it's the exact knot the first
page of a real concerto ties you in.

## The three systems

| On the page | Means | Looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Arabic 1 2 3 4** (0 = open) beside a notehead | which **finger** of the left hand | small number, right by the note |
| **Roman I II III IV**, or **sul G / D / A / E** | which **string** — counted from the **top** (I = E, IV = G) | Roman numeral over the notes, or a word |
| **Roman numeral, or "III. pos." / "3rd pos.", at a passage start** | which **position** — where the whole hand sits on the neck | at the front of a phrase, often boxed |
| a **circled** number | a **string** — circled on purpose, so you don't read it as a finger | ③ |

The trap: **"III" over one note** = the D string. **"III" at the start of a phrase** = third
position. Same numeral, different jobs — *where it sits* tells you which.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Open a fingered piece. For every number you can see, point at it and say out loud — "finger,"
  "string," or "position." Don't play a note; just classify. Do a whole page. It's a
  five-minute drill that stops a whole category of misreadings.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing a string numeral as a finger — seeing "III" and putting down the 3rd finger, when it
means "the D string." Playing a position numeral as a finger. Freezing at a circled number.
Every time, the fix is the same: **where is it printed, and what shape is it?**

<Cite source="docs/LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md §3; standard string notation">
  The first page of the Mozart concerto carries 1–2–3–4 for fingers and III–IV for positions,
  a few lines apart. Naming the three systems now — finger, string, position — is what lets
  you read that page without stopping to untangle it every time. Positions themselves are
  Stage 6.
</Cite>
