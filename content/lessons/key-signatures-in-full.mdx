Stage 1 taught you what a key signature *is* — a standing instruction, at the front of every
line: *these notes are sharp (or flat) every time, all the way through.* It also gave you the
first three sharp keys. This lesson finishes the job: the **full order**, why it's that order,
and two tricks that name any key signature in a second.

## The order never changes

Sharps always arrive in this order: **F C G D A E B.**
Flats always arrive in the exact reverse: **B E A D G C F.**

A key with three sharps has F♯, C♯ *and* G♯ — the first three of the list, always in that
order. A key with two flats has B♭ and E♭. On the staff they sit in a fixed zig-zag shape, so
you learn the *picture*, not a fresh calculation each time.

## Why *that* order

Each new sharp, as you move to a new key, is a **perfect fifth above** the last (F → C → G →
D…). Each new flat is a perfect fifth **below** the last. That's not a coincidence — it's
because the *keys* march up (or down) in fifths, and each move forces exactly one new note:

- Going **up a fifth** (C → G → D…), the note that has to change is the new key's **7th degree**
  — it must be raised to a leading tone. G major's new sharp, F♯, is G major's 7th. D major
  keeps F♯ and adds C♯, which is D major's 7th.
- Going **down a fifth** (C → F → B♭…), the new note is the new key's **4th degree**, flattened.
  F major's flat, B♭, is F major's 4th.

## Trick 1 — sharp keys

**The last sharp is the leading tone. Go up one half step and you're on the tonic.**

- Last sharp is F♯ → up a half step → **G major**.
- Last sharp is C♯ → **D major**.
- Last sharp is G♯ → **A major**.

## Trick 2 — flat keys

**The second-to-last flat names the key.**

- Flats B♭, E♭ → second-to-last is B♭ → **B♭ major**.
- Flats B♭, E♭, A♭ → **E♭ major**.
- Flats B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭ → **A♭ major**.

**One flat is the exception:** B♭ alone is **F major** — memorise that one, because "second-
to-last" needs at least two flats.

## The other half of the answer

Every key signature belongs to **one major key *or* its relative minor** — the two share all
seven notes. One sharp is **G major or E minor**. No sharps or flats is **C major or A minor**.
Which one a piece is actually in is a listening judgement, and it's the next movement's job.
For now: read the signature, name the *major* possibility, and keep the minor one in mind.

## The usual mistake

Working out the scale from scratch to identify a key ("okay, one sharp, F♯, so W-W-H from
where…"). You did that once, in *The major scale formula*. Now use the trick: last sharp plus a
half step, or second-to-last flat. It takes a second, and a good sight-reader does it before
playing a note.

<Cite source="the flat sign — Italian 'be molle', 'soft B'; the oldest accidental in Western notation (c. 10th century)">
  Long before key signatures, medieval singers were already flattening B to soften the harsh
  F–B tritone — an unwritten habit called *musica ficta*. The ♭ sign is a stylised lowercase
  b; the ♮ and ♯ signs are the "hard b" of the same medieval pair. Key signatures are that one
  habit, formalised and extended.
</Cite>
