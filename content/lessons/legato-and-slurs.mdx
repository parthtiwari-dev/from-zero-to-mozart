**Legato** means smoothly connected — no gap, no re-attack between notes. The way the music
tells you to play connected is a **slur**: a curved line over a group of notes meaning *take
all of these in one bow*. You met the symbol in Stage 2 and Stage 3; here you make it work.

## A slur is a bowing instruction

The bow **moves continuously in one direction** while the **left-hand fingers change the
notes**. That split — right arm doing one smooth thing, left hand doing a busy different thing
— is the whole difficulty of legato. Nothing about the bow stops or restarts; only the fingers
move.

## Dividing the bow — stop first, then smooth

Four notes in one bow means each note gets **a quarter of the bow**. The way to get that even:

1. Play the slur as **separate stops** — actually halt the bow a moment at each quarter-point.
   Now you can hear and feel the four equal portions. (This is really a rhythm exercise, and
   rhythm is your strong suit.)
2. **Take the stops away**, keep the portions identical. Now it's legato.

## Slurring across a string

When a slur crosses from one string to the next, the connection lives in the **left hand**:
**don't lift the finger on the old string until the new note has already sounded** — a hair of
overlap, never a gap. The bow rolls between the arm levels (the crescent path from
`string-crossings`) without a bump.

## Bowing variations — the best bow workout there is

Take a one-octave scale and apply **one bowing pattern** to the whole thing: all separate; two
slurred + two separate; three slurred + one separate; one + three; hooked. Then the next
pattern. The Russian teacher Yuri Yankelevich drilled his advanced students on the Kreutzer
studies this way for years — *"one of the single most effective methods of practising and
improving bow control."*

Each pattern is a small **rhythmic cell** laid over the notes — which plays straight to your
ear for grouping. Feel "2 + 2" or "3 + 1" first, then let the bow follow.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  One octave of G major, three ways: (1) all separate détaché; (2) two notes slurred per bow;
  (3) the whole octave in one slow bow, up and down. For (3), you have to save bow — use less

## The usual mistake

Letting the bow **bump** every time a finger drops — an accent on each note inside the slur,
so it comes out as a row of little separate sounds instead of one line. The bow speed and
weight stay flat and even; the fingers move independently underneath. The other mistake: a
long slur that starts with big bow and runs out — plan it backwards from the last note.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756); Simon Fischer, Basics, 'Bowing patterns'">
  Leopold Mozart taught that a left-hand finger should stay down until it absolutely must move,
  precisely so the line stays smooth — the same rule violinists use today for a seamless
  slurred string crossing.
</Cite>
