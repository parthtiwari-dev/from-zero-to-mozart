Your violin has a tuner built into it, and it's free. When a stopped note is exactly in tune
with an open string, that open string **starts to vibrate on its own** — a faint, warm hum
under the note you're playing. Sympathetic resonance. When you hear it, the note is right.

## The 3rd-finger octave

The clearest one: your **3rd finger** on the A, D and E strings plays the octave of the next
open string but one below — and rings it.

## The 1st-finger octave

Your **1st finger** on the G and D strings does the same thing with the string *two above*:

## Which notes ring

- **A string:** 3rd finger (D) rings the D string · 4th finger (E) matches the open E
- **D string:** 1st finger (E) rings the E string · 3rd finger (G) rings the G string · 4th
  finger (A) matches the open A
- **G string:** 1st finger (A) rings the A string · 4th finger (D) matches the open D
- **E string:** 3rd finger (A) rings the A string

## The usual mistake

Not listening for it. The ring is quiet, and until you know to expect it, it slips past. Play
the note, then *stop moving* and listen into the sound for a second.

<TryThis>
  Play a slow G-major phrase — G, B, D, E, D, B, G on the G and D strings. As each note lands,
  hold it and listen for a string humming somewhere else. The D and G especially should light
  up their open strings.
</TryThis>

<Cite source="ringing tones — Simon Fischer, and Laurie Niles on violinist.com">
  Great players tune to these constantly, without thinking about it. It ties directly to the
  harmonic series (Stage 4) — an in-tune note *shares* overtones with the open string, so the
  string can't help but answer.
</Cite>
