A reference card. Beyond secondary dominants and borrowed chords, there's a small family of
**chromatic chords** — spicy, dramatic harmonies that use notes from outside the key. They're
the vocabulary of Romantic opera and the big emotional moments. **Recognise them; you don't
need to build them yet.**

## The Neapolitan sixth

A **major triad built on the ♭2** of the key, almost always in first inversion (so ^4 is in
the bass — hence "sixth"). In A minor, that's a **B♭ major chord**. It appears just before the
dominant, adding a dark, fatalistic weight to a cadence.

- Written **♭II6** or **N6**.
- The tell: a **♭2** appearing in a chord, not resolving up.

## The augmented sixth chords

Three related chords, all containing the interval of an **augmented 6th** (which sounds like a
minor 7th but is spelled to splay *outward* to an octave). Named by nationality for no
particularly good reason:

| Name | Extra note above the basic shape | Flavour |
|---|---|---|
| **Italian sixth** (It+6) | just the three notes | lean, stark |
| **French sixth** (Fr+6) | + a ^2 | shimmery, Impressionist |
| **German sixth** (Ger+6) | + a ♭^3 | rich, Wagnerian |

All three drive powerfully to the **dominant**. They're a Romantic-era favourite for the
moment before a big arrival.

## The diminished seventh as a pivot

The **fully diminished 7th** chord (Movement H, *the other seventh chords*) is symmetrical —
stack of minor 3rds — so it belongs to no single key. That makes it the ultimate **pivot
chord**: a composer can spell it one way, then re-spell it and resolve it into a completely
distant key. It's how a lot of dramatic key changes happen.

## Where you land

For Stage 4: if you see a **♭II** (Neapolitan), a **+6** (augmented sixth), or a diminished
7th being used to swing into a far-off key — recognise it as *Romantic chromatic harmony*,
know it's heading to the dominant or pivoting somewhere, and read on. Building and resolving
these properly is a later harmony stage.
