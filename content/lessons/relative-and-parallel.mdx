A major key and a minor key can be linked in two different ways, and they're easy to mix up.
One shares the **notes**; the other shares the **home**.

## Relative — same key signature, different home

**Relative** keys use the **same seven notes** (the same key signature) but treat a different
one as home. G major and **E minor** are relatives: both have one sharp, F♯.

**To find a major key's relative minor:** go down a **minor 3rd** (three half steps) from the
tonic — or, same thing, start on the **6th degree**. G major's 6th is E → E minor. D major's
6th is B → B minor. C major's 6th is A → A minor.

**Going the other way** — a minor key's relative *major* is up a minor 3rd, on the **3rd
degree**: E minor → G major; A minor → C major.

## Parallel — same home, different key signature

**Parallel** keys share the **tonic** but not the signature. G major and **G minor** both call
G home — but G minor lowers the 3rd, 6th and 7th (B→B♭, E→E♭, F♯→F♮), which works out to a key
signature of **two flats** instead of one sharp.

Composers switch between parallel major and minor mid-piece for exactly that effect — a sudden
shift of light without leaving home.

## How to tell a major key from its relative minor

One sharp on the page is **G major or E minor**. To decide:

- **Where does it rest?** Does the tune keep landing on G, or on E?
- **Is there a D♯ floating around?** E minor's leading tone is D♯, and it's *not* in the key
  signature — it's added as an accidental every time it's wanted. A tune in "one sharp" that's
  peppered with D♯ accidentals is almost certainly in E minor.

## The usual mistake

Muddling the two words. Remember it by what each one keeps: **relative keys keep the notes**
(so you can slip between them without an accidental), **parallel keys keep the home** (so the
tonic stays put while the mood flips). And relatives do *not* sound the same — they share a
note collection, but a different note has gravity, and that changes everything.

  Schubert was a master of the parallel switch — a phrase in the major, repeated in the minor,
  same melody, the light gone out of it. It works because the home note never moves; only the
  colour does.
</Cite>
