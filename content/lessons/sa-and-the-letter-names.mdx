If you've grown up with **sargam** — Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni — Western note-reading can feel
oddly rigid at first. This lesson connects the two, because the connection is genuinely useful
and it plays to an ear you already have.

## Two different jobs

In North-Indian classical music, **Sa is movable**. You pick a Sa to suit your voice or the
instrument, and everything else is defined *relative* to it. Sa Re Ga is a set of
relationships — a shape — not fixed frequencies.

Western staff notation does the opposite: **A B C D E F G** are nailed to fixed pitches and
never move. Middle C is always middle C.

Neither is more correct. One keeps the *relationships* constant so you can transpose freely;
the other keeps the *pitches* constant so an orchestra can read one page together.

## The bridge: movable "Do"

Western musicians have a movable system too, kept for exactly this: **solfège** — Do Re Mi Fa
Sol La Ti — where **Do is the tonic**, wherever the key puts it. It lines up almost
one-to-one with sargam.

## The key signature tells you where Sa is

One sharp → the music is in G major → **G is Sa**. And Ni — the leading note, F♯ — is exactly
what the key signature is adjusting.

## The usual mistake

Treating the letter names as the music. The letter is an address; the *function* — this is Sa,
this is the leading note pulling home — is what your ear actually tracks, and yours already
does it well.

<TryThis>
  Sing the G-major scale as Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa while you play it. Then play it in D
  major and sing the *same syllables*. Your voice keeps the shape; your hand relearns the
  letters.
</TryThis>
