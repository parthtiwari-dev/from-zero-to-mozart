If you've grown up with **sargam** — Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni — Western note-reading can feel
oddly rigid at first. This page connects the two, because the connection is genuinely useful.

## Two different jobs

In Indian classical music, **Sa is movable**. You choose a Sa to suit your voice or the
instrument, and everything else is defined *relative* to it. Sa Re Ga is a set of
relationships — a shape — not fixed frequencies.

Western staff notation does the opposite: the letters **A B C D E F G** are nailed to fixed
pitches and never move. Middle C is always middle C.

Neither is more correct. They're solving different problems — one keeps the *relationships*
constant so you can transpose freely; the other keeps the *pitches* constant so an orchestra
can read the same page.

## The bridge: movable "Do"

Western musicians have a movable system too, borrowed for exactly this: **solfège** — Do Re Mi
Fa Sol La Ti. In "movable Do", *Do is the tonic*, wherever the key puts it. It lines up almost
one-to-one with sargam:

So the **key signature** is the thing that tells you where Sa is. One sharp → the music is in
G major → **G is Sa**, and the leading note (Ni, the F sharp) is what the key signature is
adjusting. When you read, you can hear the scale-degree — "that's Pa", "that's the leading
note" — *and* name the fixed letter. Both at once is the goal, and your ear already knows how
to do the first half.
