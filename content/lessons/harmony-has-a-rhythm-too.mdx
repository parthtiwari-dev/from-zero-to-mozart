You've got a strong sense of rhythm from tabla. Here's a place it pays off directly: **the
chords change on a pulse too.** How often they change — and how that rate speeds up or slows
down — is called the **harmonic rhythm**, and hearing it is a rhythm skill pointed at harmony.

## Slow and fast

- **Slow harmonic rhythm** — one chord per bar, or one chord for several bars. Calm, spacious.
- **Fast harmonic rhythm** — two, three, four chords per bar. Busy, driving, urgent.

The *melody* can be rattling along in sixteenths while the *harmony* underneath changes only
once a bar. They're two different rhythms happening at once — like a fast bol pattern over a
slow theka.

## It speeds up into a cadence

A useful thing to listen for: as a phrase approaches its ending, the **chords often change
faster** — one per bar becomes two per bar, then the final cadence chords land squarely on the
strong beats. The quickening is a signal: *we're arriving.*

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the four-bar phrase above with a G drone on. For the first two bars the drone and the

  that's the harmony changing to V, and you can hear the drone "go wrong" underneath. In bar 4
  it settles back. You just tracked the harmonic rhythm by ear.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Assuming the harmony changes every time the melody moves. It usually doesn't. Hear the two
layers separately — the surface rhythm of the tune, and the slower pulse of the chords
underneath. Musicians who can feel both at once phrase far better, because they know which
notes are structural and which are just passing decoration.

<Cite source="harmonic rhythm — standard term in music analysis; 'Telling a Story with Harmonic Rhythm', Berklee">
  "The rate at which chords change, in relation to the rate of notes." Final cadence chords
  are usually on stronger beats than the chord before them — the harmony lands on the downbeat
  to say *this is the end of the sentence.*
</Cite>
