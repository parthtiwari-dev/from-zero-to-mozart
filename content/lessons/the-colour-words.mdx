A few Italian phrases change nothing about the notes or the rhythm — they change the
**colour** of the sound. Either by telling you **where** to bow, or by asking you to clip a
**mute** onto the bridge.

## The words

| Mark | What you do | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **sul ponticello** (sul pont.) | bow right up against the **bridge** | thin, glassy, metallic, slightly eerie |
| **sul tasto** (also **flautando**) | bow out over the **fingerboard** | soft, hollow, flute-like — few overtones |
| **naturale** / **ord.** / **modo ordinario** | back to your normal spot | your usual tone |
| **con sordino** | put the **mute** on (a small clamp on the bridge) | veiled, darker, a touch quieter |
| **senza sordino** (or *via sord.*) | take the mute **off** | — |

These are the "lanes" from Stage 0 (*the geography of the bow*), used deliberately for effect.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Three long bows on the open A, same speed and weight each time. First in your normal spot.
  Then way out over the fingerboard — listen for it to go soft and flute-like. Then right up
  by the bridge — listen for it to go glassy and whistling. You've just played *ord.*, *sul
  tasto* and *sul ponticello*.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing *sul tasto* as merely **soft** and *sul ponticello* as merely **loud** — they're
colours, not volumes. Move the contact point and hold the bow speed and weight near normal.
And forgetting to return to **ordinario** when the marked passage ends, so the strange colour
leaks into the next phrase.

<Cite source="Hector Berlioz, Grand traité d'instrumentation (1844); Galamian's sounding-point model">
  Berlioz's orchestration treatise fixed these colour terms in the repertoire. The mechanism
  is the one from Stage 0: the closer to the bridge, the more upper partials and edge; the
  closer to the fingerboard, the purer and softer the tone.
</Cite>
