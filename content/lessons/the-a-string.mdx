This is where you start — the A string. The hand falls into its most natural shape here, the
bow arm sits at a comfortable middle height, and the tunes that live on this string are ones
you can already sing. Five natural notes; learn them and Twinkle, Ode to Joy and half of
Suzuki Book 1 are open to you.

## The five notes

| Note | Finger | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 — open | the string itself |
| B | 1 | on the middle line of the staff — a landmark worth knowing cold |
| C | 2, low | back against the 1st finger (C♯, a high 2, is the one A major wants — see below) |
| D | 3 | a whole step above natural C |
| E | 4 | the same pitch as your open E string |

The **E** you play with the 4th finger should be exactly your open E string. Play 4th-finger E,
then open E, back and forth — if they don't match, your whole hand has slid up or down the
neck. That's your tuning check for the entire string.

<Margin>
  The A string sits in the top half of the treble staff: open **A** in the second space, **B**
  on the middle line, **C** in the third space, **D** on the fourth line, **E** in the top
  space. Reading these off the page comes in Movement B.
</Margin>

## The C is a choice

Natural **C** is a *low* 2 — back against the 1st finger. But most A-string tunes are in A
major or D major, and those want **C♯** — a *high* 2, slid forward against the 3rd finger.
This is the first place the low/high-2 decision from "Low fingers and high fingers" actually
bites in real music. When a tune sounds sour on this string, a natural C where it wanted C♯ is
the usual reason.

## The usual mistake

Playing natural C where A major wants C♯. And letting the 4th-finger E fall short — the little
finger is weak and lazy at first, so it lands low. Reach for it, and check it against the open
E every time.

<TryThis>
  A B C♯ D E and back, slowly, saying each name. Then the same with natural C, and hear the
  difference. Finish by checking 4th-finger E against the open E string — they should ring as
  one.
</TryThis>
