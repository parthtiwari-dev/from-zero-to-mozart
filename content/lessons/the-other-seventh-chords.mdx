A reference card. The **dominant seventh** (last lesson) is the one that runs Classical music.
There are four other seventh-chord types — you'll meet them properly in jazz and Romantic
harmony, but it's worth knowing they exist and roughly what each sounds like.

| Chord | Recipe | Sound | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Major 7th** (Cmaj7) | major triad + **major** 7th | lush, still, slightly dreamy | jazz; Romantic colour |
| **Minor 7th** (Cm7) | minor triad + minor 7th | mellow, soft-edged | jazz; the ii7 chord |
| **Dominant 7th** (C7) | major triad + minor 7th | *wants to move* | **everywhere in tonal music** — the one that counts |
| **Half-diminished 7th** (Cø7) | diminished triad + minor 7th | tense, aching, unresolved | ii°7 in a minor key; vii7 in major |
| **Fully diminished 7th** (C°7) | diminished triad + **diminished** 7th | maximum tension, no clear home | vii°7 in minor; a pivot to anywhere |

## Where you land

For Stage 4: recognise a four-note stacked chord as *a seventh chord*, and know that if it's
built on ^5 of the key and it's straining home, it's the **dominant 7th**. The rest is a
vocabulary you'll build later.

<Cite source="the five seventh-chord qualities — standard jazz and common-practice theory">
  Major-major, minor-minor, major-minor (dominant), half-diminished and fully diminished.
  Common-practice music leans overwhelmingly on the dominant seventh; the others come into
  their own from the late Romantics onward and in jazz.
</Cite>
