The strip of string between the bridge and the fingerboard is only a few centimetres wide, and
where in that strip your bow sits changes the sound completely. Teachers number it in **five
lanes** — **1 right by the bridge, 5 over the fingerboard** — and moving between them is one of
the most useful things you can do to your tone.

## What each end sounds like

- **Toward the bridge (lanes 1–2)** — the string is stiff there, so it takes a **slower bow
  and more weight** to speak. In return you get a **bright, focused, powerful** sound with lots
  of edge. It carries. It's also fussy — get the balance a little wrong and it scratches or
  skates.
- **Toward the fingerboard (lanes 4–5)** — the string is slack and responsive, so it wants a
  **faster, lighter bow**. The sound goes **soft, veiled, flute-like** — the Italians call it
  *flautando*, "flute-like". Easy to play, but it won't project.
- **The middle (lane 3)** — the forgiving default. A full, round, singing sound, and a wide
  margin for error. This is where you start every note and where most playing lives.

## Hear it — the whole sweep

The extremes have names you already met when you learned to *read* them (Stage 3): playing in
lane 1 is **sul ponticello** ("on the little bridge"); playing past lane 5 is **sul tasto**
("on the fingerboard"). Sweep between them and you hear the whole range.

Paul Rolland described the middle zone as sounding like a clarinet — "full, round" — the bridge
end like an oboe — "brilliant, intense" — and the fingerboard end like a flute. Three reeds in
one string.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play open D in lane 3 and get a good full sound. Now, without changing anything else, move
  the bow one lane toward the bridge — it will start to scratch. Add a little weight and slow
  down until it clears and rings *brighter* than before. Then go the other way, toward the
  fingerboard, and lighten and speed up until it turns soft and hollow. You've just played
  three real tone colours on one open string.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Letting the bow **drift** between lanes without meaning to — usually toward the fingerboard,
because it's easier there. The sound goes vague and won't carry, and you don't notice because
each note is only slightly weaker than the last. Pick a lane for the phrase and hold it. Simon
Fischer's image: **ride the hair against the sounding point like a surfer riding against a
wall of water** — you're always leaning gently against that exact spot.
