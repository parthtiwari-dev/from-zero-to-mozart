Stack one more third on top of a triad and you have a **seventh chord** — four notes: root,
3rd, 5th, 7th. The one that matters for everything Classical is the **dominant seventh**, and
it's the engine that drives almost every "we've arrived home" in tonal music.

## What it is

Built on the **5th degree** — a **major triad plus a minor 7th** above the root. In G major
that's **D – F♯ – A – C** (sol – ti – re – fa). Written **V7**.

## Why it pulls so hard

The 3rd and the 7th of V7 are **^7 and ^4** of the key — the two most restless degrees — and
they sit a **tritone** apart. The whole chord is built around that tension. When it resolves:

- **^7 → ^1** (F♯ up to G) — the leading tone doing its job
- **^4 → ^3** (C down to B)

Both notes squeeze inward, and the chord collapses onto the **tonic triad**. That's the
strongest resolution in the language.

## "The dominant is transportation"

Because V7 → I is such a definite arrival, a piece can **move to a brand-new key** just by
playing *that key's* V7 first. Toss in the V7 of D major (A–C♯–E–G) and the ear is instantly
pointed at D. That's how modulation works, and it's why the circle of fifths is a map of where
music can travel.

## The usual mistake

Playing the leading tone (^7) of a V7 flat, or letting it sag downward. It has one job: climb
a half step to the tonic. Give it a touch of extra height and let it rise. The whole chord's
pull depends on that note leaning up.
