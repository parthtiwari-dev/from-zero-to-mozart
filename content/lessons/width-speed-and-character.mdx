Vibrato is not one setting. It has **two independent dials** — how far the pitch swings
(**width**) and how fast it swings (**speed**) — and you turn them to fit the music. A player
with one vibrato for everything sounds the same in a lullaby and a fanfare.

## The two dials

**Width.** The working range is roughly **a fifth to a third of a semitone** — a narrow
shimmer. Push it toward a full half-tone and it reads as dramatic, hot, Romantic. Pull it in
and it reads as controlled, clear, Classical.

**Speed.** Roughly **5 to 7 wobbles a second** for expressive playing. Slower feels broad and
singing; faster feels intense or brilliant.

They're **independent** — wide-and-slow, wide-and-fast, narrow-and-slow and narrow-and-fast
are four different sounds:

## Width follows the dynamic

The most natural pairing: **more vibrato in loud playing, less in soft.** A *forte* wants width
and energy behind it; a *piano* wants a narrow, still shimmer — sometimes almost none. This
links straight back to Stage 5's dynamics-with-the-bow: as the bow gets faster and heavier,
the vibrato opens up with it; as the bow eases, the vibrato closes down.

## Hear it

Two recordings: a **period-instrument Mozart** slow movement (narrow, cool, selective) and the
**Tchaikovsky or Bruch concerto** slow movement (wide, warm, continuous). Same instrument,
opposite ends of both dials. Neither is "better" — each fits its music.

## The usual mistake

One vibrato, applied to everything — usually a medium-fast, medium-narrow default that never
varies. And confusing "more expressive" with "wider": a narrow, *faster* vibrato can be more
intense than a wide slow one. Expression is choosing the dials, not maxing them.
