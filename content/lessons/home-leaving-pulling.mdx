Chord quality (major/minor) tells you a chord's *colour*. Chord **function** tells you its
*job* — where it sits in the story of the key. There are only three jobs, and hearing which
one is playing is how you follow a piece's harmony without a score.

## The three jobs

Stage 4's `the-chords-of-a-key` named these tonic / predominant / dominant. In plainer words:

- **Home** — the **I** chord (and its stand-in, **vi**). Rest. Nothing pending. The story is
  at a stable point.
- **Leaving** — **IV** and **ii**. Motion away from home; a touch of travel or lift. Not
  tense, just *departed*.
- **Pulling home** — **V**, **V7**, **vii°**. Tension, lean, the strong wish to resolve to I.
  V7 especially: it contains the tritone (^4 and ^7 grinding), and it *aches* to land.

Most tonal music is a loop: **home → leaving → pulling → home**, over and over, at different
speeds. Hear the loop and you can predict where the harmony is going.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play open G–D as a drone-ish double stop (that's the bottom of I). Add B on the A string —
  a G chord, home. Now move to C–E–G in your ear (or play C on the G string, 3rd finger, with
  open D above) — IV, leaving. Now D–F♯–A — V, pulling. Back to G — home. Say the job out loud
  each time. Do it as a slow four-chord loop until "pulling home" is unmistakable.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to name the exact chord (a G, a C, an E minor) before you can hear its function. Flip
that: **function first** — is it resting, leaving, or pulling? — then the specific chord
follows. The other mistake: hearing IV as tense. It isn't; it's just *away*. Only the V-group
truly pulls.
