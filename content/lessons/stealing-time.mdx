Stage 3 taught you to *read* the tempo instructions — *rit.*, *accel.*, *rubato*. This lesson
is about doing it yourself, in a phrase that has **no marking at all**, because the music wants
it. The word is **rubato** — Italian for "robbed" — and the rule that keeps it from turning
into a mess is: **steal time, then give it back.**

## Steal, and repay

A phrase has a shape: it presses toward a high point and settles toward its close. Time can
follow that shape — hurry a little into the rise, **broaden** on the peak, then ease back so
the phrase still ends where the pulse says it should. What you took at the top, you repay on
the way down. The bar lands on time; the *inside* of it breathed.

## Leopold Mozart's picture

He described rubato as the melody pressing ahead and falling back **while the accompaniment
keeps strict time** — the tension between the two is the expression. Chopin said the same a
century later: the left hand is the conductor and keeps time; the right hand is free over it.
On a solo line you play both roles — one part of you keeps the pulse, another part bends over
it.

## Where it belongs — and where it doesn't

Rubato is generous in **Chopin, Kreisler, late-Romantic** playing — a real, audible ebb and
flow. In **Mozart** it's tiny and structural: a hair of space before an important arrival, a
breath at a phrase end, never a wilful slowing. Playing Mozart with Romantic rubato is the same
style error as playing him with Romantic vibrato (Movement F).

## The usual mistake

Stealing time and never giving it back — the phrase just gets slower and slower and the pulse
dissolves. Rubato is a *loan*, not a gift. The opposite mistake is rubato so constant and
wilful that there's no steady pulse left for it to pull against.
