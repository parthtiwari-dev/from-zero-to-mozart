You met the basic shape back in Stage 2: noteheads stacked at the same point in time mean
"play these together," not one after another. Here's the fuller notation around that idea.

A **double stop** is two notes at once, on two strings — two stacked noteheads. Three or four
stacked noteheads are a **chord** (or "multiple stop").

<NotatedExample

M:C
L:1/4
K:C clef=treble
[DA]2 !arpeggio![DFA]2 |]`}
  caption="A plain double stop (D + A together), then a three-note chord marked to be rolled — the wavy line means spread the notes quickly bottom to top rather than striking them all at once."
  defaultBpm={60}
/>

That wavy line before a chord is important: the curved bridge on a violin makes it physically
impossible to sound three or four strings in a single simultaneous stroke, so composers either
accept a quick **roll** (written with the wavy line, or just implied by convention) or write the
chord knowing a player will break it. Don't read a plain chord symbol as "all notes perfectly
simultaneous, no exceptions" — the physical reality of the instrument is baked into how it's
performed.
