Before you can hear a note as ^3 or ^6, there has to be a **home** for it to be ^3 or ^6
*of*. Establishing that home — planting the tonic in your ear — is the first move of every
listening task, and it's a move you already know from choosing Sa.

## Set the key, then everything has a place

Two ways to put "home" in your ear before a drill:

- **A drone on the tonic.** Hold ^1 underneath (the practice bar now has G, D, A, E, B and
  C). Every note you then hear is measured against that constant — its distance and its
  pull. This is the sargam/tanpura method exactly.
- **A short I–IV–V–I.** Four chords, or even just **^1 ^4 ^5 ^1** played as single notes,
  tells your ear where home is by *going away from it and coming back*. Useful when you can't
  keep a drone running — it "tunes" your ear to the key in two seconds.

Once home is set, a melody stops being a string of unrelated pitches. It becomes a set of
distances from one known point — which is a thing the ear can actually hold and repeat.

## The "is this home?" drill

The most basic functional judgement: a note sounds — **is it ^1, or not?** Home has a
particular feeling of arrival, of the tension going slack. Everything else is, to some
degree, *away*.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Set a G drone. Play, at random, either G (^1) or one of A / B / e (^2 / ^3 / ^6). Each time,
  before you look at your hand, ask: *was that home?* Home feels settled; the others each pull
  somewhere. Do ten. You'll be near-perfect on ^1-or-not within a few sessions — it's the
  easiest functional call there is.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Starting a listening drill cold, with no key set — then you're hearing intervals in a vacuum
and every note is ambiguous. Always plant home first: a drone, or a quick ^1–^4–^5–^1. The
other mistake: confusing ^5 with ^1. ^5 is stable too, and it can *feel* like a resting
place — but it's a lit, open kind of stable, not the "we're done" of ^1. Sing down from it
and you'll feel it wants one more step home.
