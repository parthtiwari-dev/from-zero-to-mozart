You know that **V7 pulls hard to I** — the leading tone climbing home, the 7th falling. A
**secondary dominant** borrows that pull and points it at a chord that *isn't* the tonic. It's
the single most common way tonal music adds a splash of chromatic colour.

## "A dominant of a dominant"

In G major, the dominant is D. What's the dominant *of D*? It's A — and **A7** (A–C♯–E–G)
pulls toward D exactly the way D7 pulls toward G. Drop an A7 into a G-major phrase just before
the D chord, and for one moment D feels like a mini-tonic. That A7 is written **V7/V** ("five
of five").

## The tell: an unexpected accidental that resolves up

You spot a secondary dominant on the page as a **sharp (or natural) that doesn't belong to the
key** — and it almost always resolves **up by a half step** into the next chord's root or 3rd.
The C♯ above, in G major, is the giveaway: it's not in the key, and it climbs to D.

Any diatonic chord can get its own secondary dominant: **V7/ii, V7/iii, V7/IV, V7/vi**. In a
Mozart or a Schubert phrase you'll see several in a row — a chain of little "arrivals" on the
way to the real cadence.

## This is called *tonicization*

Making a chord *briefly* feel like a tonic — without actually changing key — is
**tonicization**. A secondary dominant is how you do it. If the new "tonic" sticks around for
a while with its own cadence, that's a full **modulation** (Movement C); a quick in-and-out is
just tonicization.

## The usual mistake

Reading the out-of-key accidental as a mistake or a modulation. It's neither — it's a
**borrowed pull**, on loan for one chord. Follow it: it will resolve up a half step, and then
the music is back in the home key as if nothing happened.
