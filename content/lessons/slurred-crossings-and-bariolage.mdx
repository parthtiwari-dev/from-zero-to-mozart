Crossing a string on **separate bows** is one thing — the crossing lines up with the bow
change, so the two events happen together. Crossing **inside one slur** is harder: the bow is
still travelling the same direction, and it has to change strings mid-stroke without a bump.

## The mid-bow crossing — the crescent path

When a slur crosses strings, the bow doesn't travel in a straight line — it traces a shallow
**arc**, or crescent, so the hair rolls onto the new string smoothly instead of clipping the
edge of it. The move is mostly **forearm rotation** (the doorknob turn from `string-crossings`),
small and continuous.

## Bariolage — the rocking stroke

**Bariolage** (French, "multi-coloured") is rapid rocking between two neighbouring strings,
usually with **one of them an open string** — often the *same pitch* played alternately
stopped and open, so you hear the two strings' different colours flicker. Bach's *E-major
Partita* Preludio is the famous case; it turns up all over Baroque music and in fiddle
playing.

As *repertoire* it's for later. But the **rocking motion itself** is the clearest possible
feel of a forearm-rotation crossing, and it uses open strings — which you have — so it's worth
trying now.

## The usual mistake

Using the whole **upper arm** for a fast slurred crossing or a bariolage — it's too big and
too slow, so the notes come out uneven and lurching. Fast crossings are a **forearm** job: a
small rotation, elbow nearly still. The other mistake in a slurred crossing: slowing the bow
at the crossing point, which drops the sound.

<Cite source="J. S. Bach, Partita No. 3 in E major, BWV 1006, Preludio; the term 'bariolage' — French, from bariolé, 'streaked with colour'">
  Bach writes the Preludio's bariolage with an open E string ringing against stopped notes on
  the A string — sometimes the *same* pitch on both — so the ear hears a shimmer of changing
  colour under a single fast line.
</Cite>
