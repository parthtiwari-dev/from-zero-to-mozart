This course teaches you to **read, play, hear and understand music** on the violin — until you
can open an unfamiliar piece and make something honest of it on your own. Mozart's Third Violin
Concerto is the summit it points toward, the piece that proves you've got there — not the
finish line in itself. Before any of that, here is how the course is built and how to use it.

## Three separate skills

Playing the violin from a page is really three jobs, and they grow at different speeds:

- **Reading** — looking at a note and knowing its name, which string and finger it's on, how
  long it lasts, and what every mark around it is asking for.
- **Playing** — your hands and bow actually doing it, in tune, with a good sound.
- **Interpreting** — deciding how it should *go*: the phrasing, the character, the choices a
  performer makes.

You will get good at **reading** long before you can **play** everything you can read, and
that is exactly the plan — this course is honest about which layer each lesson is training.
Your first real victory isn't imitating a fingering video. It's this: *you can look at a note,
know what it is, know where it lives on the violin, know how long it lasts, and explain the
other marks around it.*

## Nothing gates you

There are no streaks, no locked lessons, no quiz you have to pass to continue. Worksheets are
offered; the answer is always one tap away. Go at your own pace, repeat anything, skip ahead
and come back. The order is a good default, not a rule.

## Four tags

Every lesson carries one:

| Tag | Means |
|---|---|
| **NOW** | Build this skill in your current stage. Do the exercises. |
| **NEXT** | Add it once your first-position reading and hand feel settled. |
| **LATER** | Real for Mozart and serious repertoire, but premature today. |
| **REFERENCE** | Know what the word or symbol means so a score doesn't look alien — no need to drill it. |

## The scaffolding comes off

Early notated examples show the **note name, string and finger under every note**. As you get
steadier, those cues drop away one at a time, until you're reading plain notation. Always try
an example first — play it, work it out — *then* reveal the answer.

One more thing you'll see often: violin fingering is **contextual**. A written note can
sometimes be played more than one way. Where that's true, this course gives you the
**beginner, first-position default** and tells you it's a choice — never a fake "one correct
fingering."
