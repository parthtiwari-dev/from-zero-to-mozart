A whole bow runs out. At the frog and at the tip you have to **turn the bow around** and go
the other way — and a good change is one nobody can hear. Two things can give it away: a
**gap** (a hole in the sound) or a **bump** (a little unwanted accent). The job is to have
neither.

## What a clean change is

Think of it as the bow **turning a corner**, not stopping and restarting. Three things make it
smooth:

- **Keep the bow moving through the turn.** The commonest fault is slowing down into the
  change and speeding up out of it — that dips the sound at every join. The speed stays even
  right through the corner.
- **Let the hand and wrist take up the reversal.** The arm changes direction a fraction before
  the hand does, so the fingers and wrist "give" and absorb the jolt. They're the shock
  absorber. A stiff hand makes a hard corner.
- **Match the weight across the change.** An up-bow starting at the frog wants more weight than
  feels natural; a down-bow arriving at the tip wants more arm. Otherwise the new stroke
  starts weaker than the old one ended.

## Hear the difference

Play the same four notes twice: first deliberately **stop** the bow at each change (you'll
hear four separate notes with silence between — that's a *staccato*, and it's a real stroke,
later). Then play them **joined**, turning the corner without stopping. The second version is
one unbroken sound that just changes direction. That's *legato*, and the bow change is the
thing that makes it possible.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, play slow half-bows near the frog, down and up, and put a finger of
  your left hand lightly on the bow stick near the frog so you can feel it reverse. Feel how
  the fingers flex as the direction turns. Then do the same near the tip. The change near the
  frog needs the most give; near the tip it's smaller.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Squeezing the bow at the moment of the change — clamping the fingers down to "control" it. A
clamped hand can't absorb the reversal, so it passes the jolt straight to the string as a
bump. Keep the hold loose right through the corner; the bow almost turns itself if you let it.

<Cite source="Ivan Galamian, Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching (1962) — the 'System of Springs'">
  Galamian described the whole bow arm — shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers — as a set of springs,
  each giving a little so that force reaches the string smoothly and no joint ever locks. The
  bow change is where you first feel that system working.
</Cite>
