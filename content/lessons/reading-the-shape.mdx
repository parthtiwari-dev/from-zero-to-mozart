Stage 1's `steps-and-skips` taught you to see whether a line moves by step or by skip. Fluent
reading takes that further: you read the **whole contour** — the up-and-down shape of the
line — as one thing, and the exact notes fall into place inside it.

## Step, skip, leap — on the page

The staff makes the distance visible:

- **Step** (a 2nd) — line to the next space, or space to the next line. The note *changes
  colour*: line → space. Adjacent fingers, or the neighbouring note.
- **Skip** (a 3rd) — line to the next line, or space to the next space. The note *stays the
  same colour*: line → line. A finger's gap, or across a string.
- **Leap** (a 4th, 5th, 6th, octave) — a bigger jump; count the lines-and-spaces crossed. A
  string crossing, and/or a finger jump. You recognise these by *how far* the notehead moves,
  not by naming both notes.

## A string on the page

Picture the melody as a piece of string laid on the staff, and follow it with your eye — up,
over, down, along. You're tracking the *line*, not stopping at each bead. Where it climbs
smoothly, that's a scale. Where it zig-zags in even jumps, that's a broken chord. Where it
darts up and comes back, that's a turn or a neighbour figure. The next lesson names those
shapes.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take a first-position tune. Before playing, run your finger along the melody line on the
  page and *say the contour out loud* — "up a bit, big jump up, back down slowly, step step
  step, home." Don't name a single note. Then play it. Your hand will have a head start,
  because it knows the *shape* it's about to make.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading the *interval between every pair of notes* as a separate calculation — "up a 2nd… up a
2nd… up a 2nd…" — which is barely faster than naming notes. The point is to see the *whole
run* as "a scale, up" in one glance. Steps and skips are how you *check* an unclear spot, not
how you read the easy parts.
