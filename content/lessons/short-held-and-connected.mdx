Every note is written with a length — a quarter, an eighth. A small mark over or under the
notehead then says **how much of that length to actually sound**, and whether to join it to
its neighbour. This is articulation, and it runs on a spectrum from a tiny click to a fully
connected line.

## The spectrum, shortest to longest

| Mark | Name | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| a **wedge** / spike ▾ | staccatissimo | very short — a spit, a fraction of the value |
| a **dot** · | staccato | roughly **half** the written value, clear air after |
| *(nothing)* | *(plain)* | the note as written, with a small natural gap |
| a **short line** ‒ | tenuto | the **full** value, held, with a slight lean into it |
| a **slur** ⌒ | legato | no gap at all — the notes joined, one continuous line |

The dot and the line are the two you'll meet most.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play G–A–B–C as four plain quarter notes at 72. Now **staccato**: stop the bow on the string
  after each, so half of every beat is silence. Now **tenuto**: each note its full length,
  only a tiny lift of weight between them. Now **slurred**: one bow, no stops at all. Feel your
  bow arm do four different jobs on the same notes.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing staccato by hitting **harder** — but staccato is *shorter*, not louder. Length and
accent are separate instructions. And playing tenuto as a punch: it's a held note with a lean,
not a jab. On the very short notes, keep the left-hand finger down through the silence — lift
it early and the pitch smears.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756)">
  Leopold Mozart treats the plain, unmarked note as already slightly detached — lifted, with
  air around it. The fully connected legato is the marked exception, not the default. Movement
  C's later lesson picks this up.
</Cite>
