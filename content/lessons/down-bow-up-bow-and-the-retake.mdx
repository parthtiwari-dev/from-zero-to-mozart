Two small symbols over the notes say which way to move the bow. You met them in Stage 0 (*the
geography of the bow*); here you learn to **read them in a part** — and to notice that they
tell you more than direction. They tell you where the strong beats are.

## The three marks

| Mark | Name | Direction | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **⊓** — a bracket / staple | down-bow | frog → tip, you **pull** | naturally the **stronger** stroke — gravity helps |
| **V** | up-bow | tip → frog, you **push** | naturally **lighter** |
| a **comma** or lift-hook | retake | lift the bow and reset (usually back toward the frog), **same direction** | resets the weight; makes a small silence |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play G–A–B–C as down–up–down–up. Feel the down-bows sit into the string with the arm's
  weight; the up-bows float. Now play the same four all **down-bow**, lifting and carrying the
  bow back to the frog between each. Slower, more deliberate — and every note has the same
  strong start.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Fighting a printed bowing because an up-bow "feels weak" on a note you want strong. The editor
or composer almost always chose it to make a *later* note land correctly — trust it and look
ahead. And treating a retake as just a quick up-bow: it's a **lift**, off the string and back,
with the silence that comes with it.

<Cite source="Georg Muffat, Florilegium Secundum (1698), preface; via David Boyden, The History of Violin Playing">
  The "rule of the down-bow" comes from Lully's orchestra, the 24 Violons du Roi: the first
  note of every bar is taken down-bow, so the naturally strong stroke lands on the naturally
  strong beat. The symbols themselves likely grew from the letters **n** (*nobilis*, strong)
  and **v** (*vilis*, weak).
</Cite>
