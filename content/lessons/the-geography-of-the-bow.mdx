Scores and teachers talk about *where* in the bow you are and *which way* it's going,
constantly. "At the frog." "Upper half." "Whole bow." Learn the map now so those instructions
mean something.

## Along the bow

<BowDiagram />

From your hand outward: the **frog** (also "the heel"), the **lower half**, the **middle**, the
**upper half**, the **tip** (also "the point"). The parts feel different — the frog is heavy
and powerful, the tip is light and needs help from your arm.

## Which direction

- **Down-bow** — written **⊓**, like a little table — you *pull*, moving from frog toward tip.
  It naturally gets a bit heavier as you go.
- **Up-bow** — written **V** — you *push*, tip back toward frog. It naturally gets lighter.

Strong beats often fall on down-bows — that's a Baroque default, "the rule of the down-bow",
not a law.

## Where it crosses the string

<BowLanesDiagram />

The bow travels in a straight lane **parallel to the bridge** — never drifting onto the
fingerboard or climbing the bridge. *Where* the hair meets the string, between bridge and
fingerboard, is the **contact point** (or sounding point), and you steer it. Galamian divides
that space into five lanes: lane 1 at the bridge is loud, full and hard to control; lane 5 at
the fingerboard is soft and flute-like. For now, start every note in **lane 3** and keep the
bow straight.

## The usual mistake

- the bow **skidding toward the fingerboard** as it nears the tip — the arm collapses; keep
  reaching;
- **climbing the bridge** near the frog;
- **changing the bow hold** to change strings — instead, raise or lower the **whole arm** to a
  new level, like stopping a lift on a different floor. The hand and bow just come along.
