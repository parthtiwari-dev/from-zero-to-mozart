On a piano, a dynamic is one thing: how hard you strike the key. On the violin it's **three
things at once**, all in the bow.

> **Read now, perform later.** This lesson is here so that when you *read* `f` or `<` you know
> what it's actually asking of your arm. Producing it with an even, controlled hand is
> **Stage 5**, where the bow strokes get their own full treatment.

## The three ingredients

| On the page | Bow speed | Bow weight | Contact point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **p** | slower | light | toward the **fingerboard** — soft, few overtones |
| **f** | faster | fuller | toward the **bridge** — bright, lots of core |
| **`<`** | gradually faster | gradually fuller | drift **toward the bridge** as it grows |
| **`>`** | gradually slower | gradually lighter | drift **toward the fingerboard** as it fades |

You met the "lanes" between bridge and fingerboard in Stage 0 (*the geography of the bow*).
Louder music lives in the lanes nearer the bridge; softer music nearer the fingerboard.
**Weight is the smallest of the three** — most of a dynamic is speed and where the bow is.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  One very slow bow on the open A. Start it near the fingerboard, light and unhurried. Halfway
  through the bow, drift toward the bridge and let the bow speed up — the note swells. Then
  drift back and slow down — it fades. One unbroken sound that grows and dies: that's what a
  `<` `>` over a long note is asking for.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Making every dynamic with **weight** — pressing down for `f` until the string chokes and
scratches, lightening for `p` until it goes breathy and whistles. Reach for **bow speed** and
**contact point** first; add weight last and least. A bigger sound usually means *move the
bow toward the bridge*, not *push harder where you are*.

<Cite source="Ivan Galamian, Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching (1962); Giuseppe Tartini, on the messa di voce">
  Galamian's model: tone is bow speed, bow weight and sounding point, governed together.
  Tartini told his students that mastering the swell-and-fade on a single note is equal to
  mastering the bow itself.
</Cite>
