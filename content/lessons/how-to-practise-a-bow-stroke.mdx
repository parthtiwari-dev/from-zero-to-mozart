## Why it's worth a method

A bow stroke is a **gesture you're grooving into your arm** — like a tennis serve or a
handwriting stroke. Practised carelessly, the arm learns the careless version and you're stuck
with it. Practised slowly and exactly a few minutes a day, it becomes automatic and clean.
Every stroke lesson in this stage assumes you'll come back and work it this way.

## The routine

1. **Read it first.** Know what the stroke is *for*, where in the bow it lives, and what it
   should sound like — before the bow moves.
2. **Slow, and from the string.** Start with the hair resting on the string, not dropped from
   the air. Slow enough that you can watch every part of the motion.
3. **One element at a time.** Working on the attack? Ignore the rest of the note for now.
   Working on evenness? Forget the attack. Don't try to fix four things at once.
4. **On an open string first**, then a one-octave scale, then a fragment of a real tune. Add
   the left hand only once the bow arm is doing its job on its own.
5. **Stop the moment the hand tightens.** A tense stroke only teaches tension. Shake the arm
   out, and start again loose — or stop for the day.

## The two exercises to keep forever

- **Son filé** (`one-long-tone`) — one sustained note, whole bow, frog to tip, 30 seconds and
  building toward a minute, metronome at 60. Every day, at the start. It levels the bow's
  weight and trains the ear more than anything else.
- **Bowing variations** — take a one-octave scale and apply one bowing pattern to the whole
  thing (all separate; two slurred + two separate; three slurred + one; hooked), then the next
  pattern, and so on. You'll meet this properly in `legato-and-slurs`. It's the single most
  efficient bow-control workout there is, and it plays straight to your feel for rhythm — each
  pattern is a small rhythmic cell laid over the notes.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Pick any stroke you're currently learning. Spend three minutes: one minute on open A just
  feeling the motion slowly, one minute on a G major scale with that stroke, one minute on the
  first line of a tune you know. Then stop. Short and exact, every day, beats a long grind once
  a week.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Practising a stroke fast before it's clean slow — "I'll get it up to speed and then it'll
click." It won't; speed just locks in whatever's wrong. Also: practising a stroke *only* on
scales and never on real music, so it never transfers to a phrase. Do both.

<Cite source="Simon Fischer, Practice and Basics; Ivan Galamian's practice method">
  Galamian's principle: work in short focused bursts with rests, not one long crawl, so the
  arm learns to move and then let go. And always slowly enough that the motion is *correct* —
  correct slow becomes fast on its own; fast and wrong stays wrong.
</Cite>
