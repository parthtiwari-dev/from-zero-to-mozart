Modern violinists vibrate almost all the time — it's the default sound, and turning it *off*
is the deliberate choice. For most of the violin's history it was the other way round: vibrato
was an **ornament**, chosen note by note, like a trill. Knowing that is what lets you play
Mozart in Mozart's style instead of Tchaikovsky's.

## The short history

- **1751 — Geminiani** tells students to use vibrato "as often as possible." He's the outlier,
  and even he cut that advice from later editions of his treatise.
- **1756 — Leopold Mozart** (Mozart's father, and his teacher) calls it "the tremolo" and
  treats it as an ornament that "arises from Nature herself" — the wave you hear when a bell is
  struck. Use it *"on a long note,"* and *"only... where nature herself would produce it."*
  And the famous warning: *"Performers there are who tremble consistently on each note as if
  they had the palsy."*
- **1832 — Spohr** teaches a restrained, intentional vibrato for passionate passages and
  swelled long notes — and actually writes little signs in his music marking *where* to do it.
- **Mendelssohn** did the same in his violin concerto — specific notes marked for vibrato,
  the rest plain.
- **c. 1900 — Ysaÿe, then Kreisler.** Kreisler vibrated *constantly*, even through fast
  running passages — something no famous player had done. Carl Flesch called his vibrato
  "extraordinarily intensive." It caught on, and within a generation it was the norm.
- **1921 — Auer** is still fighting it: *"This curious habit of vibrating on each and every
  tone amounts to a physical defect."*

The New Grove Dictionary sums it up: vibrato "seems always to have been accepted as an ornament
until the first quarter of the 20th century, when its continuous use gradually became the
norm."

## The honest version

It was **not** "nobody used vibrato before Kreisler." Early recordings show it was there — but
*selective and varied*, applied to chosen notes for chosen effects, not poured over everything.
The change was from vibrato as a spice to vibrato as the broth.

**Hear the contrast:** put on one of **Fritz Kreisler's own recordings** (his 1920s *Liebesleid*
or *Schön Rosmarin*) — the vibrato never stops. Then put on a **period-instrument Mozart**
recording (Rachel Podger, or the Academy of Ancient Music) — the vibrato comes and goes, an
ornament on chosen notes. Neither is wrong; they're different styles, and Mozart belongs to
the second one.

## The usual mistake

Assuming "more vibrato = more expressive = better," and pouring it over a Classical phrase.
For Mozart, *less* and *narrower* and *chosen* is the expressive choice. This is the same rule
Stage 3 gave you for every symbol: **the mark tells you what, the era tells you how.**
Movement F comes back to this in full.
