Two more small-symbol ornaments. The **mordent** is a single quick flip to a neighbour note
and back. The **turn** is a little four-note curl around the note.

## The symbols

*(P = the written note · U = the step above · L = the step below)*

| Symbol | Ornament | Play |
|---|---|---|
| a short **zigzag**, no line | upper mordent | **P – U – P**, fast |
| a short **zigzag with a vertical line** | lower mordent | **P – L – P**, fast |
| a lazy sideways **S** | turn | **U – P – L – P** |
| the **S with a vertical stroke** (or drawn upside-down) | inverted turn | **L – P – U – P** |

**Where the turn sits matters:** directly **above** a note → play it on that note. Offset to
the **right**, between two notes → a **delayed** turn: hold the first note, then curl into the
next. And an **accidental above** the sign bends the **top** note of the ornament; **below**
it, the **bottom** note.

## The naming trap

In a **modern** edition, "upper mordent" and "lower mordent" mean exactly what the table
above says. But in **Baroque** usage the bare word **"mordent"** meant only the **lower** one
(P–L–P). The upper flip had its own names — **Pralltriller** or **Schneller** — and, depending
on context, its own slightly different execution.

So the same zigzag can mean different things in a Bach edition and a modern one. Recognise the
shape, note the era, and don't lose sleep over it.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the B (1st finger, A string): play **B–C–B** as fast as you cleanly can, then stop.
  That's an upper mordent. Now **C–B–A–B**, fast, then stop — a turn. These two turn up
  constantly in Classical music; get them into the fingers slowly first.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Playing the turn's four notes as even, deliberate sixteenths — it should sound like a single
gesture *decorating* the beat, not a scale fragment. And on the mordent, mixing up the two
kinds: no line through the zigzag goes **up**; a line through it goes **down**.
