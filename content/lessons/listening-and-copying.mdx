You didn't learn to talk from a grammar book — you copied the people around you, for years,
before you knew a single rule. Expression works the same way. The fastest route to a phrasing
vocabulary is to **copy great players**, closely and deliberately, and only then start making
your own choices.

## The method

1. **Pick one player and one short phrase** — four bars of a recording you love.
2. **Listen ten times.** Sing along until you have the phrase — the tune, but also the
   *breaths*, the swells, where the time bends.
3. **Copy it on the violin, as exactly as you can.** Match the shape: where it grows, where it
   breathes, how wide the vibrato is on the long note, where the player steals a little time.
4. **Then change one thing on purpose.** Breathe in a different place. Put the peak a beat
   later. Make the vibrato narrower. Listen to what that does.

Step 4 is the point. Copying builds the vocabulary; changing one thing turns it into a choice.

**Recordings to copy from:** the opening of the **Adagio of Mozart's K.216** — compare
**Hilary Hahn**, **Arthur Grumiaux** and **David Oistrakh**; each shapes the first phrase
differently. Or the opening of **Bach's *Air***. Pick one, copy it, then try another and hear
how two great players disagree.

## Hilary Hahn did exactly this

Hahn has said that every note she plays "began with the Twinkle Variations," and she credits
Suzuki with a lifelong habit of listening to recordings. The greatest players are the most
deliberate copyists — early on.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  One phrase, one recording, this week. Days 1–3: copy it as exactly as you can. Day 4: change

  change or the original. Either answer is fine; having an opinion is the win.

## The usual mistake

Two opposite ones. Copying forever and never risking a choice of your own — you become a
photocopy. Or refusing to copy at all, in the name of originality, and having no vocabulary to
be original *with*. Imitation is a stage on the way to choosing (that's the next movement), not
the destination.

<Cite source="Shinichi Suzuki, the mother-tongue method; Hilary Hahn interviews (Strings Magazine) on listening to recordings from childhood">
  Suzuki built a whole method on the observation that children learn music the way they learn
  language — by immersion and imitation, long before analysis. Copying a great phrase is how
  you find out what's possible; then you get to decide.
</Cite>
