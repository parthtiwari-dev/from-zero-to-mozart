The violin has no frets, so a finger isn't tied to one spot. The same finger can land a little
lower or a little higher — a **half step** apart — and that's two different notes. One finger,
two notes. This is how the violin plays every sharp and flat: not with a different finger, with
the *same* finger moved.

## Which fingers have a choice

- **1st finger** — only on the **E string**. F♮ is a *low* 1, pulled back toward the nut; F♯
  is the normal 1. On the other three strings the 1st finger has one home.
- **2nd finger** — on *every* string, and this is the one you meet constantly. **Low 2** sits
  back against the 1st finger; **high 2** sits forward against the 3rd — a full centimetre
  apart. F♮ / F♯ on the D string, C♮ / C♯ on the A, B♭ / B♮ on the G.
- **3rd finger** — 3 and high 3 (G♯, D♯, A♯). Less common early; you meet it in the sharp keys.

The half step is the two fingers **almost touching**. The whole step is a finger's-width gap.
Move one finger and the whole hand shape changes — back to the four finger patterns.

## The usual mistake

Forgetting the E string's low 1. A tune sits on the E string, the music writes F♮ just above
the open string, and the hand plays F♯ out of habit — a half step sharp, and sour. When a note
is a step above an open string, ask *which* step: a whole step (normal finger) or a half step
(finger pulled back).
