Music is built in nested units, like language builds words into sentences into paragraphs.
Three sizes are worth naming.

## Motif → phrase → period

| Unit | Size | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Motif** | 2–6 notes | the smallest recognisable idea — the seed a piece grows from |
| **Phrase** | ~4 bars | a musical sentence: a beginning, a middle, and an end (a cadence). "One breath." |
| **Period** | ~8 bars | two phrases — a **question** and an **answer** |

**The motif.** Ode to Joy's whole tune grows from one tiny stepwise idea. Beethoven's Fifth
Symphony is built almost entirely from four notes — "da-da-da-DUM."

## The period: question and answer

A period is an **antecedent** phrase (ends weak — a half cadence — "?") followed by a
**consequent** phrase (ends strong — a perfect cadence — "."). The two usually *start* the
same and *end* differently.

## Sequence — a motif that climbs

One more device: a **melodic sequence** is a motif immediately repeated a step (or a third)
higher or lower. It's how a tune develops without new material — and it's what a
circle-of-fifths progression sounds like as a melody.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the period example above. Stop at the end of bar 2 (on C) and wait — feel it hang,
  unanswered. Then play bars 3–4 and let the G land. Now try playing *only* the antecedent, or
  *only* the consequent: each sounds incomplete on its own. A period is a matched pair.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading a long melody as one undifferentiated stream. It has joints — every ~4 bars there's a
cadence, a breath, a phrase boundary. Find them (the cadence lesson tells you how), and the
tune stops being a wall of notes and becomes a set of sentences you can shape one at a time.

<Cite source="Classical phrase structure — William Caplin, Classical Form (1998); Arnold Schoenberg, Fundamentals of Musical Composition">
  The period and the sentence are the two standard 8-bar theme types of the Classical style.
  The period is a question-and-answer built from two phrases with contrasting cadences.
</Cite>
