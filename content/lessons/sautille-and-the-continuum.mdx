**Sautillé** is a fast bouncing stroke where **the bow bounces itself** — you're not lifting
each note. The springiness of the stick does the work at speed. It's a **LATER** stroke, and
the useful thing to understand now is how it relates to spiccato: they're the **same gesture
at two different speeds.**

## Sautillé — the bounce that happens *to* you

- **The stick springs.** At a fast enough tempo, a small, light stroke in the middle of the
  bow starts to bounce on its own — the stick flexes and rebounds and you just keep it going.
  The hair barely leaves the string.
- **Dead centre of the bow**, where the stick flexes most.
- **Fast only** — roughly above 120–130 notes a minute. Slower than that and it won't
  self-sustain, and you're back to spiccato.
- **Sound:** light, glittering, brilliant, and more *connected* than spiccato — the notes blur
  together a little.

## The continuum — tempo moves you along it

This is the whole idea, and it's the one thing to take away:

> **Slow bounce → you lift and drop each note → that's spiccato.**
> Speed it up. At some point you *can't* lift each note individually any more, and the bounce
> transfers to the flex of the stick → **that's sautillé.**
> Same gesture. Different speed. Not two unrelated tricks.

So the way in, later, is: get a comfortable spiccato going, then gradually play it faster and
faster until it "takes off" and starts bouncing on its own. You don't switch techniques; you
slide along the continuum.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Nothing to drill here yet — this one is genuinely LATER. But you can *feel* the idea: play a
  light détaché on open A in the middle of the bow, small strokes, and slowly speed up. Keep
  it light. Somewhere fast, the bow may start to skip a little on its own. That skip is the
  seed of both spiccato and sautillé.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to actively bounce a sautillé — pumping the hand up and down fast. It can't keep up,
and the stroke collapses. Sautillé is *let* to happen: a small, loose, fast stroke, and the
stick does the bouncing. The other mistake is expecting a slow sautillé — there's no such
thing; below its tempo it's spiccato.

<Cite source="The Strad, 'Spiccato and sautillé — two important bow strokes which are often confused'; virtualsheetmusic bow-stroke guides">
  The clearest way teachers describe it: in spiccato *you* jump the bow; in sautillé the
  *stick* jumps and you go along with it. Tempo is what moves you from one to the other.
</Cite>
