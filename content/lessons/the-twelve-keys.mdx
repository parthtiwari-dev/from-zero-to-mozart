## Twelve keys, fifteen signatures

There are **12** notes to build a major key on. But there are **15** possible written key
signatures, because **three keys have two legal spellings**:

| Sharp spelling | = Flat spelling |
|---|---|
| **B** major (5 sharps) | **C♭** major (7 flats) |
| **F♯** major (6 sharps) | **G♭** major (6 flats) |
| **C♯** major (7 sharps) | **D♭** major (5 flats) |

Same piano keys, same violin fingers — different names on the page. So "12 pitch classes"
does **not** mean "12 key signatures." A composer picks the spelling with **fewer
accidentals** (D♭'s five flats beat C♯'s seven sharps), or the one that fits where the music
is heading.

| A | 3♯ | A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯ A |
| E | 4♯ | E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E |
| B | 5♯ | B C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A♯ B |
| F♯ | 6♯ | F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯ F♯ |
| F | 1♭ | F G A B♭ C D E F |
| B♭ | 2♭ | B♭ C D E♭ F G A B♭ |
| E♭ | 3♭ | E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D E♭ |
| A♭ | 4♭ | A♭ B♭ C D♭ E♭ F G A♭ |
| D♭ | 5♭ | D♭ E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭ C D♭ |
| G♭ | 6♭ | G♭ A♭ B♭ C♭ D♭ E♭ F G♭ |

The ones you'll actually play are at the top: **C, G, D, A** major this stage (plus **E
minor**), and **F** and **B♭** as your fingering grows.

## Double sharps and double flats

A **double sharp** (an **x**-shaped sign) raises a note two half steps; a **double flat**
(**♭♭**) lowers it two. They never sit in a standard key signature — but they appear as
**accidentals**, most often as a **raised leading tone inside a sharp minor key**. G♯ minor's
leading tone is written **F-double-sharp** (F♯ raised again — it *sounds* like G natural, but
it's spelled as a raised 7th degree, and the 7th degree of G♯ is some kind of F).

## Where you land

Read a signature, name the key with the tricks from *Key signatures in full*, check whether
it's the major or the relative minor, and know that a strange-looking spelling (C♭,
F-double-sharp) is
just a familiar sound wearing an unfamiliar name.
