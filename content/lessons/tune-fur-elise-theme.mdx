Beethoven's *Für Elise* opens with one of the most recognisable ideas in music — and it's a
perfect little study in **a minor key and its leading tone.** First position, A minor, in
**3/8** (a quick, light triple pulse).

## The oscillation — and the leading tone

The famous opening rocks between **E** and **D♯**. That D♯ is the **leading tone of the
dominant** (E is ^5 of A minor; D♯ is a half step below it) — a chromatic lean that gives the
tune its restless, questioning tug. Then the line falls **B D C** down to **A**, home.

## The answering figures — two rising arpeggios

The phrase answers itself with two **rising arpeggios**: **C–E–A** (the A minor chord),
landing on B; then **E–G♯–B** (an E major chord — there's the **raised leading tone G♯**),
landing on C.

## The usual mistake

Playing D♯ and G♯ flat — as plain D and G — out of "it's a minor key" habit. Both are raised
leading tones doing a specific job: D♯ leans toward E, G♯ leans toward A. Place them high, up
against the next finger, and let your ear hear them pull. The other mistake: heavy, romantic
playing. Für Elise is delicate — small bows, light fingers.
