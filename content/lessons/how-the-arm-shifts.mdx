A shift is not "slide the finger." It's a coordinated move of the **whole arm** — the finger
just happens to be along for the ride, in contact with the string the entire time.

## Two zones, two leaders

- **Shifting up to about 3rd position:** the **lower arm** (forearm) leads. The hand and
  fingers travel with it; the upper arm swings gently back toward your body as the elbow drops
  a little under the violin.
- **Shifting higher than that:** the **hand** starts to lead the fingers more, and the **upper
  arm** takes over more of the work — the elbow swings further under the instrument as the hand
  travels toward the violin's body.

You don't need to think about this mechanically while playing — it happens on its own once the
arm is loose. But it explains *why* a big shift into 5th position feels different in the arm
than the small hop into 3rd: different joints are doing the leading.

## The thumb goes first — on the way down

Going **up** the neck, the thumb just travels along with the hand. Going **down**, the
**thumb has to arrive first** — before the finger lands on its new note. If the thumb drags
along behind, the hand comes down flat-footed and the finger lands late and unsteady.

## No squeeze, even in motion

Stage 0 already taught you: the left hand doesn't grip the neck. That matters even more
mid-shift — squeezing the string, or clamping the thumb against the neck, turns a clean glide
into a scrape and makes the landing insecure. The contact needs to stay **light** while the
hand is moving, and settle back to normal only once you've arrived.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the A string, play B with your 1st finger (1st position). Shift up to D, 1st finger, 3rd
  position — feel the forearm lead the motion, hand loose, no grip. Then shift back down to B —
  this time pay attention to your thumb: it should slide into place *before* the finger lands.
  Do this slowly, five times, before ever trying it in a piece.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Gripping the neck harder to "hold on" during the shift — it's an understandable instinct
(nobody wants to drop the violin) but it's exactly backwards: grip is what makes a shift
inaccurate. The violin doesn't need your left hand to hold it up; your jaw and shoulder already
do that job (Stage 0, `holding-the-violin`). The hand is free to travel.

<Cite source="The Strad, 'How to shift correctly on the violin,' citing Joseph Fuchs on Heifetz's thumb technique">
  Heifetz was famous for a thumb that stayed loose and resilient through every shift — never
  gripping, always arriving exactly on time. It's cited as one of the clearest examples of "the
  thumb leads on the way down" in the pedagogy literature.
</Cite>
