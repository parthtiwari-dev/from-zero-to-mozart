A triad has three notes, and **any** of them can be the lowest. Which one is on the bottom is
the triad's **inversion** — same chord, same name, different bass note, slightly different
feel.

| Position | Bass note | Shorthand | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Root position** | the root | *(no figure)* | most stable — the chord standing up |
| **First inversion** | the 3rd | **6** | lighter, mobile — very common under a melody |
| **Second inversion** | the 5th | **6/4** | unstable, wants to resolve — often decorates a cadence |

## Those numbers

**6** and **6/4** are **figured bass** — an old shorthand where the numbers count *intervals
above the bass note*. A "6" chord has a 6th and a 3rd above the bass; a "6/4" has a 6th and a
4th. Baroque keyboardists read a bass line plus these figures and built the chords on the fly.
You just need to **recognise** the figures when an edition or an analysis uses them.

## Why a composer inverts a chord

Two reasons, mostly:

- to give the **bass line a smooth shape** instead of jumping around;
- to keep a particular **melody note on top**.

The harmony doesn't change — only which note is holding the floor.

## The usual mistake

Treating an inverted triad as a new, different chord. It isn't — a first-inversion G major is
still G major, still doing the tonic's job in G. Learn to spot the *root* even when it's in the
middle or on top: find the two notes a 3rd apart at the bottom of the stack, and the lower of
those is usually the 3rd, which means the root is the note just below it in the chord.
