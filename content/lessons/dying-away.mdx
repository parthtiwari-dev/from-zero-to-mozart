A small family of Italian words all mean one thing: **let it die away**. And two more mean
something close but different — sing with a **deliberately covered voice**.

## Fading

| Word | Literally | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **morendo** | dying | fade toward silence |
| **calando** | dropping | softer *and* usually slower — a settling |
| **smorzando** (smorz.) | extinguishing | dampen the sound out |
| **perdendosi** | losing itself | thin away until it's gone |
| **al niente** / **a niente** | to nothing | the sound vanishes completely — often drawn as a hairpin ending in a small circle |

Most of these also relax the tempo a little as they fade — the music is coming to rest, not
just getting quiet.

## Covered voice

| Word | Literally | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **sotto voce** | under the voice | hushed, held in — quiet *and* veiled, like a secret |
| **mezza voce** | half voice | not full tone — a deliberately smaller, gentler sound, not necessarily soft |

## Try it

<TryThis>
  One long down-bow on the open D string. Start at a comfortable *mf* and, over the whole bow,
  thin it to *al niente* — the last few centimetres barely moving, the sound narrowing until
  you honestly cannot say where it stopped. Don't cut it off; let it evaporate.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reading all of these as plain `dim.` They're softer *and* the music is coming to rest — the
pulse eases with the volume. And on an `al niente`, chopping the sound off at the end instead
of letting it disappear. For `sotto voce`, playing merely quiet and missing the veiled,
withheld colour that's the actual instruction.

<Cite source="Grove, on expression marks; standard Romantic-opera practice">
  This vocabulary blooms in Romantic music — Verdi and Puccini end phrase after phrase
  *morendo*. It says: the sentence is finished; let it settle rather than stop.
</Cite>
