This is the single most concrete, useful fact in this whole stage — worth reading twice,
because it's counter-intuitive and easy to get backwards.

## The time comes from the note before, not the note after

Simon Fischer, quoting Galamian directly: *"One of the commonest faults found in shifting is
that of shortening the note preceding the move... Conscious attention to the rhythmic value
and sound of the note preceding the shift is imperative until correct habits are formed."*

Read that again: the fault is in the note **before** the shift, not the note you're shifting
*to*. The common beginner mistake is starting the physical shift exactly when the new note is
due — which forces a late, rushed, insecure landing. What you actually hear as "clipped" is the
*departure* note, even though the damage happens because the *arrival* got rushed.

**The fix: steal the shift's time from the note before it, on purpose.**

## The practice method, step by step

1. **Cut the note before the shift in half.** If it was a quarter note, play it as an eighth,
   then use the freed-up time to shift — deliberately early, and slow.
2. **Gradually lengthen that note back to full value** — which means delaying the start of the
   shift later and later — **while speeding up the shift itself**, until the glide takes only a
   fraction of a second and the new note lands exactly on the beat.

Do this slowly enough, early enough, and the two ends of the process meet in the middle: an
early, unhurried shift becomes a shift so fast it's barely there — landing precisely on time,
every time.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Take any shift you're working on. Play the note before it at **half** its written value,
  shift in the time you freed up, then hold the arrival note. Repeat five times. Then play the
  note before it at its **full** value, and see how much faster the shift itself has to be to
  still land on the beat. That's the whole method — do it daily on whatever shift is currently
  giving you trouble.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Waiting until the new note is due to start moving the hand. It feels intuitive — "I'll shift
right when I need the note" — but it's exactly backwards, and it's the single most common
shifting fault in the pedagogy literature for a reason: it guarantees a late, insecure landing
every time.
