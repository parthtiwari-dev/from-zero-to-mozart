An accent mark says: **lean on this one note.** More attack, a little more weight, so it
stands out from its neighbours. It does **not** change the dynamic level of the passage — it's
a local bump, not a new setting.

## The two marks

| Mark | Name | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **`>`** — a wedge open to the left | accent | a stress on the note — more bite at the start, within the current dynamic |
| **`^`** — a small caret / roof | marcato | heavier still, **and separated** — "marked", almost hammered; the note doesn't blend into the next |

You've also met **`sf` / `fz`** (Movement B) — a sharper, more sudden jab than a plain accent.
Same idea, more violent.

## Hear it

Eight even eighth notes, with an accent on each half-bar downbeat. Listen to the metre step
forward.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play eight even eighth notes on the open A at 96. Now put a quick catch of **bow speed** —
  not weight — on the first of each group of four. The note should bite at its start and then
  relax. You've placed an accent, and the eight notes now have a shape.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Making an accent by **crushing** the string — you get a scratch, not a stress. A fast little
surge of bow at the *start* of the note does it cleanly. Second: playing every accented note
up at `f` regardless of the passage — an accent in `p` stays inside `p`. Third: reading `>` the
accent as `>` the diminuendo hairpin. The accent sits on **one** note; the hairpin stretches
across several. Check the width.

<Cite source="Leopold Mozart, Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (1756); standard metrical practice">
  Accent normally follows the bar: the first beat is naturally the strongest, the third next.
  A written `>` either reinforces that pulse or deliberately pushes against it — and knowing
  which is half of reading the phrase.
</Cite>
