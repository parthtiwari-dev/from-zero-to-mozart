Minor comes in **three forms**. They all share one key signature — they differ only in how the
**6th and 7th degrees** behave near the top of the scale. Here's *why* there are three, using
**E minor** (one sharp, F♯) throughout.

## The problem natural minor has

Play E natural minor up and stop on the 7th degree, **D**:

That whole step means natural minor has **no leading tone** — no strong pull home. Composers
fix it by raising the 7th.

## Harmonic minor — raise the 7th

Raise **D to D♯**, a half step below the tonic. Now there's a real leading tone, and the chord
built on the 5th degree becomes major and strong (that's the "harmonic" part — it's about the
chords).

## Melodic minor — raise the 6th too, going up

The augmented 2nd sounds fine in a chord, but jumpy in a running melody. So when the line is
**ascending**, raise the **6th as well** (C → C♯) to smooth it out. Coming **down**, there's
nothing to smooth — no pull toward the tonic — so it reverts to plain natural minor.

## Hear the difference

<TryThis>
  On the A string: play C (low 2), then D (3rd), then E (open) — the top of natural minor,
  smooth and mild. Now play C (low 2), then **D♯** (3rd finger stretched forward), then E — the
  top of harmonic minor. Feel the stretch, hear the leap, then hear how hard that D♯ pulls up
  to E. That leap is the price of the leading tone.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to memorise all three forms in all twelve keys — the source book warns against exactly
this, and it's right. Learn the **idea**: one key signature, and the 6th/7th get raised or not
depending on whether you need a leading tone (harmonic) or a smooth melodic line (melodic).
Then drill the specific minor keys your pieces are actually in. For this stage, that's E minor.

<Cite source="the raised leading tone in minor — C.P.E. Bach and the common-practice theory tradition; the augmented-2nd colour is shared with flamenco, klezmer, and the pitch material of some ragas">
  Raising the 7th in minor is done to borrow the major key's strong "arrival" cadence. The
  wide ♭6–♯7 step it creates turns up in music all over the world — but a raga or a maqam is a
  whole grammar, not just this scale, so don't equate them.
</Cite>
