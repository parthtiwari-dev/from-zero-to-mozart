Plain détaché is one member of a family. The relatives are all "a separate bow per note" with
one thing changed — more bow, or a firmer start, or a gentle swell, or a small gap. You don't
need to drill these now; the point is to **recognise them** when a phrase asks for one, and to
try each once so the word means something.

<Margin>
  The sub-names aren't perfectly standard — different books draw the lines in slightly
  different places. Galamian's *Principles* is the usual reference. Treat the exact labels
  loosely; the *sounds* are what matter.
</Margin>

## Grand détaché — the big one

Whole bows, fast, with weight — a broad, ringing, energetic sound. Each note is so long it
feels slightly separated just from its size. This is the forte orchestral sound, and the sound
of the opening of the Mozart D-major concerto.

## Accented détaché — a firm start

A defined attack at the front of each note — a small lean or bite — then the note carries on
and rings. Used for marked, marcato passages where the notes should "speak" clearly without
being hammered.

## Détaché porté — the singing one

Each note gently **swelled** — a soft bloom in the middle, made with **bow speed**, not
weight, and no hard start. "Carried". This is the stroke of a lyrical slow movement, and it's
`dynamics-are-a-bow-thing` in miniature: you're shaping every single note with the bow.

## Détaché lancé — a small gap

A "darted" note: the bow slows or briefly stops between notes, leaving a tiny silence, but
**without** an accent. Light and articulate. Several often fit in one bow direction. It sits
between plain détaché and the stopped strokes coming up.

## How the family connects

Everything here is a dial between two neighbours you already know or are about to meet:

- add a gap → toward **staccato**
- add a hard prepared bite *and* a stop → **martelé** (next movement)
- swell each note with speed → **détaché porté**
- take away everything extra → plain **détaché**

## The usual mistake

Making *every* détaché an accented one — starting every note with a little push out of habit,
so a smooth passage comes out lumpy. Plain détaché has **no** accent. Save the firm start for
where the music actually asks for it.

<Cite source="Ivan Galamian, Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching (1962); the French school (Baillot, Rode, Kreutzer)">
  The French *Méthode du Conservatoire* catalogued the bow strokes into families like this in
  the early 1800s — the modern (Tourte) bow had just made a long, even, sustained détaché
  possible for the first time, and teachers were working out everything it could now do.
</Cite>
