In Stage 0 you built a bow hold that was comfortable *at rest*. This stage asks the hand to do
real work — sink weight for a loud note, prepare a sharp attack, absorb a bow change, roll
through a string crossing — and a hold that was fine sitting still tends to **lock up under
load**. Keeping the hand elastic *while it works* is what the whole stage's tone rests on.

## The string is holding the bow too

Most bow-hand tension comes from one wrong belief: *that you have to grip the bow to control
it.* On the string, the **string carries part of the bow's weight** — your hand only has to
**balance and steer** it. That's a much smaller job than "hold this stick up and press it
down."

## What each part does under load

- **Thumb** — bent, soft, tucked in the nook where the stick meets the frog. It's the
  **counter-pressure** point that everything else pushes against, and the pivot of the whole
  spring system. Never locked straight, never jammed hard.
- **Little finger** — curved, resting on top of the stick. It's the **counter-weight** that
  balances the heavy frog, and it's what **lifts the tip** in a retake or an off-string
  stroke. Near the tip it can relax right off.
- **Index finger** — sits slightly apart from the others. This is where the **arm's weight
  enters** the string. For a powerful stroke it slides a little further from the thumb, for
  leverage.
- **Middle two fingers** — draped over the stick, the steady contact that actually holds it.

## The tension audit — run it constantly

You'll see this check at the end of every stroke lesson, because a stiff hand quietly ruins
everything:

- Let your right hand hang **completely limp** at your side for a few seconds. Memorise that
  feeling of no effort. Pick up the bow and carry *that* in.
- While playing a slow note, **tap each bow-hand finger** in turn against the stick. If a
  finger won't tap, it's clenched — soften it.
- Check the **shoulder** (down, not hunched), the **thumb** (bent), and your **left hand and
  jaw** — bow-arm tension often shows up as a death-grip somewhere else.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Rest the bow on the open D string. With your **left** hand, hold the bow up too, taking most
  of its weight, so your right hand is barely doing anything. Play a slow note like that. Then
  very gradually let go with the left hand, letting the right hand take over — but keep the
  right hand as loose as it was when the left hand was helping. That looseness is the target.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Bracing the whole hand into a fixed shape and holding it there — especially for loud or
accented notes. A fixed hand can't spring, so every bow change and every accent comes out as a
jolt, and your forearm aches after ten minutes. The hand **assembles** power for the instant
it's needed and **releases** straight after. Firm, then loose, then firm again.
