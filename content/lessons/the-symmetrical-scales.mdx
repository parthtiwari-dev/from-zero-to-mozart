Two scales built from a **repeating step pattern**, so they look the same starting from any of
their notes. That symmetry is their whole character: **no note is more "home" than another**,
so the ear floats, unmoored. Composers use them for exactly that — dreams, magic, menace,
disorientation.

## Whole-tone — all whole steps

Six notes, every step a **whole step**. No half steps means no leading tone, no tonic pull.
Debussy's signature sound.

There are only **two** whole-tone scales in all of music — one starting on C, one on C♯ —
because after six whole steps you're back where you started.

## Octatonic (diminished) — alternating whole and half

**Eight** notes, alternating whole step, half step, whole, half… It has a jumpy, eerie,
"which key is this?" quality. Stravinsky and countless film composers use it for menace and
the supernatural.

## The augmented scale (a note)

One more symmetrical scale you'll see named: the **augmented** (or hexatonic) scale —
alternating **minor 3rd, half step** (C E♭ E G A♭ B, back to C). Six notes, built from two
augmented triads (C–E–G♯ and E♭–G–B). Used for a similar floating, uncanny effect. Recognise
the name; don't drill it.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the whole-tone scale from D against a D drone. Every note sits the *same* distance from
  its neighbour, and against the drone none of them "click" the way ^1, ^3, ^5 do in a major
  scale. It's like standing in a room with no walls — no reference point. That's the sound
  Debussy wanted.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Trying to hear these as "wrong major scales" and correcting the odd notes. They're *supposed*
to have no home. The whole point of a symmetrical scale is the absence of gravity — play it
as it is and let it drift.

<Cite source="symmetrical scales — the whole-tone scale (Debussy, Liszt) and the octatonic scale (Rimsky-Korsakov, Stravinsky, Messiaen); both defined by a repeating interval pattern">
  Because the step pattern repeats, these scales have no unique tonic — a feature, not a bug.
  Messiaen called scales like these "modes of limited transposition": there are only a handful
  of each, because the symmetry runs out.
</Cite>
