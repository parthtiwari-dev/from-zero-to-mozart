Right after the clef, before the music starts, you'll often see one or more sharps (or flats).
That's the **key signature** — a standing instruction: *these notes are sharp every time, in
every octave, for the whole piece*, unless a later mark cancels one. It saves writing the sign
on every single F.

## One sharp is G major

The single sharp is always **F♯**, and it means **G major** — the key of *Ode to Joy*, of the
open strings ringing, and of **Mozart's Violin Concerto No. 3**, the piece at the end of this
whole course.

## The order of sharps

They always arrive in the same order — **F C G D A E B** — and each key adds the next one:

| Sharps | Key | Which sharps |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | C major | — |
| 1 | **G major** | F♯ |
| 2 | **D major** | F♯ C♯ |
| 3 | **A major** | F♯ C♯ G♯ |

That order — F C G D A E B — is itself a chain of ascending fifths, and each new key is a fifth
above the last. You'll build the whole logic in Stage 4; for now, learn the top three, because
**G, D and A are the keys that fall under the first-position hand** (the same open-string
reason the violin is tuned G–D–A–E).

## The usual mistake

Playing F♮ in a sharp key because there's no ♯ printed next to that particular note. The
signature already said it — once, at the front, for good.
