The perfect cadence is the full stop. Three others fill in the rest of the punctuation.

## Plagal — the "Amen"

**IV → I.** Softer than a perfect cadence — no leading tone straining up, just the subdominant
settling home. It's the "A-men" at the end of a hymn.

## Half — the comma

Ends **on V**. The phrase leans forward, unfinished, expecting an answer. Very common at the
end of a first phrase (the antecedent of a period).

## Deceptive — the swerve

**V → vi.** The V sets you up for the tonic, and at the last moment it goes to **vi** instead —
the relative minor's chord. A little surprise; the phrase carries on to find a real ending
later.

## The era changes it — the Picardy third

Here's a "the symbol tells you what, the era tells you how" moment. A minor-key piece can end
its final cadence on a **major** tonic chord — you raise the 3rd of the last i chord. It's
called a **Picardy third**. Bach does it in roughly **half** his minor-mode pieces; by the
Classical era it had largely gone out of fashion. Same cadence on the page (V → i), a
different final chord depending on *when* it was written.

## The usual mistake

Hearing a half cadence or a deceptive cadence and thinking the piece has ended (or gone
wrong). They're **open** punctuation on purpose — the music is telling you *keep listening,
there's more*. Only the perfect and plagal cadences are genuine full stops.
