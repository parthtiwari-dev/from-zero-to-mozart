5th position is numerically further from home than 2nd or 4th — and it's still the second
position most players learn, right after 3rd. The reason is simple: **it's the easiest one to
find.**

## A landmark you can touch

Every position between 1st and 5th has no physical reference point — you find it purely by ear
and muscle memory. 5th position is different: slide the thumb forward along the neck until the
**hand touches the body of the violin**, roughly where the neck meets the upper bout. That
touch is real, repeatable, and doesn't depend on your ear being warmed up yet.

## Two landmarks now — triangulate between them

With 3rd position's hand-frame copy and 5th position's thumb touch, you have **two** reliable
reference points on the neck, on either side of the awkward, landmark-free positions still to
come (2nd, 4th). That's exactly why this course — and most methods — teach 5th right after 3rd,
skipping 2nd and 4th for now: two anchors are easier to build confidence around than one.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Slide your thumb forward until your hand touches the body of the violin. Without looking,
  place your 1st finger on the A string — check it against F♯ from the example above (or by ear
  against a tuner/drone). Then find 5th position on the other three strings the same way, each
  time leading with the thumb-touch landmark before the finger.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Reaching for the note by ear first and the landmark second — backwards. The whole advantage of
5th position is that the *hand* finds its place before the *ear* has to do any work. Lead with
the touch, then let the ear confirm.
