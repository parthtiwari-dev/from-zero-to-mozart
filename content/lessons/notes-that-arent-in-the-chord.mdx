When you work out the harmony under a tune, you'll hit melody notes that **aren't in the
chord** — and that's not a mistake in your analysis. Melodies are full of notes that pass
through, lean in, or hang over the harmony on their way to a chord note. They're called
**non-chord tones**, and they're what makes a melody a melody rather than a broken chord.

## The four common ones

They're told apart by **how you get to them and how you leave**:

| Name | Approached | Left | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Passing tone** | by step | by step, *same direction* | fills the gap between two chord notes |
| **Neighbour tone** | by step | by step, *back where it came from* | a quick step away and back |
| **Suspension** | held over from the previous chord | resolves *down* by step | a note that's late letting go |
| **Appoggiatura** | by a leap | by step, *opposite direction* | a leaning accented dissonance |

## Why it matters for you

When you're reading the harmony of a line (that's a lesson later this stage), you'll be
tempted to think you've got the chord wrong because a melody note doesn't fit. Check whether
that note is **passing between** two notes that *do* fit, or **leaning** on its way to one. If
so, the chord is right and the note is just decoration.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play the C-major arpeggio: C, E, G, then back down. Now play the C major *scale*: C, D, E, F,
  G, F, E, C. The scale is the arpeggio with passing tones filling the gaps. Every stepwise
  tune you know is built this way — chord notes with connective steps between them.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Hearing a non-chord tone and "correcting" it — flattening a passing F to fit a C chord, say.
Don't. The clash is brief and intentional; it resolves on the next note. If you smooth out
every dissonance, the melody goes limp. The tension of a passing or leaning note *against* the
harmony is a large part of what makes a tune expressive.
