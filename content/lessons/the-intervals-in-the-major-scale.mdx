The **quality** of an interval says how wide it is, in half steps. There are two families:

- **2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths** come as **minor** or **major** — minor being one half step
  narrower than major.
- **unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves** come as **perfect** — never "major" or "minor".

The quickest way to get your bearings is to measure everything from a **major scale's tonic**.

That's a genuinely useful check.

## Why some are "perfect"

The unison, octave, 5th and 4th are the intervals that sit at the **bottom of the overtone
series** — the simplest, most blended sounds a vibrating string makes (you'll meet this
properly in *The harmonic series*). They're also **the same in major and minor keys**: C up to
G is a perfect 5th whether you're in C major or C minor. Thirds and sixths *change* with the
mode, so they get labelled "major" or "minor"; these four don't change, so they're just
"perfect."

## Working out any interval

You won't always be measuring from a tonic. To name **any** interval — say D up to F:

1. **Number:** D–E–F, three letters → a **3rd**.
2. **Quality:** is F in D major? D major's 3rd is F**♯**. Plain F is a half step lower — so
   it's been narrowed. A narrowed major 3rd is a **minor 3rd**.

## Turning an interval upside down

Move the bottom note up an octave (or the top note down) and you **invert** the interval. Two
rules:

- **Rule of 9:** the number and its inversion add up to 9. A 3rd inverts to a 6th (3 + 6 = 9);
  a 2nd to a 7th; a 4th to a 5th.
- **Quality flips:** major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, **perfect stays perfect**.

So a major 3rd (C up to E) turned over becomes a minor 6th (E up to C) — the *same two notes*,
seen from the other end. That's why a melody and a harmony line can be described as "a 3rd
apart" or "a 6th apart" and mean the same relationship.

## The usual mistake

Assuming a "3rd" is always major or a "6th" always sounds sweet. The number is only half the
name. C–E and C–E♭ are both 3rds, but one is bright and one is dark — and which one a piece
uses is often the whole point.
