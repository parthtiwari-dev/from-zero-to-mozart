A small dot **directly after** a notehead adds **half of that note's own value** on top. One
rule, and it works on every value.

- a dotted **half note** = 2 + 1 = **3 beats**
- a dotted **quarter note** = 1 + ½ = **1½ beats**
- a dotted **whole note** = 4 + 2 = **6 beats** (only in a bar long enough to hold it)
- a dotted **eighth note** = ½ + ¼ = **¾ of a beat**

The dot is exactly half again — never a vague "a little longer."

## The dotted quarter + eighth — the one you'll meet most

This is the "long–short" limp of a march or a carol. The dotted quarter holds through beat 1
and *half* of beat 2; the eighth lands on the "**and**".

## In a real tune

*Ode to Joy*'s second phrase ends on a dotted figure: four steady notes, then a stretched
note, a clipped one, and a long one to close.

## The usual mistake

Two versions. Playing the dotted quarter as a full **two** beats — forgetting the dot is only
*half* again. Or evening it out to "long–long" and losing the limp entirely. Both are fixed
by saying the count aloud with the "&" in it: "**1 – & 2**", and the note ends exactly on the
"&".

<Margin>
  A **double dot** (two dots after the head) adds half, then half again — a double-dotted
  quarter is 1 + ½ + ¼ = 1¾ beats. Rare, mostly older music. Recognise it; you won't drill
  it here.
</Margin>

<TryThis>
  Clap four even quarters, then clap the dotted version (dotted-quarter, eighth, dotted-quarter,
  eighth) at the same tempo. Switch between them a few times without the pulse changing.
</TryThis>
