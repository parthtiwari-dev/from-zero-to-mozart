Stage 1 was about *which note* — the pitch, the string, the finger. Stage 2 is about *when*:
where a note sits against a steady beat, and how long it lasts. That's **rhythm**, and the
first thing to know is that it starts away from the violin.

Rhythm is one simple thing underneath everything else: **a steady pulse, divided.** A pulse
you could tap with one finger, split into longer and shorter pieces. Feel the pulse, say the
pattern, clap it — *then* pick up the bow.

## You already have most of this

You come from tabla. That tradition is built on exactly this — holding a pulse and dividing
it cleanly, learned by voice and hand long before the drum. Most of this stage is putting
**Western notation** onto rhythms your hands can already feel.

One thing to settle now: we count the standard Western way — "**one, two, three, four**",
"**one and two and**". Not because it's better than what you know, but because it's what every
teacher, every method book and every play-along recording uses. Keeping your counting the same
as theirs means the page and your voice always line up. (If you have your own syllables,
there's a short optional lesson later on how they map — but the course speaks in "one and
two and".)

## The usual mistake

Letting the pulse follow your hands instead of the other way round. Beginners slow down at
the hard bar and speed up through the easy one without hearing it happen. **The beat is
fixed; the hands obey it.** When you practise, the metronome or your counting voice is in
charge — not your fingers.

<TryThis>
  Tap a slow, even four on your knee — "one, two, three, four" out loud — and keep it going
  for a full minute without drifting. That steady tap is the thing every rhythm in this stage
  is measured against.
</TryThis>

  teaches: rhythm first, and steady.
