Stage 3 ended by walking every *symbol* on a dense line. This is the other half: walking the
**musicianship** of a line — the key, the degrees, the chords, the cadences, the phrase shape.
It's the sentence you'll be able to write about every piece from here on: *"this is in G major;
the first phrase climbs over the tonic and stops on a half cadence; the second answers it and
closes home."*

We'll do it on the opening of **Ode to Joy**, in G major.

Now read it:

- **Key:** one sharp, and the tune rests on G — **G major** (relative minor E minor, but it's
  clearly major here).
- **Degrees:** the melody lives in ^1 to ^5 — no leaps, all steps, a gentle arch.
- **Chords:** bars 1–3 sit on notes from the tonic chord (^3, ^4, ^5, ^1, ^2 — mostly I, with
  the ^4 and ^2 passing through). Bar 4 lands on **^2 (A)** — part of the **V** chord.
- **Cadence:** ending on V = a **half cadence**. A comma. The phrase is a *question*.
- **Phrase:** this is the **antecedent** of a period — it stops open, expecting an answer.

- Same climb, same degrees — until the last bar.
- Bar 8 ends **^2 → ^1 (A → G)**, landing on the **tonic**, over a **V → I** motion.
- **Cadence:** ending on I from V = a **perfect cadence**. A full stop. The *answer*.
- **Phrase:** the **consequent** — it resolves what the antecedent left open.

**Put together:** an eight-bar **parallel period** in G major — two phrases that start alike
and end differently, a question and an answer, defined entirely by their two cadences.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  Play both phrases in sequence. As you reach the end of the first, *think* "half cadence,
  question" and leave the A hanging. At the end of the second, *think* "perfect cadence,
  answer" and let the G settle. You're not just playing the notes — you're reading the harmony
  and shaping the phrase to match it.
</TryThis>

## Where this goes

From here on, every tune in this course gets this treatment — a one-line harmony note per
phrase, alongside the fingering and the bowing. "Understand what you're playing" stops being a
slogan and becomes something you can actually do on sight.

<Cite source="reading harmony from a melodic line — the annotated-edition tradition; PRD.md §6.2, 'understand what you're playing runs through every repertoire lesson'">
  A good annotated edition tells you not just which notes but what they're *doing* — the key,
  the harmony, the phrase. That's the reading skill this stage has been building toward.
</Cite>
