A reference card, and the end of the chord road. Everything so far has stacked **thirds**. The
20th century broke that habit — altering the notes, stacking different intervals, or piling
chords on top of each other. You'll see these on jazz charts and in modern scores.
**Recognise the idea; don't drill it.**

## Altered dominants

A dominant 7th chord with one or more of its colour notes **bent** — the 5th or the 9th raised
or lowered:

| Symbol | The alteration | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **7♭5** / **7♯5** | 5th flattened / sharpened | unstable, "outside" |
| **7♭9** | 9th flattened | dark, Spanish-tinged |
| **7♯9** | 9th sharpened (the "Hendrix chord", E7♯9) | crunchy, bluesy-angry |
| **7alt** | everything altered at once | maximum tension before resolution |

They still do the dominant's job — pull to the tonic — just with more grit on the way.

## Quartal harmony — stacking 4ths

Instead of thirds, stack **perfect 4ths**: C–F–B♭, or D–G–C. The result has no clear
major/minor identity — it's open, modern, a little cold. McCoy Tyner built a piano style on
it; film composers use it for "space" and "technology."

## Polychords and clusters

- **Polychord** — two triads stacked and played at once (a C major over an F♯ major, say).
  Stravinsky's *Petrushka* chord is the famous one. The effect is two keys colliding.
- **Cluster** — a fistful of adjacent notes (C–D–E–F all together). Used for noise, impact, or
  texture rather than harmony.

## Where you land

If a chart or a score throws **7♯9**, a stack of 4ths, or two triads at once — recognise it as
**20th-century / jazz harmony**: the old "stack thirds, resolve dissonance" rules have been
loosened or dropped. As a single-line player you read it for the *bass note and the mood*, and
carry on.
