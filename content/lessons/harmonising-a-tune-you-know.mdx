Three chords — **I, IV and V** — can accompany a startling amount of music. Here's why, and how
to work out which one goes where.

## Why three chords is often enough

- **I** contains ^1 ^3 ^5 (do–mi–sol)
- **IV** contains ^4 ^6 ^1 (fa–la–do)
- **V** contains ^5 ^7 ^2 (sol–ti–re)

Between them, those three chords hold **all seven degrees** of the scale. So whatever note the
melody is on, at least one of I, IV, V has it — and usually the ear tells you which.

**The rough rule:**

- a bar leaning on **do / mi / sol** → **I**
- a bar leaning on **sol / ti / re** (or **fa**) → **V** (or V7)
- a bar leaning on **fa / la** → **IV**

## The usual mistake

Forcing a chord change every bar, or never changing at all. Let the melody tell you: when it
moves to notes that clash with the current chord and agree with a new one, that's your cue.
And a tune can happily sit on one chord for several bars — Twinkle's whole first line is just
I, then IV, then I.

<Cite source="harmonising a melody with primary chords — the standard first exercise in every harmony course; Nancy Rogers, Music Theory I handout, and others">
  "Measures that use mainly do, mi and sol should be harmonised with the tonic; measures using
  mainly sol, ti, re and fa with the dominant." It really is that direct to start.
</Cite>
