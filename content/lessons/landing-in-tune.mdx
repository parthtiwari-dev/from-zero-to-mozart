Knowing where a finger *goes* is not the same as playing *in tune*. There's no fret to stop
you a millimetre short. So reading and intonation are separate skills — and intonation is the
real work of this course for you, because it's built on the ear, not the hand.

## The method: hear it, place it, check it

Three steps, in this order, for every note that matters.

1. **Hear it first.** Before the finger moves, have the pitch in your head — sing it, sound it
   on the drone, or play the open string it's related to. You can't land a note you haven't
   imagined.
2. **Place it.** Drop the finger where you think the note lives. Don't slide around hunting —
   aim, land, commit.
3. **Check it.** Compare what you hear to what you meant, and to something true: an open string
   a fourth or fifth away, or the drone. If it *beats* — a wobbling, pulsing sound — it isn't
   in tune yet. Move the finger a millimetre and listen again.

## Checks you always have

- **The open string it matches.** Your 4th finger plays the same pitch as the next open string
  up — 4th-finger A on the D string *is* the open A.

- **A drone.** Set it to the key's home note and play your phrase against it. In-tune notes
  lock in and seem to swell; out-of-tune notes buzz.
- **Resonance.** When a stopped note is exactly in tune with an open string an octave or a
  fifth away, that open string **rings on its own** — a faint sympathetic hum.

- **Your own ear, after the fact.** Did that sound higher or lower than you meant? Name the
  error — "a touch sharp" — before you fix it, so the correction isn't a guess. Much more on
  this in "Hearing a wrong note".

## Two rules while you learn where notes live

- **No vibrato.** It smears the pitch and hides small errors from your own ear. Play clean and
  still until the location is solid.
- **Don't fish.** Sliding around hunting for a note trains the hand to be vague. Aim, land,
  judge, adjust once.

<TryThis>

  most valuable thing you can do for your playing right now.
