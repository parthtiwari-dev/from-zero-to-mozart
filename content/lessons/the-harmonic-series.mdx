Play one note on the violin and you're actually hearing **many** notes at once. This is the
physics under intonation, under "why the major scale sounds natural," and under why your open
strings ring.

## One string, many pitches

A vibrating string moves as a whole length — *and*, at the same time, in halves, thirds,
quarters, fifths, and so on. Each of those extra vibrations makes a fainter, higher pitch
stacked on the main one. The stack is the **harmonic series**, and the pitches arrive at
**exact whole-number multiples** of the lowest one (called the *fundamental*).

## Two things fall out of this

**Why some intervals are "perfect."** The octave, fifth and fourth sit at the very bottom of
the series, with the simplest ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3). That's why they sound so pure, why they
tune so cleanly by ear, and why the violin is strung in fifths.

**Why the major triad sounds "natural."** Look at harmonics **4, 5 and 6** — G, B, D. That's a
**G major chord**, sitting inside a single vibrating string. The major scale and the major
triad aren't arbitrary; they're the low overtones of any note, written out.

## Natural harmonics

Touch the string *lightly* (don't press) at exactly the halfway point, and you silence the
fundamental and let only the ×2 harmonic sound — a clear, glassy note an octave up. Touch at
1/3 and you get the ×3 (an octave and a fifth). These are **natural harmonics** — Stage 3
taught how they're written (a small circle over the note); Stage 6 will drill playing them.
Here, they're proof the series is real: you can pick out one member of the stack and hear it
alone.

## Try it

<TryThis>
  On the open D string, rest your 3rd finger *lightly* where you'd normally stop the octave D
  (about halfway up the string) — barely touching, no weight. Draw the bow. A pure, floaty D
  an octave above the open string sings out. That's the ×2 harmonic, isolated. Slide the light
  touch toward the bridge to about a third of the way and you'll find the ×3, a fifth higher
  still.
</TryThis>

## The usual mistake

Imagining a played note as a single clean frequency. It never is — it's a fundamental plus a
whole ladder of overtones, and the particular mix is what makes a violin sound like a violin
and not a flute or a beep. When you hear a note "bloom" or "ring," you're hearing its
overtones line up and reinforce.

  Pythagoras found the simple whole-number ratios by dividing a single string. Helmholtz
  showed that those ratios are literally present in every note as overtones, and that our
  sense of consonance follows from how much two notes' overtones overlap.
</Cite>
