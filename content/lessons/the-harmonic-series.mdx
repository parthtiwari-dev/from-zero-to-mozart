Here's the physics underneath everything you've read so far, and the reason the "landing in
tune" resonance trick from Stage 1 actually works.

When a string vibrates, it doesn't just vibrate as one whole length — it *simultaneously*
vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters, and so on, each producing a fainter, higher pitch layered
on top of the main one. That stack of extra pitches is the **harmonic series**, and it's why a
violin (or any instrument) has a rich, complex tone instead of a bare electronic beep — you're
actually hearing many pitches at once, blended.

Two consequences that matter to you directly:

- **Open strings ring with unusual richness** because nothing is damping their vibration —
  that's part of why they're such reliable reference pitches.
- **Sympathetic resonance** — when you stop a note that matches an open string's pitch (or an
  octave/fifth of it) closely enough, that open string picks up the vibration through the
  bridge and rings faintly on its own, even though you never touched it. That faint ring is a
  built-in tuning check.

The harmonic series is also *why* octaves and fifths sound so naturally "consonant" together —
they share so many overtones that your ear hears them as closely related, almost blended,
rather than as two separate pitches. It's the same physics whether you call the underlying
tuning system "just intonation" or the "equal temperament" your piano and tuner actually use
(a practical compromise that makes every key sound equally good, at the cost of no interval
being mathematically perfect).
