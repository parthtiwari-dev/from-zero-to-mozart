## Why Stage 6 is load-bearing

Every stage so far has stayed inside **first position** by design (`RULES.md`, `THE-PATH.md`).
That was correct sequencing — you don't teach shifting before the reading, rhythm, theory and
bow foundations exist to hang it on. But it means the entire published course today plays
roughly one octave and a fourth of usable range per string, in one hand shape, with no way to
sustain more than an open-string-anchored double stop. Stage 6 is where the fingerboard
actually opens up. Two concrete debts this stage pays, both flagged back in
`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` from the deferred K.216 attempt:

- **§2 "Chords and double stops need real theory *and* real technique."** Stage 4 built the
  theory (triads, inversions, 7ths — `what-a-triad-is` etc.) and Stage 3 built the *notation*
  (stacked noteheads, rolled-chord squiggle). Stage 5 built the **bow** side of a 3-/4-note
  chord (`rolling-a-chord-with-the-bow`). Nobody has yet built the **left-hand** side — how you
  actually shape the hand to hold a stopped 3rds/6ths double stop, or a stopped chord, in tune.
  That's this stage.
- **§3, the "numeral tangle."** Stage 3's `finger-string-or-position` (G3) already taught the
  *reading* distinction — a roman numeral over a note can mean a **string** (`sul I` etc.) or a
  **position** (`III` = 3rd position), context decides, and neither is a finger number. Stage 6
  is where that stops being trivia and becomes something your hand has to *do*.

### Sequencing note — a correction to the standing scope note

`CLAUDE.md`'s Stage 6 scope line (written before this research pass) says *"positions one at a

## Pass 1 — What a "position" actually is

A **position** is where the whole hand sits on the neck, named by which note the **1st
finger** plays on each string in that spot — it is not a finger number and not (necessarily) a
string number. "1st position" is the hand's home base: 1st finger a whole step above the open
string. Moving the whole hand up the neck without changing which finger is "1st" is a
**shift**; every position beyond 1st needs one.

- **Half position** sits *below* first position, not above: the 1st finger drops to a
  **half step** above the open string instead of a whole step (so on the A string, 1st finger
  = B♭ instead of B). It's used for fast passages that would otherwise force an awkward
  string change, and for keys with many flats/sharps that have no comfortable open-string
  anchor (F major, B♭ major, E♭ major are the ones that come up). It is *not* commonly needed
  for slow, singing lines — there's always time to shift properly instead.
- **3rd position**: the 1st finger lands where the **3rd finger** sat in 1st position. G
  string 1st finger = C; D string 1st finger = G; A string 1st finger = D; E string 1st finger
  = A. This is *why* 3rd position is the easy one to learn first (Pass 2) — the hand frame
  (the spacing between fingers) is identical to 1st position, just picked up and set down a
  major-third-ish distance higher (the exact interval varies slightly by string because of the
  half/whole step pattern, but the *shape* transfers directly).
- **5th position**: the landmark changes character. The **thumb** slides forward until it
  touches the **body of the violin** (roughly where the neck meets the upper bout) — a
  touchable, repeatable physical reference, unlike 2nd/3rd/4th which have no such landmark and
  rely purely on ear + muscle memory. 1st finger notes: G string → E, D string → B, A string →
  F♯, E string → C♯.
- **Higher positions (6th, 7th+)**: the hand keeps climbing past the shoulder onto the body
  itself; fingers cluster closer together (the frets-that-aren't-there get closer at the
  octave), and the pinky in particular loses its comfortable curve and has to flatten. **7th
  position's 1st finger lands exactly on the octave harmonic** — the same pitch as the open
  string, one octave up, on every string. This course treats 6th/7th as **REFERENCE**, not a
  performed skill — see the movement plan.

## Pass 2 — The verified position-teaching order (corrects the standing scope note)

Two independent pedagogy sources (Violinspiration; Heather Kaye's Broadbent School of Violin
Artistry) state the same traditional order in almost identical words: **first → third → fifth
→ second → fourth.** The reasoning given, consistently:

- **3rd position gains you real repertoire** — its hand-frame is a direct copy of 1st
  position's, so it's the cheapest position to add. **2nd position gains you comparatively
  little** — you can dodge most of what 2nd position would solve with a different fingering in
  1st or 3rd, so it's the position most methods defer.
- **5th position is the second "essential" one** — a lot of intermediate repertoire (and the
  K.216 solo entrance, notably, sits partly in a range that wants 3rd/5th) is unreachable
  without it, and its thumb-on-the-shoulder landmark makes it easier to find reliably than 2nd
  or 4th, despite being numerically further away.
- **4th position is usually last** of the low/mid group — it has no distinctive landmark of its
  own (it's "between" 3rd and 5th) and, like 2nd, is often substitutable.

**Suzuki's own sequence corroborates this shape** rather than a strict numeric climb: 3rd
position arrives in Book 3, "serious" shifting (where you *must* shift to reach the notes)
starts with the Vivaldi A-minor concerto in Book 4, 5th position is the explicit focus of
Book 5, and 7th position doesn't appear until Book 7 — 2nd and 4th are folded in along the way
rather than given their own dedicated book.

**Verdict for this course:** teach **1st (recap) → half → 3rd → 5th → 2nd → 4th**, with
6th/7th+ as a REFERENCE capstone. This is the order the movement plan below uses, replacing
the earlier placeholder order in `CLAUDE.md`.

<Cite>Violinspiration "Ultimate Guide to the Violin Positions"; Heather Kaye / Broadbent School
of Violin Artistry "What is the Best Learning Order for the Positions on the Violin"; Suzuki
Method book-by-book position introduction (violinist.com discussion threads + Kennedy Violins
"How to Learn the Suzuki Method"), cross-checked for consistency.</Cite>

## Pass 3 — Shifting mechanics: what actually moves

A shift is not "slide the finger" — it's a coordinated move of the **whole arm**, with
different parts leading depending on how far up the neck you're going:

- **Lower positions (up to about 3rd):** the **lower arm** (forearm) leads the hand and
  fingers; the **upper arm** swings back toward the body as the elbow drops slightly under the
  violin.
- **Above roughly 3rd position:** the **hand** starts to lead the fingers more, and the
  **upper arm** does more of the side-to-side work, swinging the elbow further under the
  instrument as the hand travels toward the bout.
- **The thumb is not a passenger.** Shifting *up*, the thumb travels along the neck and
  settles into whatever curve of the neck/shoulder is the new landmark (5th position's
  shoulder-touch, see Pass 1). Shifting *down*, the **thumb has to lead** — it must arrive at
  its new spot **before** the playing finger does, not simultaneously, or the hand drags itself
  down flat-footed and the finger lands late and insecure.
- **Grip kills shifts.** A shift needs the string contact to go light during the glide —
  squeezing the string (or the neck, with the thumb) turns a shift into a scrape and makes the
  landing insecure. This is the same "no squeeze" principle Stage 0's `left-hand-frame` already
  taught for the static hand; Stage 6 is where it gets tested in motion.

<Cite>The Strad, "How to shift correctly on the violin" (citing Joseph Fuchs on Heifetz's
thumb-to-1st-finger resiliency); cross-checked against the general arm/thumb mechanics
consensus in Simon Fischer's shifting materials (Pass 5) and Galamian's *Principles of Violin
Playing and Teaching*.</Cite>

## Pass 4 — The four shift types (Galamian) + guide fingers

Ivan Galamian's classic classification, still the standard vocabulary teachers use:

| Type | Also called | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical / French shift** | — | you slide on the **old** (departing) finger to the new position, *then* place the new finger. Cleanest, most controllable — the default for beginners. |
| **Combination shift** | — | slide partway on the old finger, then swap to the new finger for the rest of the glide. A hybrid, used when neither pure type sounds right. |
| **Romantic / Russian shift** | — | slide on the **new** (arriving) finger from the start — you commit to the new finger immediately and glide *on it*. More audible by nature; an expressive/stylistic choice as much as a mechanical one. |
| **Delayed shift** | — | the new finger goes down on the new note, and only *then* does the rest of the hand/arm "catch up" into the full new position. Popular in the Galamian school for fast, clean shifts where you don't want the glide heard at all. |

**The guide note / guide finger:** whichever finger is doing the sliding (old or new,
depending on shift type) passes through an intermediate pitch on the way — that passing pitch
is the **guide note**, and it's what a player's ear checks against to know the shift is
tracking correctly, even though it's not meant to be *heard* as a separate note in a clean
shift (see Pass 6 — that's the line between a shift and a portamento). **Same-finger shifts**
(1st finger to 1st finger, a whole step up, say) are the simplest case — there's only one
finger in the story. **Different-finger shifts** (e.g. 1st finger note up to a 3rd-finger
note) are harder because the hand has to travel *and* change which finger is doing the
stopping, and the guide note matters more for keeping the shift honest.

<Cite>violinist.com discussion (Galamian's four-type classification, widely reproduced in
pedagogy literature); The Strad "How to shift correctly on the violin" (guide-note mechanism);
cross-checked terminology against Simon Fischer's *Basics* shifting chapter (Pass 5).</Cite>

## Pass 5 — Shift timing: steal it from the note before

This is the single most concrete, teachable fact this pass turned up, and it's worth quoting
precisely because it's counter-intuitive and easy to get backwards.

**Simon Fischer, citing Galamian directly** (*Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching*, New
Jersey 1962, p.26): *"One of the commonest faults found in shifting is that of shortening the
note preceding the move… Conscious attention to the rhythmic value and sound of the note
preceding the shift is imperative until correct habits are formed."*

Fischer's own gloss resolves what looks like a contradiction: **the time for the shift is
taken from the note *before* the shift, not from the arrival note.** The common beginner error
is to start the physical shift exactly when the new note is due, which forces a late, rushed,
inaccurate landing — and *that* is what makes the previous note sound clipped, even though the
clipping happens to the *next* note's timing, not the note itself. The fix, as a practice
method:

1. **Cut the value of the note before the shift by half**, and use the freed-up time to shift,
   deliberately early and slow.
2. **Gradually lengthen the note before the shift** (i.e. delay the start of the shift later
   and later) while **increasing the speed of the shift**, until the glide takes only "a
   fraction of a second" and the arrival note lands exactly on the beat.

This is a direct, reusable practice recipe — the shifting equivalent of Stage 2's rhythm-first
method and Stage 8's `the-never-stop-rule` family, and it should anchor whichever lesson
teaches "how do I practise a shift."

<Cite>Simon Fischer, "BASICS: Changing position" (simonfischeronline.com, PDF), directly
quoting Ivan Galamian, *Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching* (New Jersey, 1962), p.26.</Cite>

## Pass 6 — Shift vs glissando vs portamento (and Classical vs Romantic style)

Three related but distinct things, worth keeping straight because Stage 3 already taught the
*glissando line* as notation and this stage has to connect it to what the hand actually does:

- **A clean (silent) shift** disguises the slide: the departing finger lifts "just enough that
  it does not make a sound" as the hand travels, landing the new finger cleanly on the new
  note. This is the default, invisible-technique goal for almost all shifting.
- **Portamento** ("to carry") is a **short, subtle, expressive** slide, usually starting late —
  just before the destination note — rather than evenly paced through the whole distance. Two
  common executions: slide on the old finger and place the new finger only on arrival, or swap
  fingers partway through the slide.
- **Glissando** is the more literal, evenly-paced slide the whole distance, usually notated
  with a straight line between two noteheads (Stage 3's `slides-and-octave-signs`) and meant to
  be *heard* as a slide from note to note, not disguised.
- **Historical style split:** portamento/glissando as expressive devices are a **Romantic
  (19th-century) development** — they were not part of Classical-period violin technique.
  Playing Mozart or Haydn with lush Romantic-style slides is a style violation the same way
  continuous vibrato on every note would be (Stage 7's territory); playing Brahms or Elgar
  with *zero* portamento misses part of the Romantic idiom. "Which era, which slide" belongs
  next to Stage 3's existing "the symbol tells you WHAT, the era tells you HOW" framing.

<Cite>Violin Lounge "Shifting vs Glissando vs Portamento on the Violin"; The Strad, "Portamento,
when used tastefully, can bring a piece to life" (Aaron Rosand); cross-checked against CMUSE
"Portamento vs Glissando: What's The Difference?" and the historical-style consensus in
violin-pedagogy literature on Romantic performance practice.</Cite>

## Pass 7 — 3rd position in depth

The note map (1st finger, all four strings): **G string → C · D string → G · A string → D ·
E string → A.** Because the hand-frame is a direct copy of 1st position (low-2/high-2 etc. all
transfer), the fastest way to *teach* 3rd position is by analogy: "wherever your 3rd finger
used to land in 1st position, that's now your 1st finger." A useful **self-check landmark**:
on several strings, a note in 3rd position **matches an open string a fourth away** — e.g. 1st
finger on the A string in 3rd position (D) is the same pitch as the open D string, so you can
check a 3rd-position shift by ear against an open string exactly the way Stage 1 taught
same-pitch open-string checks in 1st position. This is a strong tie-in for Stage 8's ear gym
(hearing degree, then confirming against a ringing open string).

3rd position is also the **first place a first-position player meets a genuinely useful new
range** without leaving the comfortable low-2/high-2 hand vocabulary, which is why student
concertos (Vivaldi A minor, Seitz, Rieding — all already in `REPERTOIRE.md` Tier 2/3) lean on
it immediately after 1st position repertoire.

<Cite>Violinspiration "How to Play in 3rd Position on the Violin"; Violin Lounge "Violin Third
Position — all notes, finger chart and exercises"; cross-checked against the D-string
1-2-3-4 = G-A-B-C 3rd-position pattern independently confirmed in a second search result.</Cite>

## Pass 8 — 5th position in depth

The note map (1st finger): **G string → E · D string → B · A string → F♯ · E string → C♯.**
The defining physical landmark — and the reason 5th is taught *before* 2nd/4th despite the
bigger numeric jump — is that the **thumb slides under the neck until the hand touches the
body of the violin** (roughly where the neck joins the upper bout). That's a real, repeatable,
*feel-able* reference point, unlike the positions between 1st and 5th, which have no
comparable landmark and rely on the ear plus muscle memory built from repetition. Teaching 5th
right after 3rd gives the student **two** reliable landmarks (the 3rd-position hand-frame
copy, and the 5th-position thumb/body touch) to triangulate from before tackling the
"in-between, no-landmark" positions (2nd, 4th).

<Cite>Violinspiration "The Ultimate Guide to the Violin Positions" and "Fifth Position on the
Violin: All You Need to Know" (thumb-under-neck / body-touch landmark, note map); cross-checked
against tunableapp.com's full 1st–7th position chart and, independently, against the rule that

(they are, on all four strings, at every position in that chart — a self-consistency check run
across the whole table during re-verification, Pass 20).</Cite>

## Pass 9 — 2nd and 4th position

**2nd position** note map (1st finger): **G string → B♭, D string → F, A string → C, E
string → G.** It sits between 1st and 3rd with no landmark of its own — you find it by ear, or
by "un-shifting" partway back down from 3rd. Its main use: smoothing a line that would
otherwise need an awkward string crossing, or reaching a note just outside 1st position's
comfortable top without the bigger jump to 3rd.

**4th position** note map (1st finger): **G string → D, D string → A, A string → E, E string
→ B.** Also landmark-free; it sits between 3rd and 5th the same way 2nd sits between 1st and
3rd, and is used for the same reason — smoothing, or reaching one note further than 3rd
without a full jump to 5th.

Both are genuinely useful once the player has 1st/3rd/5th solid — this course places them
**after** 5th (Pass 2) but does **not** skip them; they round out full fingerboard coverage
and both come up constantly once double stops and chords (Pass 13) start requiring "whichever
position keeps this chord fingerable."

put your first finger where your fourth finger was, when placed in first position"), then

## Pass 10 — 6th, 7th and higher (REFERENCE, not performed)

Past 5th position the hand keeps climbing onto the body of the violin itself; notes cluster
closer together (an octave is a shorter physical distance the higher you go), and the **4th
finger in particular loses its comfortable curved shape** and has to flatten to reach. Suzuki
doesn't introduce 7th position until Book 7 — well past where this course's Intermediate gate
sits. Note maps (1st finger), for the REFERENCE card: **6th position — G string → F, D string
→ C, A string → G, E string → D. 7th position — G string → G, D string → D, A string → A, E
string → E** (i.e. exactly one octave above the open string, on every string — the cleanest,
most memorable landmark of any position, and the same node the 7th-partial natural harmonic
sits on). **Decision for this stage:** 6th/7th+ get a single REFERENCE lesson (what's up
there, why it's hard, a card showing the note map) rather than a full performed unit —
matching how Stage 5 treated the far-shore bow strokes (ricochet, fouetté) as
recognise-not-perform. Full treatment is repertoire-module territory (Stage 9+), when an
actual piece calls for it.

## Pass 11 — Reading position numbers vs finger numbers, fully resolved

Stage 3's `finger-string-or-position` already established the reading-level fact: a roman
numeral over a note can be a **string indicator** (I=E, II=A, III=D, IV=G — high string to low,
the reverse of what the number-shape suggests) **or** a **position indicator** (III = 3rd
position), and only context — the editor's key, the numeral's placement, whether Arabic finger
numbers 1–4 already appear nearby — tells you which. Two independent sources in this pass
confirm this is a **well-known, named source of confusion** in real editions, not a course
invention: multiple fingering-chart guides flag that "string numbers... look quite like
fingering numbers," and the K.216 print itself (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §3) uses `III`/`IV`
for **positions** while separately using Arabic `1 2 3 4` for fingers.

Stage 6's job is to make the **position** reading physical: when a roman numeral (or the more
modern "3rd pos." text) appears, the hand has an actual place to go, and the player can *verify*
they're there (the 3rd-position open-string check from Pass 7, or the 5th-position thumb touch
from Pass 8) rather than just noting the number abstractly.

<Cite>Recap + extension of Stage 3 `finger-string-or-position`; corroborated by notat.io
"Violin Family - String Numbering" and the general fingering-chart literature in Pass 1's
search on roman-numeral ambiguity.</Cite>

## Pass 12 — Double stops: the technique ladder

The double-stop *reading* ladder already exists (Stage 3 introduces the notation; Stage 8's
`reading-two-notes-at-once` reads open+open and open+stopped fluently). The **playing**
ladder, confirmed by two independent sources in near-identical language:

1. **Two open strings** — no left hand at all; purely a bow/right-hand-control exercise
   (string-pair contact, even weight on both strings).
2. **Open + one stopped note** on the adjacent string — the left hand only has one job.
3. **Both notes stopped, on adjacent strings** — the genuinely new skill: two fingers down at
   once, in tune with each other, not just each in tune with an open string.

**Specific finger patterns for the common intervals** (all in first position to start, then
transferable to 3rd/5th once those exist):

- **Thirds:** typically 1st finger on the lower string with 3rd finger on the string above (or
  2nd with 4th) — a "skip one finger" shape. Major vs minor 3rd changes *which* low/high
  finger variant you use on each string, not the finger numbers themselves.
- **Sixths:** typically 1st finger on the lower string with 4th finger on the string above (or
  the reverse spacing) — a wider stretch than a 3rd, needing more hand flexibility.
- **Octaves:** 1st and 4th finger, one string apart, in a fixed "frame" shape that moves as a
  unit — the hand carries the interval rather than each finger being placed independently.

**Intonation reference:** double stops are tuned "by feel and by wiggle" against each other,
not purely by finding each note in isolation — the source material is explicit that you
"wiggle your fingers around to tune the interval until it sounds right," which is exactly the
kind of ear-check Stage 8's Movement F (hearing thirds/harmony) sets up and this stage cashes
in physically.

<Cite>Violinspiration "How to Play Double Stops on Violin" (progression + finger patterns);
8notes.com "The Beginners Guide to Violin Double-stopping" and violinmasterclass.com "Scales,
Arpeggios, and Double Stops" (open→2-open→octaves→sixths→thirds→fourths→fifths→fingered-

## Pass 13 — Fingered octaves and beyond (REFERENCE)

"Fingered octaves" — where the interval is played with 1st and 3rd finger (or other non-4th
combinations) instead of the standard 1st/4th frame — and **tenths** are named in the source
material as the hardest standard double-stop category, requiring "excellent left-hand
flexibility." Ševčík Op. 9 ("Preparatory Exercises in Double-Stopping") is the standard étude
reference for finger independence within a double-stop hand frame — already present in this
course's étude ladder (`REPERTOIRE.md`). **Decision:** REFERENCE only at this stage — a card
naming what's out there and why it's hard, not a performed unit. Regular octaves (1st/4th) and
open+stopped/stopped+stopped 3rds and 6ths are the performed set.

double-stop étude, already listed in `REPERTOIRE.md`'s étude ladder.</Cite>

## Pass 14 — Stopped chords: the left-hand half of the Stage-5/K.216 debt

Stage 5's `rolling-a-chord-with-the-bow` solved the **bow** side of a 3-/4-note chord: roll
bottom-to-top, land and hold the top note, because the bridge's curve makes true
simultaneity impossible. The **left hand's** job, new to this stage, is holding the *shape*
that makes such a chord physically reachable at all:

- A playable stopped chord needs its notes spread across **3 or 4 different strings** (the
  same physical constraint `RULES.md` Mistake #3 already encodes for notated examples) — but
  now at least one, sometimes two, of those notes must be **fingered**, not open, which is
  what makes it new: the hand has to hold two or three fingers down simultaneously across
  adjacent strings while the bow rolls through.
- The practical strategy repertoire uses: identify which notes of the chord are **essential**
  (usually the outer two — the bass and the melody top) and which can be treated as a quick
  passing note in the roll if the hand truly can't hold all three/four at once; this is a
  standard triage skill, not a shortcut — full chords in fast passages are frequently
  simplified this way even by professionals.
- This is where **3rd position** chords start mattering: shifting the whole chord shape up
  keeps some voicings reachable that are cramped or impossible in 1st position.

notation lesson; the "spread across 3+ strings" constraint is the course's own established
rule (`RULES.md` Mistake #3), now applied to *playing* rather than just notating.</Cite>

## Pass 15 — Trills as finger action

Reading a trill (which note, Baroque-starts-on-the-upper-note vs Classical-starts-on-the-
principal-note, does it take an accidental) is already taught — Stage 3 Movement F. The
**physical execution**, confirmed consistently across multiple pedagogy sources:

- **Motion source:** lift and drop the trilling finger from its **base knuckle** (the
  knuckle where the finger meets the hand), *not* the middle joint — using the middle joint
  "gets stuck" and can't sustain speed. The motion is compared to a small hammer tapping the
  string.
- **Relaxation between taps** is the speed secret, not force: tension anywhere in the hand,
  wrist, or arm caps how fast a trill can go, more than raw finger strength does.
- **Building speed:** rhythmic-cell practice — quarters → eighths → sixteenths →
  thirty-seconds, each with a metronome, aiming for *even* spacing rather than a rushed blur.
  A commonly cited starting tempo is a slow, deliberate ~50 BPM, moving up gradually.
- **Practising without the bow first** (finger taps only — single taps like a grace note,
  then groups of 2–4, then a sustained trill) isolates the motion before the bow's timing gets
  added.
- **Finger-combination coverage:** trills aren't only 1–2; a full trill vocabulary drills every
  adjacent pair a piece might ask for — 0–1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–4 (half-step or whole-step neighbours)
  — across all four strings.

<Cite>Violinspiration "Trills on the Violin – An Easy Guide"; The Strad "3 simple trill
exercises for players and teachers" and "7 ways to improve your trills" (base-knuckle motion,
relaxation, rhythmic-cell speed building — corroborated across all three independent
sources).</Cite>

## Pass 16 — Natural harmonics

**How they're played:** touch the string **lightly** at a node — finger laid flatter than
normal stopping, never pressed down — while the bow, ideally already moving before contact,
stays close and parallel to the bridge for a clean tone. Pressing turns the harmonic into a
dead, muted thud; the whole technique is "less is more."

**The three practical natural harmonics per string** (all reachable without shifting far, using
the 1st-position frame):

| Touch point | Splits the string into | Sounds | Usual finger/position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Middle of the string** (the octave node) | halves | an octave above the open string | 4th finger, 4th position (or 3rd finger, high up) |
| **⅓ of the string** from bridge or nut | thirds | an octave **and a 5th** above the open string | 4th finger, first position (a light touch on the note that would normally be a stopped high note) |
| **¼ of the string** from bridge or nut | quarters | **two octaves** above the open string | 3rd finger, first position |

**Notation, two conventions both in use:** a small **circle (`0`)** above a normal notehead
marks a natural harmonic at that written pitch/finger; a **diamond-shaped notehead** marks the
*sounding* harmonic pitch directly. Editions mix both — sometimes a small numeral above the `0`
tells you which harmonic (which finger/node) produces that pitch.

**Artificial ("touch" or "fourth") harmonics** are a distinct, harder technique — one finger
holds down the real (stopped) fundamental note while the 4th finger lightly touches a fourth
above it, and because the effective string length keeps changing with every fundamental, the
node position has to be relearned for every new note. **Decision:** natural harmonics are a
performed skill this stage (they're a direct extension of the 1st-position hand plus one new
motion); artificial harmonics are **REFERENCE/LATER** — genuinely advanced and out of scope
until well past the Intermediate gate.

<Cite>Violinspiration "Violin Harmonics Simplified"; Meadowlark Violin Studio "Easy Violin
Harmonics Explained"; Violin Lounge "Easy Guide to Violin Harmonics" — the node
fractions/octave relationships and the flat-finger/light-touch technique are corroborated
identically across all three.</Cite>

## Pass 17 — Left-hand pizzicato, glissando execution, finger substitution

- **Left-hand pizzicato**: the left hand's own fingers (usually the pinky, sometimes another
  finger not needed for the next note) pluck the string instead of the bow. Standard notation
  is a small **`+`** above or below the note — distinct from the arco/pizz. *bow* switch Stage
  5 already teaches. Common in accessible/orchestral repertoire and also, at the virtuoso
  extreme, in Paganini-style writing (both ends of the difficulty spectrum use the same sign).
- **Glissando/portamento execution** (Pass 6 gave the definitions): the physical skill is
  controlling *how audible* the slide is on purpose — a silent shift, a subtle portamento, and
  a full glissando are the same physical motion at three different levels of intentional
  audibility, not three different techniques.
- **Finger substitution**: changing which finger holds a sustained note *while it's still
  sounding*, without a new attack or an audible bump — used to free up a finger for what comes
  next, or to set up a smoother shift. Genuinely harder under the bow (arco) than in pizzicato,
  because a sustained bowed note exposes any noise in the substitution far more than a plucked
  note does.

<Cite>sites.google.com (Ms. Macleod's Orchestra Page) and justviolin.org on the `+`
left-hand-pizzicato sign; Wikipedia "Finger substitution" (arco vs pizzicato difficulty,
corroborated by the general pedagogy framing in the same search results).</Cite>

## Pass 18 — Practice methodology for the whole stage

Threads that repeat across every source in this research, worth stating once as the stage's
practice-method spine (mirrors Stage 5's `how-to-practise-a-bow-stroke` and Stage 8's
`the-ear-gym-routine` — same shape, applied to the left hand):

- **Isolate the shift, not the whole phrase.** Practise just the two notes either side of a
  shift, slowly, before or a shift, exactly the way Fischer's method (Pass 5) prescribes:
  start with an exaggeratedly early, slow shift and tighten it up over repetitions.
  do a whole passage. This mirrors Stage 8's `the-four-layer-rescue` — isolate, fix, recombine.
- **Silent practice** (fingers only, no bow) works for shifts, trills and chord shapes alike —
  it isolates the left hand's job from the right hand's timing, the same principle Stage 5
  used for bow-only rehearsal of a bowing plan.
- **Check every new position against the ear, not just the eye.** The 3rd-position
  open-string check (Pass 7) and the drone-referenced hearing built in Stage 8's scale-degree
  gym are the same tool pointed at a new problem: "did I land where I meant to."
- **A mirror or the case's own scroll-to-body reference** helps early on for confirming hand
  *location* (not pitch) — useful, optional, not load-bearing the way the ear check is.

## Pass 21 — Finger extensions and contractions (the gap Pass 20 found)

An **extension** is stretching a single finger forward or backward, beyond the interval it
would normally cover in the current position's hand-frame, to reach one extra note **without
moving the hand or the thumb.** A **contraction** is the reverse — pulling a finger back
inward to reach a note that would otherwise need a downward shift. Both keep the thumb and
palm anchored where they are; only the one finger's reach changes.

- **This is not a new idea to the course — it's a bigger version of one already taught.**
  Stage 1's low-2/high-2/low-1/high-3 vocabulary (`low-and-high-fingers`) *is* a small
  extension: the finger moves a semitone off its "default" spot without the hand shifting.
  Stage 6 generalises that same move to a **whole step or more**, and to any finger, in any
  position — e.g. stretching the 4th finger forward to reach a note that would normally need a
  shift up to the next position, then relaxing it back.
- **Why it exists as a distinct skill from shifting:** a shift moves the *whole hand's*
  reference point; an extension reaches *one* note past that reference point and springs back.
  For a single passing note, an extension is often faster, quieter, and less disruptive to the
  established position than shifting there and back for one note and returning.
- **The physical execution:** the hand has to **release, not just stretch** — treated as tension
  the same way Stage 0's `left-hand-frame` already teaches "no squeeze." A common image from
  the pedagogy literature: think of the palm opening like a hand slowly opening around an
  orange, or a fan spreading, rather than a rigid reach. Practise by first *imagining* the
  target pitch, then feeling for it — the same audiate-then-place order Stage 8's
  `hearing-it-first` already teaches, now aimed at a finger stretch instead of a whole phrase.
- **Pivoting / "creeping fingering" / the "retarded" and "half" shift** (Galamian's terms,
  also described as "pivoting or crawling" by Ruggiero Ricci) are all names for **combining** a
  small shift with an extension or contraction — moving the hand only part of the way to a new
  position while one finger (often the 1st) anchors the old position, letting the hand "creep"
  up gradually rather than jumping the full interval in one shift. This is the more advanced
  cousin of a plain extension, useful for smoothing a fast passage that would otherwise need
  several separate small shifts in a row.
- **Scope for this stage:** teach the **extension/contraction concept and a basic forward
  4th-finger extension** as a performed skill (it's genuinely common, low-risk, and a direct
  generalisation of Stage 1 content); treat **pivoting/creeping-fingering as a named
  REFERENCE** concept (what it is, why virtuoso passages use it) rather than a drilled
  technique — it's a refinement on shifting fluency this course's Intermediate-gate scope
  doesn't need to demand.

<Cite>Strings Magazine, "Extend Yourself: How to Practice Extensions, a Notoriously Tricky
Technique" (search-result synthesis — direct fetch was blocked, so treat the practice-method
