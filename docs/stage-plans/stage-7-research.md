## Why Stage 7 is load-bearing

Every stage through 6 has taught the reader to put the right note in the right place at the
right time with the right bow. None of it has taught the reader to make that note *mean*
something. Vibrato, rubato, tone colour and character are the difference between "correct" and
"music" — and they are exactly what `PRD.md` §3's fifth capability ("**Choose** — make your
own interpretive decisions and defend them") is made of. K.216 is *musically* diploma-level
precisely here: its notes sit in upper-first-to-fifth position, but its phrasing and taste are
the summit. `LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4 named this stage as the one that must carry real
weight: *"Classical restraint, where to breathe, vibrato as an ornament not a constant, the
messa di voce on a long note, not over-Romanticising Mozart — that's Stage 7 (expression) +
the K.216 module's listening study, and it deserves real weight — it's not a coda."*

The Intermediate gate (`PRD.md` §4) lists **"vibrate on a sustained note"** as a criterion,
so Stage 7 feeds the gate directly. Stage 8b (positions at sight + the Intermediate check)
has Stage 7 as its last remaining dependency.

### Sequencing — why vibrato comes after Stage 6, not earlier

Every source agrees vibrato waits for: in-tune first position without tension, a relaxed left
hand, and — critically — **a hand that is free to move along the neck**, i.e. one that holds
the violin without gripping and has begun shifting. Stage 6 built exactly that hand. General
pedagogy puts vibrato at roughly 6–12 months of study; this course puts it after the
Foundation gate and Stage 6, which is more conservative and correct. No sequencing problem.

<Cite>Violinspiration "How to do Vibrato on the Violin"; Violin Lounge "Violin Vibrato:
Complete Guide"; both state the same readiness criteria and both put vibrato study after basic
first-position intonation and (Violin Lounge) comfort in third position.</Cite>

### The one sorting question this whole stage runs on

Every claim you meet about vibrato is a claim about **one of two different things**, and the
lesson's job is to keep them apart and name a source for each:

- **What the string physically does** — an acoustic / measurement claim.
- **What you tell a student to do** — a pedagogical *cue*, which exists because of how the
  error distributes (students who reach *upward* for vibrato go sharp), not because it
  describes the waveform.

Mixing the two is how "vibrato oscillates *below* the pitch" got asserted as physics in this
project's own docs (`CURRICULUM.md` / `CLAUDE.md` say "below"; the source PDF §68 says
"*around*"). A lesson that states **both, each labelled**, is more accurate than one that
picks a winner — and it is the honest teacher move. Apply this test to every vibrato sentence
written for the stage.

## Pass 1 — What vibrato is: the cue and the waveform

**The idea.** Vibrato is a small, regular variation in the pitch (and, as a by-product, the
tone and loudness) of a *sustained, stopped* note, produced by rocking the fingertip on the
string. It is an expressive device, not a correction — and there is **never a notehead or a
symbol for it** on an ordinary part (the course's own `beamed-notes-double-stops-and-vibrato`
already tells the reader this). When and how much to use it is the player's choice.

**The cue you are taught** (near-universal across pedagogy — Violinspiration, the
Suzuki-tradition method by Meghan Faw, Violin Lounge, and Seashore's own 1930s description):
the **in-tune pitch sits at the top of the oscillation**. You start on the true note and roll
the finger *back* — toward the scroll, flattening the pitch — then return to the note. You
**never reach above** an in-tune note. Some methods teach the same thing from the other side:
start with the finger placed slightly flat and roll *up* to the true pitch. Either way, the
true pitch is the **ceiling** of the wobble, never its centre or its floor.

**Why the cue works:** reaching above an in-tune note simply sounds sharp. If the top of the
wobble is the in-tune pitch, the ear accepts the whole note as in tune. Students who try to
*centre* the oscillation on the note tend to drift sharp.

**What the string actually does** (acoustic studies — Geringer, Allen & MacLeod, 2000s–2010s;
an earlier 1930s claim that violin vibrato went *upward only* from the pitch was later
contradicted): once vibrato is fluent, the fingertip oscillates **both above and below**
wherever it settles. Listeners perceive the pitch **near the mean** of the oscillation — and a
note *with* vibrato is generally judged **slightly lower** than a dead-steady note at the same
mean frequency. So a fluent player's mean pitch tends to sit a hair below the nominal pitch —
which is exactly the result the "roll back from the note" cue is designed to produce. **The
cue and the acoustics converge; they just describe different things.** Teach both, labelled.

<Cite>John M. Geringer, Michael L. Allen & Rebecca B. MacLeod, string-vibrato perception
studies (Journal of Research in Music Education, 2010; International Journal of Music
Education, 2014) — "alternations both above and below the conceived pitch", perceived "very
near the mean frequency", vibrato tones "judged as lower in pitch than non-vibrato stimuli
with the same mean frequency". Pedagogical cue: Violinspiration and Meghan Faw (Suzuki
tradition), both "start from the note and go backward / below".</Cite>

## Pass 2 — Rate and width (the numbers, and the check that keeps them honest)

**Rate** — how many oscillations per second (Hz):

- **Expressive performance vibrato: roughly 5–7 Hz**, most often cited around **6 Hz**. Carl
  Seashore's 1930s measurements (*Psychology of Music*, 1938) put musical vibrato at "five to
  seven undulations per second"; modern string-pedagogy sources repeat ~6 Hz as the typical
  centre, with a usable range from about 4 Hz (slow, wide, warm) to 7+ Hz (fast, bright,
  intense).
- **Practice speed is deliberately far below that.** Vibrato is built with a metronome at
  ~60 BPM doing 1, then 2, then 3, then 4 evenly-spaced rocks per beat — i.e. 1–4 Hz. The PDF's
  own 6-week plan ends at "2–4 oscillations per beat", which at 60 BPM is **2–4 Hz — still
  below performance rate.** A practice number is not a performance number, and a lesson must
  never let "week 6" read as "finished".

**The consistency check** (this stage's mechanical check, the analog of Stage 6 Pass 20's
perfect-fifth-between-strings proof):

> **pulses per beat × BPM ÷ 60 = oscillations per second (Hz)**

Run it on *every* drill that carries a metronome number. It catches one specific error class:
a lesson implying that a practice-tempo oscillation *is* performance vibrato. If a drill's
result lands below ~5 Hz, the lesson must say so explicitly ("this is a practice speed; a real
vibrato is faster and you'll get there over months").

**Width / extent** — how far the pitch swings:

- **Violin expressive vibrato: about 0.2–0.35 of a semitone in depth** — roughly a **quarter
  tone**, i.e. ~20–35 cents to each side of the mean (100 cents = one equal-tempered semitone).
  Seashore found violinists' extent averaged about **a quarter of a semitone** — roughly half
  as wide as singers'. A width near a full half-tone reads as dramatic / Romantic; a narrow
  width (~a quarter tone or less) reads as classical and controlled.
- **Width and speed are largely independent controls**, and both are varied for expression:
  wider + slower for a big lyrical Romantic line; narrower for Classical restraint; wider in
  *forte*, narrower in *piano* (width tracks the dynamic).

<Cite>Carl E. Seashore, *Psychology of Music* (1938) — the classic 5–7 Hz / ~0.25-semitone
measurements. Modern figures (~6 Hz; ~0.2–0.35 semitone depth; width tracks dynamic and era)
corroborated across Violin Lounge "Complete Guide", violinist.com pedagogy discussions, and
the CMUSE cents-to-hertz reference. Numbers stated as ranges, not precise values — a wrong Hz
figure is unfalsifiable to this user (`RULES.md` #8).</Cite>

## Pass 3 — The three types, and the "which first" disagreement

Ivan Galamian's *Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching* (1962) divides the vibrato motion
into three, and the division is standard:

- **Arm (forearm) vibrato** — the impulse comes from the forearm; the motion pivots roughly at
  the **elbow**; the hand and wrist travel as a unit with the forearm. The fingertip stays
  planted but stays supple. Larger motion, generally **easier to feel first**; can sound warm
  and wide. Risk: if the whole arm shakes, it can knock the instrument or disturb the bow.
- **Hand / wrist vibrato** — the forearm stays comparatively still; the hand **rocks at the
  wrist** ("drops backward from the wrist and returns forward to its starting position"); the
  base knuckles lead. Often described as even and capable of speed; the "classic" default in
  many studios. Requires a supple, non-collapsing wrist — hence the readiness gate.
- **Finger vibrato** — the motion comes from the finger's own **base knuckle bending in and
  out**; no arm motion at all. Smallest and subtlest; a **later refinement**. One respected
  view (Fritz Gearhart, and implied by others) is that it is "a result of the other two, not
  an independent motion." → **REFERENCE** for this course, matching the PDF's own
  "[ADVANCED REFERENCE]" tag on §71.

Henryk Szeryng's summary is the honest end state: *"a perfect vibrato is a combination of
finger, wrist and forearm."* You end up using all three, blended, matched to the note.

**Which to learn first is a genuine, unresolved teacher disagreement** — do not resolve it by
fiat:

- Many teachers start with **arm** because the bigger motion is easier to feel and control
  (Violin Lounge: "many teachers — myself included — start students on arm vibrato").
- Many start with **wrist**, treating it as the core motion (Gearhart teaches hand vibrato as
  the thing; several method books lead with it).
- Galamian catalogues all three without prescribing an order; Simon Fischer teaches both arm
  and wrist.

**Course synthesis (for the flagged decision below):** teach **arm and wrist — whichever the
student finds first — then the other; finger = REFERENCE.** State the disagreement in the
lesson rather than hiding it. This matches the standing CLAUDE.md / CURRICULUM.md sketch
("this course teaches arm *and* wrist, no dogma") and is defensible; the alternative (pick one
and drill it) is *not* supported by the literature.

<Cite>Ivan Galamian, *Principles of Violin Playing and Teaching* (1962) — the arm / hand /
finger division. The Strad, "Developing arm, wrist and finger vibrato" (the mechanics of
each). Violin Lounge and Fritz Gearhart (fritzgearhart.com) for the "which first" positions;
Henryk Szeryng, quoted in The Strad, for "a combination of finger, wrist and forearm".</Cite>

## Pass 4 — The readiness gate

Verified, consistent criteria across sources — this is a **readiness self-check, not a
progress gate** (`RULES.md` #4), like `the-foundation-check` and `the-position-check`:

1. **You play in tune in first position without tension.** Vibrato learned on top of an
   unreliable pitch centre just wobbles the wrongness.
2. **Your left hand is relaxed** — thumb not clenched against the neck, a supple wrist that
   does not collapse inward.
3. **You can hold the violin securely without your left hand.** "If the violin drops when you
   release your left hand, your hold needs work before vibrato training." The hand must be
   *free to move*, not propping the instrument.
4. **You have begun shifting / are comfortable moving to third position.** A hand that already
   knows how to travel the neck loosely is a hand that can vibrate. (This is why Stage 7
   follows Stage 6.)

General pedagogy puts this at roughly 6–12 months of playing; for this course it is "after the
Foundation gate and Stage 6", which is stricter.

<Cite>Violinspiration "How to do Vibrato on the Violin" (the in-tune-without-tension gate);
Violin Lounge "Complete Guide" (the hold-without-the-left-hand test, comfort in third
position). Corroborated by the source PDF §68, which uses Violinspiration's gate explicitly.</Cite>

## Pass 5 — The preparation drills

The standard set, corroborated across Violinspiration, the Meghan Faw Suzuki method, Fritz
Gearhart, and The Strad. All are done **without the bow first** — the motion is isolated
before the bow's timing is added.

**Off the instrument:**

- **Finger-joint loosening** — make an "O" with the thumb and each finger in turn; slowly
  straighten and curve the joints. The **pinky** is the one that fights this and the one that
  matters (4th-finger vibrato is the hardest).
- **The wave** — wave the relaxed hand back and forth at the wrist, forearm still, to feel
  wrist-only motion.
- **The vibrato pulse** — a quick, light *forward-and-rebound* motion of the hand, off any
  surface; the seed of the whole technique (Gearhart's starting point).

**On the instrument, no pitch:**

- **The knock** — rock the hand/wrist backward toward the pegs in a knocking motion; you can
  gently tap a knuckle or the heel of the hand against the violin's upper bout or the pegs to
  feel the rhythm and the release.
- **Polishing / wiping / shining the string** — 3rd finger down in first position, slide the
  whole hand up and down the neck (1st ↔ 3rd position) as if polishing the string, letting the
  finger's first joint release. Teaches the loose lengthwise motion that vibrato is a small
  version of.
- **Vibrato on the body of the violin / a matchbox / against a wall** — the motion with
  nothing to tune.

**On the string, with pitch:**

- **The pulse ladder** — metronome ~60 BPM: 1 rock per beat, then 2, then 3, then 4, then
  dotted patterns and triplets and sixteenths, then continuous. Each **finger**; each
  **string**. (Meghan Faw's "pizza / pepperoni / I-would-like-a-slice-of-pizza" mnemonic is
  the same ladder.)
- **Start on the 1st or 2nd finger** (strongest, quickest results), on the **A string** —
  e.g. rock B down toward B♭ and back. Then extend to all fingers (**4th last and hardest**)
  and all strings (**G and E strings harder** than D and A).
- **Exaggerate wide and slow at the start.** The first sound is a slow, wide "siren" and it
  **will sound bad** — that is expected and temporary; say so in the lesson.

<Cite>Violinspiration "How to do Vibrato on the Violin" (finger circles, the wave, string
wiping, the knock, the metronome ladder); Meghan Faw, "Learn Vibrato in 5 Easy Steps" (Suzuki
tradition — knocking off then on the instrument, finger rolling, the pizza ladder, "do not
develop habits the student will have to undo"); Fritz Gearhart (the pulse, the seven-step
progression); The Strad, "Developing arm, wrist and finger vibrato".</Cite>

## Pass 6 — Building it on the string, and the "months, not weeks" truth

The PDF's §72 gives a **6-week micro-plan**. It is a reasonable *sequence*, and its own closing
line is the honest caveat to keep: *"Vibrato development often takes months, not six weeks;
this is a sequence, not a guarantee. If tension or pitch-center loss increases, go backward."*

The PDF's week-by-week (to adapt, not reproduce verbatim):

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | slow wrist (or arm) motion, exaggerated width, control not speed |
| 3 | the same, other motion type / other fingers |
| 4 | 2–4 oscillations per beat at ~60 BPM (= 2–4 Hz — a practice speed, per Pass 2's check), all strong fingers, no speed race |
| 5 | sustained notes; start and stop the vibrato deliberately |
| 6 | add it to selected long notes in an easy melody; keep the pitch centre |

The non-negotiable points threaded through every source: **relaxation is the whole game**
(tension anywhere caps speed and ruins evenness); **without the bow first**; **go backward the
moment tension or sharpness creeps in**; **short sessions** (5–10 minutes) — vibrato practice
fatigues the hand fast and a tired hand practises the wrong motion.

<Cite>Source PDF §72 (the 6-week plan and its "months not weeks" caveat — kept as the honest
frame). Relaxation-as-primary and short-sessions corroborated across Violinspiration, The
Strad "Art of Vibrato", and this course's own `how-to-practise` / `how-to-practise-a-bow-stroke`
pattern.</Cite>

## Pass 7 — Vibrato in the music: continuous / terminal, width × speed, the faults

**Continuous vs terminal.** "True" continuous vibrato — an oscillation that literally never
stops, even between notes — is **an illusion**: the motion physically pauses at every finger
change and string crossing. What players call continuous vibrato is a *minimised* gap.
**Terminal (or "late") vibrato** — start the note straight, senza vibrato, and let the vibrato
*bloom* on it — is a real Baroque/Classical expressive device *and* the best control exercise
(you can only bloom it deliberately if you can start and stop it deliberately).

**Width × speed as two dials.** Match them to the music: wider + slower for a broad lyrical
line; narrow (and often a touch faster) for Classical clarity; width follows the dynamic
(more in *forte*, less in *piano*); a *messa di voce* on one long note (Stage 5) becomes, with
vibrato, exactly Spohr's 1832 prescription — the swell and the vibrato grow together.

**The common faults** (name each with its fix — this is beat 5 for the whole movement):

| Fault | Sound | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Goat" / "nanny" vibrato | too fast + too narrow + tense | back to slow wide sirens; relax |
| Slow-and-wide, uncontrolled | a lurch, not a shimmer | build wrist flexibility (the knock) |
| Stiff thumb | vibrato won't start, or is jerky | practise with the thumb lifted clear of the neck |
| Vibrato stops between notes / at crossings / at shifts | the line goes dead in the gaps | slow coordinated finger-change practice; Fischer's continuous-vibrato exercises |
| Sharp-sounding vibrato | the note reads as out of tune | keep the oscillation at/below the pitch, never above (Pass 1) |
| The wobble reaches the bow arm | the tone pulses / scratches | decouple — bow-only and vibrato-only, then combine |
| The mean pitch drifts | the note goes flat or sharp as you vibrate | anchor the pitch first; check against a drone (Stage 8 tie-in) |

<Cite>Violin Lounge "Complete Guide" (continuous vibrato as an illusion; the fault list);
Simon Fischer, *Basics* — "Continuous vibrato" (keeping the motion alive through crossings and
shifts). Terminal vibrato as a Baroque/Classical device: Greta Moens-Haenen and Clive Brown,
via the historical sources in Pass 8.</Cite>

## Pass 8 — The history: ornament → continuous (and why it decides how you play Mozart)

This is the stage's load-bearing musicological claim, so it is hedged carefully. The **strong
form** ("nobody used vibrato before Kreisler made it continuous") is overstated in popular
sources; the scholarship qualifies it. The **safe, verified form**: *before roughly 1900,
vibrato was a selective, varied ornament — applied to chosen notes for chosen effects — not
the continuous default it became in the 20th century.*

- **Geminiani (1751)** is the outlier — he recommended vibrato "as often as possible" (advice
  removed from later editions of his treatise).
- **Leopold Mozart (1756)** — vibrato ("the tremolo") is *"an ornamentation which arises from
  Nature herself"*, the wave-like undulation of a struck bell or slack string. Use it *"on a
  long note"* and *"only... where nature herself would produce it."* And the famous warning:
  *"Performers there are who tremble consistently on each note as if they had the palsy."*
  (full quote and verification in Pass 9.)
- **Spohr (*Violinschule*, 1832)** — restrained, intentional; a wave-like hand motion giving a
  slight pitch deviation; *for* passionate passages and long sustained notes with dynamic
  swells. He even wrote notational signs marking where vibrato should occur.
- **Mendelssohn** — marked specific spots for vibrato in his violin concerto — a direct
  contrast to modern continuous practice.
- **The New Grove Dictionary (2001):** *"vibrato seems always to have been accepted as an
  ornament until the first quarter of the 20th century, when its continuous use gradually
  became the norm."*
- **The pivot: Ysaÿe (late 19th c.), then Kreisler.** Henry Roth: Kreisler was the first
  renowned violinist to use vibrato *constantly*, even in passagework. Flesch called Kreisler's
  vibrato "extraordinarily intensive".
- **Auer (*Violin Playing As I Teach It*, 1921)** — still resisting it: *"This curious habit of
  vibrating on each and every tone amounts to a physical defect, whose existence those who are
  cursed with it do not in most cases even suspect."*

**The K.216 payoff** (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4): Mozart's music wants a **narrow, selective,
ornamental** vibrato — the 1756 setting, near enough — **not** the wide continuous vibrato of a
Bruch or Tchaikovsky concerto. This is the same keystone Stage 3 built for symbols ("the
symbol tells you *what*, the era tells you *how*"), now applied to the expressive layer, and it
is why the history is in the stage and not just a footnote.

<Cite>The New Grove Dictionary of Music and Musicians (2001), "Vibrato". Beverly Jerold,
"Did early string players use continuous vibrato?" (The Strad). Clive Brown, *Classical and
Romantic Performing Practice 1750–1900* (OUP, 1999); Greta Moens-Haenen (Baroque vibrato);
David D. Boyden, *The History of Violin Playing*. Geminiani, *The Art of Playing on the Violin*
(1751); Spohr, *Violinschule* (1832). Auer quote: *Violin Playing As I Teach It* (1921), widely
cited. Kreisler: Henry Roth via Strings Magazine, "When Fritz Kreisler Changed How We Hear and
Play Violin". **Hedge stated in-lesson:** "varied and selective, not the modern continuous
default" — never "nobody used vibrato before Kreisler".</Cite>

## Pass 9 — The Leopold Mozart passage, verified (→ `LINEAGE-QUOTES.md`)

The single most-cited passage in the vibrato literature, which makes it the most-garbled —
`RULES.md` #9 ("quotes are facts") applies, and `LINEAGE-QUOTES.md`'s existing discipline
("confirm exact Knocker wording before it ships") applies. From the *Versuch einer gründlichen
Violinschule* (1756), **Chapter XI, "Von dem Tremulo" / "Of the Tremolo"**; English from Editha
Knocker's translation (*A Treatise on the Fundamental Principles of Violin Playing*, OUP 1948).

Verified against three independent secondary sources with only trivial variation:

- On the nature of it: *"The tremolo is an ornamentation which arises from Nature herself and
  which can be used charmingly on a long note, not only by good instrumentalists but also by
  clever singers."* — **close paraphrase, well-attested.**
- On producing it: *"...one makes a small movement with the whole hand; which, however, must
  not move sideways but forwards toward the bridge and backwards toward the scroll."* —
  **close-verified Knocker wording** (matches standingstones.com's quotation and the
  violinist.com discussion).
- The warning: *"Performers there are who tremble consistently on each note as if they had the
  palsy."* — **quote as commonly cited.** Minor variants seen across sources: "tremble *upon
  every* note" / "as if they had *the perpetual fever*". Ship as a marked close paraphrase, or
  confirm the exact Knocker phrasing against the physical text before quoting verbatim.
- The rule: *"The tremolo must only be used at places where nature herself would produce it."*
  — **close-verified.**

He also notates **three speeds** — a slow even tremolo, an accelerating one, and a rapid one —
tying width/speed variation back to the 18th century.

## The beat-3 problem — decide it now, not at build time

`RULES.md` #4 and `CONTENT-STANDARD.md` §3 make **beat 3 ("hear it", with the one-press
example)** mandatory. But:

- **abcjs + soundfont render neither vibrato nor rubato nor tone colour.** The playback engine
  physically cannot produce the sound these lessons are about.
- **There is no notation for vibrato** — the course's own `beamed-notes-double-stops-and-vibrato`
  states it: "there are never extra noteheads for it".

So every lesson in Movements A–D (and much of E) **structurally cannot hit beat 3 the way all
~290 existing lessons do.** Stage 6 hit an analogous wall (a stopped chord's *sound*) and
handled it by writing a **stage-specific standard into `stage-6.md`** ("the genuinely
new-motion lessons state plainly where a teacher's eye would catch what this course can't").
Stage 7 needs its own version, decided up front because it shapes every lesson in the vibrato
half.

**Recommended resolution (for the flagged decision):** all three of —

1. **`<NotatedExample>` shows *where* the expressive event goes**, not its sound: a fully
   labelled long note inside a phrase (name + string + finger, always), an agogic lengthening
   marked in the rhythm, the spot in a tune where vibrato belongs. Playback gives the notes and
   rhythm; the caption carries the pointer to the sound.
2. **Named reference recordings carry the "hear it" beat**, explicitly — the course already
   runs a "20 famous works" listening thread and the K.216 listening study; a vibrato lesson
   says "the site can't play you this — listen to [specific recording / passage]".
3. **One or two new `<Diagram>` hand-motion SVGs** for "see it" — the arm pivot (at the elbow)
   vs the wrist rock. This is the only new code the stage needs, and it matches the `<Diagram>`
   additions Stages 0 and 2 already made.

And **state the limit in every vibrato lesson**, the Stage-6 way: *the sound of vibrato is the
one thing this course cannot play for you — here is what to listen to, and this is the lesson
to get a real teacher's ear on.*

## Movement-plan sketch (draft — for `stage-7.md`)

Not final; this is the shape the research points to. ~22 concept lessons + Movement P.
Larger than the CLAUDE.md / CURRICULUM.md sketch's "~12–16" because (a) vibrato's motor
breakdown needs the room every physical skill in this course gets, and (b) the expression half
has 6–8 genuinely new ideas even after the inheritance table subtracts everything Stages 3–6
already taught. Still the **smallest stage in Phase 2** (Stage 6 = 30, Stage 8a = 44).

Tracks: `left-hand` for the vibrato movements, `ear`/`bow` for the expression movements as
fits. Every pitched `<NotatedExample>` gets full name + string + finger labels (`RULES.md` #1).

- **Movement A — Vibrato: what it is, and are you ready (4).**
  `what-vibrato-is` (the cue *and* the waveform, both labelled — Pass 1) ·
  `is-your-hand-ready` (the readiness self-check, not a gate — Pass 4) ·
  `vibrato-is-not-a-fix` (learn the pitch centre first; vibrato can't rescue an out-of-tune
  note — the PDF §68 point) ·
  `vibrato-used-to-be-an-ornament` (the history → selective and varied; the Mozart-vs-Bruch
  preview — Pass 8).

- **Movement B — The motion, off the note (4).**
  `the-vibrato-pulse` (forward-and-rebound, off the instrument; the knock — Pass 5) ·
  `arm-and-wrist` (the two motions, the pivot of each, the "which first" disagreement stated
  plainly — Pass 3) ·
  `polishing-the-string` (the lengthwise release drill on the string, no pitch — Pass 5) ·
  `finger-vibrato` (REFERENCE — Pass 3).

- **Movement C — Building it on the string (4).**
  `the-pulse-ladder` (metronome 1→2→3→4 per beat, without the bow first; the Hz check spelled
  out — Pass 2, Pass 6) ·
  `every-finger-every-string` (4th finger + G/E strings are the hard ones; start 1st/2nd on A
  — Pass 5) ·
  `starting-and-stopping` (senza → con deliberately; terminal vibrato — Pass 7) ·
  `months-not-weeks` (the PDF's 6-week plan, honestly captioned; go backward if tension rises
  — Pass 6).

- **Movement D — Vibrato in the music (3).**
  `vibrato-on-a-long-note` (the long notes of a tune you already play; the swell + vibrato =
  messa di voce with vibrato, Spohr's exact combination — Pass 7) ·
  `width-speed-and-character` (the two independent dials; wider/slower vs narrower; width
  tracks the dynamic — Pass 2, Pass 7) ·
  `keeping-the-centre` (don't let it drift the pitch or shake the bow; decouple then combine;
  the drone check — Pass 7, Stage 8 tie-in).

- **Movement E — Expression you haven't been taught yet (4).**
  `stealing-time` (rubato as an *action* — take time and give it back; Leopold Mozart's
  "melody presses on while the accompaniment keeps time" made physical; where it's styled
  (Chopin, Kreisler) and where it barely is (Mozart)) ·
  `the-agogic-accent` (lengthen a note a hair to stress it — the accent with no symbol) ·
  `tone-colour-as-a-choice` (the five lanes + sul tasto / sul ponticello / flautando as a
  character palette, not just terms — builds on Stage 3 `the-colour-words`, Stage 5
  `the-five-lanes`) ·
  `what-is-this-music-about` (character / affekt as the *first* question; C.P.E. Bach's
  *Affektenlehre*; Quantz / L. Mozart — the player must feel it to move the listener).

- **Movement F — Playing in style, and the check (3).**
  `playing-mozart-like-mozart` (the synthesis — vibrato narrow + selective, portamento off,
  rubato tiny, non-legato default; the Romantic opposite; "the era tells you *how*" —
  `LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4) ·
  `making-your-own-choices` (take a tune you play cold, make ~5 expressive decisions, say
  *why* — the first real exercise of `PRD.md` §3's "Choose") ·
  `the-expression-check` (a self-audit, not a gate — `RULES.md` #4; mirrors `the-foundation-check`
  / `the-position-check`; ties to the Stage 8 ear gym and the Intermediate gate's "vibrate on
  a sustained note").

- **Movement P — repertoire.** See the decision below — the recommendation is **1–2 lessons
  that put vibrato on tunes the course has already built and source-verified**
  (`tune-ode-to-joy-shaped`, `tune-amazing-grace`, `tune-bach-musette` — all live, all with
  verified melodies), plus **Shenandoah** as the one genuinely new candidate worth sourcing
  (PD, D major, first position, long lyrical notes — flagged in `REPERTOIRE.md` as a "good
  build candidate"). **`tune-long-long-ago` is NOT available** — it is still on the Stage 1
  pending-PD-source list. Any *new* tune beyond Shenandoah is deferred, Stage-6-style, until it
  has a ≥2-source-verified PD arrangement in hand.

## Decisions this stage plan needs from the user (flag, don't silently decide)

Mirrors the way `stage-6-research.md` ended with three flagged decisions before `stage-6.md`
locked anything in.

1. **The beat-3 resolution for the vibrato lessons.** Recommend: `<NotatedExample>` for
   *where* + named reference recordings for the *sound* + 1–2 hand-motion `<Diagram>` SVGs,
   with the limit stated plainly in every vibrato lesson (the Stage-6 pattern). Confirm before
   build — it shapes every Movement A–D lesson.

2. **"Arm and wrist, whichever the student finds first; finger = REFERENCE."** Confirm this
   synthesis. The literature genuinely does not support picking one to teach first, so the
   lesson will *state the disagreement*. The alternative (this course picks wrist-first, or
   arm-first, and drills it) would be inventing a consensus that isn't there.

3. **Movement P scope.** Recommend: build 1–2 "vibrato on a tune you already own" lessons
   reusing existing verified tunes, plus source **Shenandoah**; defer any other new tune.
   The alternative is a full Stage-6-style deferral of all of Movement P.

4. **Lesson count / scope: ~22 + P, vs the sketch's "~12–16".** Confirm the expansion is
   wanted. It matches how every Phase-2 stage has gone deeper than its one-line sketch
   (Stage 6 sketch said "positions one at a time"; it became 30 lessons). If the user wants it
   tighter, the expression half (Movement E–F, 7 lessons) is where it compresses — the vibrato
   half is already near-minimal for a motor skill this course teaches at Stage-6 depth.

## The honest limitation (stated up front, the way Stage 6 Pass 20 stated it)

Everything in this document is **secondary pedagogy-literature research on a purely kinesthetic
skill.** Vibrato is the single most "you have to feel it, and someone has to watch your hand"
topic in the entire course — more so than shifting, more so than the bow strokes. No amount of
cross-referencing substitutes for a teacher's eye on an actual hand doing an actual motion.

The built lessons should carry this explicitly: **vibrato is the lesson to get a real teacher
(or an experienced player) to check in person.** A written description, however careful, of
"the hand rocks at the wrist, base knuckles leading, fingertip supple" cannot tell the reader
whether *their* hand is doing that or is quietly tensing the thumb to fake it. The course can
teach the reader what vibrato is, what it's for, how to practise it, what the faults sound
like, and when Mozart wants it narrow — and it should be honest that the last 20% needs a
mirror at minimum and a teacher ideally.

This is the same flag Stage 6 and Stage 8 carried; it applies here more than to either.
