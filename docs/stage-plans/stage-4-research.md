## Pass 1 — What a scale actually is

*(research batch 1: 2026-09-03 — 9 searches/fetches)*

### The core definition

A **scale** is an ordered set of pitches spanning an octave, heard in relation to one of them
— the **tonic** (also *tonal centre*, *keynote*). It is *ordered* (step by step, up or down),
it is *bounded* (tonic to tonic), and it is *referential* (every note is heard as "so far from
home"). The PDF's own line is good and we keep it: *"a scale is not just a finger exercise; it
teaches the pitch vocabulary of a key."*

- The **tonic** is scale-degree 1 — the note the music sounds finished on, the note a final
  cadence lands on, the note the key is named after. C major's tonic is C.
- A scale and a **key** are not the same thing. The scale is the *ladder of notes*; the key is
  the *gravitational field* — the sense that one of those notes is home and the others lean
  toward it. You can play a C-major scale as a dry exercise; you're *in the key of C* when a C
  actually feels like rest.
- **Diatonic** = the seven-note pattern of 5 whole steps + 2 half steps with the half steps as
  far apart as possible (major and the natural minor are the diatonic scales). **Chromatic** =
  all twelve half steps, no tonic implied. Stage 4's spine is diatonic; the chromatic and the
  exotic families are a REFERENCE movement (Pass 16).

### The seven scale-degree names — and why each is called that

Every degree has a name, and the names are a *map of function*, not decoration:

| Degree | Name | Where the name comes from |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tonic** | the tone — home, the key's name |
| 2 | **Supertonic** | "just above the tonic" |
| 3 | **Mediant** | "in the middle" — midway between tonic and dominant; it's also the note that decides major vs minor |
| 4 | **Subdominant** | "the dominant below" — a fifth *below* the tonic, mirroring the dominant a fifth above |
| 5 | **Dominant** | the second-most important note after the tonic — a perfect fifth up, the note the harmony pulls through on the way home |
| 6 | **Submediant** | "the mediant below" — a third below the tonic, mirroring the mediant a third above |
| 7 | **Leading tone** | it *leads* to the tonic — only a half step below it, and it aches to resolve up. When degree 7 is a *whole* step below (natural minor, modal tunes) it's called the **subtonic** instead, and it doesn't pull the same way. |

The "sub-" pairs are worth drawing: dominant/subdominant are a fifth above / a fifth below the
tonic; mediant/submediant are a third above / a third below. The scale is symmetrical around
its tonic in its *naming*.

### Stable notes and restless notes

This is the seed of everything in Movements F–H, and it's audible from day one:

- **Stable** — degrees **1, 3, 5** (the tonic triad). Land on one of these and the phrase can
  rest.
- **Restless / tendency tones** — **2, 4, 6, 7**. They want to move, and mostly by the nearest
  step: **7 → 8/1** (up, the strongest pull of all), **4 → 3** (down), **2 → 1** (down),
  **6 → 5** (down). Degrees 4 and 7 are the most unstable — they're a half step from their
  target and a tritone from each other.
- You can *hear* this without any theory: play a scale up and stop on the 7th. Your ear
  finishes it for you. That's the lesson's "hear it" beat, and it costs nothing.

### The tonic-relative naming systems — the bridge for this user

The whole point of naming notes by *degree* instead of by *letter* is that the pattern stays
put when the key moves. Two systems do this:

- **Movable-do solfège** — `do re mi fa sol la ti do`, where **do is always the tonic**,
  whatever the actual pitch. A tune is sung on the same syllables in every key. Each syllable
  carries a *function* you can learn to recognise by ear: *ti* always leans up to *do*, *fa*
  always sags toward *mi*, *sol* always sounds like it's pointing home. This is the
  ear-training vehicle in Stage 8, and Stage 4 introduces the syllables so they're familiar
  when the ear gym opens.
- **Sargam** — `Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni`, the North-Indian equivalent, and the user's native
  vocabulary. The correspondence is close and worth stating plainly: **Sa = do = tonic**,
  **Pa = sol = the fifth**. In Hindustani practice **Sa and Pa are *achala* ("fixed") — once
  you choose Sa, Pa is locked a pure fifth above it** — and the other five (Re, Ga, Ma, Dha,
  Ni) are *vikrut* ("movable"), each able to take its natural (*shuddha*) pitch or an altered
  one. Sargam is sung **against a drone**, and — the sentence that matters most for this user —
  *"the emphasis is not on the scale but on the intervals."* That is exactly the mindset Stage
  4 wants: a scale degree is a *distance from home you can hear*, not a fingering.
  - **Accuracy guard** (`RULES.md` #8, and the user *can* catch a wrong Indian-music fact): a
    **raga is not a scale.** It has an ascending line (*aaroha*) and a descending line
    (*avroha*) that can differ, notes that are stressed or skipped, characteristic phrases
    (*pakad*), ornament conventions, and often an associated time of day. The *pitch material*
    of a raga can be compared to a Western scale; the raga itself cannot. Say "the notes a raga
    draws from" — never "the raga is the such-and-such scale."

## Pass 2 — Whole steps, half steps, and the major formula

*(research batch 2: 2026-09-03 — 8 searches/fetches)*

### The two step sizes, and where they live on the violin

- A **half step** (semitone) is the smallest ordinary step in Western notation — B→C, E→F, or
  any note to the black key beside it on a piano. A **whole step** (tone) is two half steps —
  C→D, F→G, B♭→C.
- Steps are *distances*, not notes. "W" and "H" describe the gaps between the scale's notes.
- On the violin there are no frets, so the two sizes are two *hand shapes*: for a whole step
  the fingers are apart (about a finger's width of space); for a half step the fingers **touch**.
  This is the single most useful physical fact in the stage — the reader can feel a half step.
- Stage 1 already taught low/high fingers ("low 2 / high 2"). Stage 4 renames what the reader
  already does: **"fingers touching = half step; fingers apart = whole step."**

### The four first-position finger patterns

Standard beginner-method vocabulary (Doflein, Essential Elements, ViolinOnline). On one string,
first position, the pattern is *where the one touching-pair sits*:

| Pattern | Fingers that touch (½ step) | Example on the D string | The major scale it starts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | between **1 and 2** | D–E–F–G–A (½ at E–F) | C major (from D string it's degrees 2–3–4–5–6) — half step 3→4 |
| **2** | between **2 and 3** | D–E–F♯–G–A (½ at F♯–G) | **D major** and **G major** (½ step 7→8 and 3→4) |
| **3** | between **3 and 4** | D–E–F♯–G♯–A (½ at G♯–A) | **A major** (½ step 3→4 lands at G♯–A on the string above) |
| **4** | **none touch** (all whole steps) — low 1 | E♭–F–G–A on the D string | B♭ major (and the "low 1" shape) |

The insight to teach: **the major-scale pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H, laid across two strings in first
position, produces exactly these hand shapes** — pattern 2 on the lower string, pattern 2 again
on the upper string, for G and D major. The scale isn't an abstraction; it's two identical
hand shapes a string apart. (This is the tetrachord idea made physical — see below.)

### The major formula

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H.** Seven steps, back to the tonic an octave up. Two facts make it
worth a concept lesson, not just a stated rule:

1. **It's two matching halves.** Degrees 1-2-3-4 are W-W-H; a W joins them to degrees 5-6-7-8,
   which are W-W-H again. Each half is a **tetrachord** ("four notes"). Same shape, stacked a
   whole step apart. On the violin: the same finger pattern, one string higher.
2. **You can build a major scale on *any* note** by walking the formula. Start on E:
   E –(W)→ F♯ –(W)→ G♯ –(H)→ A –(W)→ B –(W)→ C♯ –(W)→ D♯ –(H)→ E. That's E major, and the
   formula *told* you it needs four sharps — nobody had to memorise it.

### Worked, in the keys this stage actually plays

| Key | Walk the formula | Sharps/flats it forces |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | C D E F G A B C | none — the half steps land on the natural pairs E–F, B–C |
| **G** | G A B C D E **F♯** G | F♯ (so the last step F♯→G is a half step) |
| **D** | D E **F♯** G A B **C♯** D | F♯, C♯ |
| **A** | A B **C♯** D E **F♯** **G♯** A | F♯, C♯, G♯ |

Every sharp appears because the formula demanded a half step where the naturals only offered a
whole step (or vice versa). **This is the mechanism behind key signatures** — Pass 3.

### Why *this* pattern is "the major scale"

Worth one or two honest sentences for beat 6, not a musicology lecture:

- The pattern is old — it's the medieval/Renaissance **Ionian** mode, named by **Heinrich
  Glarean** in *Dodecachordon* (1547) when folk music's "major" sound pushed into art music.
  Over the Baroque it hardened into *the* reference scale, and tonal harmony has been built on
  it since.
- It's often called a "natural" scale because it sits close to the low overtones of a vibrating
  string — the deep reason the violin's open strings and their scales *ring*. That's Movement J
  (the physics); Stage 4 plants the pointer here.

## Pass 3 — Key signatures: the order, and reading any one

*(research batch 3: 2026-09-03 — 6 searches/fetches)*

**What Stage 1 already did** (`the-key-signature`, `sharps-flats-and-naturals`,
`accidentals-inside-a-bar`): a key signature is a standing instruction — *these notes altered,
every octave, whole piece, until cancelled*; one sharp = G major; the order of sharps **F C G
D A E B** as far as the top three keys (C/G/D/A); and it explicitly says *"you'll build the
whole logic in Stage 4."* So Stage 4 **completes** that stub — it does not re-teach "what a
key signature is."

### The full order — and why it's that order

- **Sharps: F C G D A E B.** **Flats: B E A D G C F** — the exact reverse.
- Each successive sharp is a **perfect fifth above** the last (F→C→G→D…). Each successive flat
  is a perfect fifth **below** the last (B→E→A→D…).
- **Why a new key a fifth up adds exactly one sharp:** the new key's scale is the old one's
  upper tetrachord promoted to be the lower tetrachord, plus a new upper tetrachord — and the
  only note that has to change to keep the W-W-H shape is the **7th degree of the new key**,
  which must be raised to become a leading tone. G major's new sharp (F♯) is G major's 7th.
  D major keeps that F♯ and adds C♯ — D major's 7th. The sharps pile up in fifth-order because
  the *keys* march up in fifths.
- **Down a fifth adds one flat** by the mirror logic: the new note is the **4th degree**
  (subdominant) of the new key, flattened. C→F adds B♭ (F major's 4th). F→B♭ adds E♭.
- On the staff the sharps/flats sit in a **fixed zig-zag shape** for each clef — you learn the
  picture, not a re-derivation every time.
- This *is* the circle of fifths, approached from the notation side — Movement C draws the
  wheel.

### Reading any key signature — the two tricks

- **Sharp keys:** the **last sharp is the leading tone** — go up one half step and you're on
  the tonic. Last sharp F♯ → G major. Last sharp G♯ → A major. Last sharp A♯ → B major.
- **Flat keys:** the **second-to-last flat names the key.** Flats B♭ E♭ A♭ → the key is E♭.
  Flats B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ → A♭.
- **F major (one flat, B♭) is the exception** — memorised on its own, because "second-to-last"
  needs at least two flats.
- **C major / A minor** — no sharps or flats; you tell them apart by the music (Movement D).
- Every signature is **either a major key or its relative minor** — same seven notes, different
  home. Which one is a *listening* judgement, taught in Movement D.

### The historical aside (beat 6)

The **one flat is the oldest accidental in Western notation.** By around the 10th century
scribes were already flattening **B** to soften the harsh F–B tritone (the "diabolus"), an
unwritten singer's convention called **musica ficta** ("feigned music"). The flat sign itself
is a stylised lowercase *b* — from Italian **"be molle," soft B** — and the natural and sharp
signs are the "hard b" (*b quadratum*, the square b) of the same medieval pair. Key signatures
are the descendant of that one habit, formalised.

## Pass 4 — The circle of fifths

*(research batch 4: 2026-09-03 — 5 searches/fetches; `components/CircleOfFifths.tsx` already exists)*

### What the wheel is

Twelve keys arranged so that each step **clockwise goes up a perfect fifth** (C → G → D → A →
…) and **adds one sharp**; each step **anticlockwise goes down a fifth** (C → F → B♭ → E♭ → …)
and **adds one flat**. C major sits at 12 o'clock with a clean signature. It's the order of
sharps, the order of flats, the key-signature count and the "how related are these two keys"
question, all in one picture.

The existing `<CircleOfFifths highlight="G" />` component draws exactly this — majors on the
outer ring, relative minors on the inner ring, C at top, the sharp side down the right, the
flat side down the left, meeting at F♯/D♭ at the bottom.

### Why *fifths* — and why a violinist owns this diagram already

- After the octave, the **perfect fifth is the most consonant interval** and the strongest
  root-to-root move in tonal harmony (it's the interval between a key and its dominant). So
  arranging keys by fifth puts the *most closely related* keys next to each other.
- **The violin is tuned in fifths: G–D–A–E.** Those four notes are **four consecutive spokes
  of the circle** (…F–C–**G–D–A–E**–B…). The instrument in the reader's hands *is* a segment of
  the circle of fifths — which is also why G, D and A major "fall under the hand" (Stage 1 said
  this; now it has a reason) and why the open strings ring in those keys.
- Adjacent keys on the wheel share **six of their seven notes**; C and G differ by exactly one
  (F vs F♯ — G's new leading tone). Keys on **opposite sides** of the wheel (a tritone apart)
  share almost nothing — that's "distant."

### The inner ring — relative minors (taught in Movement D, previewed here)

Each major key shares its signature with a minor key whose tonic is **three steps clockwise**
on the circle — equivalently the **6th degree** of the major, a minor third below the tonic.
C major ↔ A minor, G major ↔ E minor, D major ↔ B minor. The circle shows the pairing; *how a
minor key behaves* is Movement D.

### The enharmonic bottom

At 5–7 o'clock the sharp spellings and flat spellings collide: **B = C♭**, **F♯ = G♭**,
**C♯ = D♭** — the same piano keys, the same violin fingers, written differently. This is why
there are **15 written major key signatures** (7 sharp + 7 flat + C), not 12 — three keys have
two legal spellings. A first-position violinist meets none of these as home keys; they're a
Movement E reference.

### What it's *for* (so it isn't a poster)

- **I–IV–V is three adjacent slots.** In C: F (IV) – C (I) – G (V), the subdominant one step
  anticlockwise, the dominant one step clockwise. The three chords that harmonise almost any
  simple tune are literally neighbours.
- **"Circle-of-fifths progressions"** — vi–ii–V–I and the like — walk anticlockwise a step at a
  time; the ear hears each chord "fall" toward home. Pachelbel's Canon is one lap.
- **Modulation** usually goes to a **neighbour** — most often the dominant. This is the missing
  "why" behind Stage 3's binary-form repeat (`stage-3-research.md` Pass 5): the first half
  travels **one step clockwise to the dominant**, the second half comes home. The circle is the
  map of that trip.

## Pass 5 — Minor

*(research batch 5: 2026-09-03 — 6 searches/fetches)*

### What "major" and "minor" actually name

Not an emotion — an **interval structure**, and the deciding note is the **3rd degree**:

- **Major 3rd above the tonic** = 4 half steps = two whole steps → *major*.
- **Minor 3rd above the tonic** = 3 half steps = whole + half → *minor*.
- C major's 3rd is E; C minor's 3rd is E♭. Move that one note and the whole scale flips
  character. The PDF's own line is right and we keep it: *"Major does not literally mean happy.
  It describes an interval/key structure. Emotional effect depends on melody, harmony, rhythm,
  register, articulation and context."*
- Minor also flattens the **6th and 7th** (compared to the parallel major): natural minor is
  **1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7**. But the 3rd is the one you *hear* as "it went minor."

### The natural minor scale

- Formula: **W – H – W – W – H – W – W**. (It's the major formula rotated to start on the 6th
  degree — see relative minor below.)
- A natural minor: A B C D E F G A — no sharps or flats, the "white-key minor."
- **E natural minor**: E F♯ G A B C D E — one sharp (F♯), the **relative minor of G major**,
  and the stage's target minor scale. On the violin it's the **same first-position hand shape
  as the G-major scale** (high 2 on the D string for F♯, low 2 on the A string for C) — the
  reader already owns the fingering. That's the lesson's gift.

### The three minors — and *why* there are three

Natural minor has a problem for tonal harmony: its 7th degree is a **whole step** below the
tonic, so it's a *subtonic*, not a leading tone — it doesn't pull home, and the chord built on
5 comes out minor (weak) instead of major (strong). Composers fix it by **raising the 7th**:

| Form | Notes (A minor) | What changed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | A B C D E F G A | — | the plain minor collection; used freely in melody, folk, modal music |
| **Harmonic** | A B C D E F **G♯** A | 7th raised | restores the leading tone → a strong **V chord** and a real "perfect cadence" in minor. Side effect: an **augmented 2nd** (3 half steps) between ♭6 and ♯7 (F→G♯) — the "exotic" gap heard in Bach chorales, flamenco, klezmer. |
| **Melodic** | ascending A B C D E **F♯ G♯** A · descending A G F E D C B A | 6th *and* 7th raised going up; both natural coming down | raising only the 7th left that ugly augmented 2nd in a *melody*; raising the 6th too smooths the line up to the tonic. Coming down there's no pull to smooth, so it reverts to natural. |

- **Accuracy guard** (Indian-music, `RULES.md` #8): the harmonic-minor's ♭6–♯7 augmented
  second *also* occurs in some ragas (e.g. the pitch material near Bhairav) and in Arabic maqām
  (Hijaz). Fine to mention as "this wide step shows up in music all over the world," **not**
  "harmonic minor is raga Bhairav" — the raga is a whole grammar, not a scale (Pass 1 guard).
- **Don't memorise 36 minor scales** (the PDF says this and it's right): learn the three
  *forms* as concepts, drill the specific minor keys your repertoire uses (E minor now).

### Relative vs parallel — the two ways a major and a minor connect

| | Shares… | Differs in… | Example | How to picture it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Relative** | the **key signature** (all 7 notes) | the **tonic** (home note) | G major ↔ **E** minor (both 1 sharp) | same notes, different one feels like home; minor tonic = **6th degree** of the major = a **minor 3rd below** |
| **Parallel** | the **tonic** | the **key signature** (3 notes: ♭3 ♭6 ♭7) | G major ↔ **G** minor (1 sharp vs 2 flats) | same home, the 3rd/6th/7th flip; composers switch parallel mode mid-piece for a colour change ("the sun went behind a cloud") |

- **Telling which one a signature is:** one sharp on the page is **G major or E minor**. You
  decide by ear and by evidence — does the music rest on G or on E? Is there a **D♯** floating
  around (E minor's raised leading tone, which won't be in the signature)? A raised-7th
  accidental is the giveaway that you're in the minor.
- **Minor key signatures never contain the leading tone** — it's raised with an *accidental*
  every time it's wanted. That's why a minor-key piece looks "full of sharps that aren't in the
  signature."

- **Twelve pitch-class keys, fifteen written signatures.** Three keys have two legal
  spellings: **B (5♯) = C♭ (7♭)**, **F♯ (6♯) = G♭ (6♭)**, **C♯ (7♯) = D♭ (5♭)**. Same notes,
  same violin fingers, different names on the page. "Twelve semitones in an octave" does *not*
  mean "twelve possible key signatures" — the PDF makes this point and it's worth keeping.
- Composers pick the spelling with **fewer accidentals** (D♭'s five flats beat C♯'s seven
  sharps) or the one that fits where the music is going.

### Double sharps and double flats

- **𝄪** (double sharp) raises a note two half steps; **𝄫** (double flat) lowers it two. They
  never appear in a *standard* key signature — but they appear as **accidentals** inside
  sharp/flat keys, most often as a **raised leading tone in a minor key that's already sharp**:
  G♯ minor's leading tone is **F𝄪** (F♯ raised again = G natural in sound, but spelled F𝄪
  because it's a raised *seventh degree*, and the seventh degree of G♯ is some kind of F).
- A first-position violinist in G/D/A/C/E-minor essentially never meets one. **REFERENCE** — read
  it, recognise it, move on.

### The keys the violin was built for

This is a genuinely useful, violinist's-eye lesson and it isn't anywhere yet:

- The open strings **G–D–A–E** mean that keys built on those notes get **sympathetic
  resonance** — when you stop an A in D major, the open A string vibrates along with it and the
  note blooms. **D major is the most resonant key on the violin**: open D is the tonic, open A
  the dominant, open G the subdominant, open E the supertonic — all four open strings are
  useful scale degrees.
- **G, D, A, E major (and G, D, A, E minor — the opens don't change) ring.** Flat keys —
  D♭, A♭, G♭ — sound comparatively **muted**, because the resonance shifts onto weaker scale
  degrees and there are no open strings to reinforce the tonic.
- Lore worth flagging *as* lore: players say Stradivari "voiced" instruments toward D major;
  string folklore, not documented fact — present it as "players have long felt…".
- This is **why Stages 1–4 live in G/D/A/C**: not arbitrary, and not just "few sharps" — the
  instrument itself is loudest and most in-tune-able there. Full physics in Movement J.

## Pass 7 — Intervals: number and quality

*(research batch 7: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches)*

### The two halves of an interval's name

An interval is the distance between two notes. Its name has a **number** and a **quality**.

- **Number** — count the letter names, **inclusive of both ends.** C up to E: C-D-E = a **3rd**.
  D up to A: D-E-F-G-A = a **5th**. The number ignores sharps and flats — C→E and C→E♭ are both
  "some kind of 3rd."
- **Quality** — how *wide* that number is, in half steps. Two families:
  - **2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths** come as **minor** or **major** (m one half step narrower than M).
    Ladder: diminished – minor – major – augmented, each rung a half step.
  - **unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves** come as **perfect** (never "major/minor"). Ladder:
    diminished – perfect – augmented.
- **Melodic** interval = the two notes one after another (a leap in a tune). **Harmonic**
  interval = the two together (a double stop, a chord). On the violin an interval is also a
  **hand span** — a 3rd is adjacent strings same finger area; an octave is the classic
  1-and-4 stretch across two strings.

### Every interval in the major scale, from the tonic

| From ^1 to… | Interval | Half steps | On open D → |
|---|---|---|---|
| ^1 | Perfect unison | 0 | D–D |
| ^2 | **Major** 2nd | 2 | D–E |
| ^3 | **Major** 3rd | 4 | D–F♯ |
| ^4 | Perfect 4th | 5 | D–G |
| ^5 | Perfect 5th | 7 | D–A |
| ^6 | **Major** 6th | 9 | D–B |
| ^7 | **Major** 7th | 11 | D–C♯ |
| ^8 | Perfect octave | 12 | D–d |

The major scale, measured from its tonic, is **all perfect and all major** intervals. That's a
clean thing to know: if you build up from *do* and land on a *minor* anything, you've left the
major scale.

### Why "perfect"

The unison, octave, 5th and 4th are the intervals that sit at the **bottom of the overtone
series** (ratios 1:1, 2:1, 3:2, 4:3) — the most blended, most "in tune with physics" sounds,
and they're **identical in major and minor keys** (C→G is a perfect 5th whether you're in C
major or C minor). Thirds and sixths *change* with the mode, so they get "major/minor";
these four don't change, so they're just "perfect." (The physics is Movement J.)

### Inversion — flip the interval over

Move the bottom note up an octave (or the top note down). Two rules:

- **Rule of 9:** the number and its inversion add to 9. A 3rd inverts to a 6th, a 2nd to a 7th,
  a 4th to a 5th.
- **Quality flips:** **major ↔ minor**, **augmented ↔ diminished**, **perfect stays perfect.**
- So a major 3rd (C–E) upside down is a minor 6th (E–C). *Same two notes* — which is why a
  melody and its accompaniment can be "a 3rd apart" or "a 6th apart" and it's the same
  relationship seen from opposite ends.

### The tritone, and consonance vs dissonance

- **Consonant** (restful): perfect unison/octave/5th (perfect consonances); major & minor
  3rds and 6ths (imperfect consonances — the *sweet* ones, what melodies and harmony lean on).
- **Dissonant** (tense, wants to move): 2nds, 7ths, and the **tritone**.
- The **tritone** — augmented 4th / diminished 5th, **three whole tones** (F–B), medieval
  nickname *diabolus in musica* — is the most restless interval in tonal music. It aches to
  resolve, the two notes squeezing to a 3rd or splaying to a 6th. It's the live wire inside
  the **dominant 7th chord** (Movement G) and the reason V pulls to I so hard.
- **The perfect 4th is the awkward one** — consonant when it's the upside-down of a 5th (in a
  chord above the bass), treated as a dissonance in strict two-part writing. Worth one honest
  sentence, not a rabbit hole.
- **Compound intervals** — bigger than an octave: a 9th is a 2nd plus an octave, a 10th is a
  3rd plus an octave. Same letter, "add 7 to the number."

## Pass 8 — Hearing it: the ear thread

*(research batch 8: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. F6–F8 below may become their own "Ear"

### The stance: function first, intervals second

This is the user's weakest axis and it gets the biggest investment (`PRD.md` §6.1). The
research is clear on *how* to invest:

- **The ear hears notes in relation to a tonal centre, not as free-floating pairs.** Multiple
  studies find that **scale-degree / functional ear training beats interval training** for
  actually reading and playing music — because "a bare interval with no key around it" almost
  never happens in real music. Interval training teaches you to recognise a thing that isn't
  there.
- So Stage 4's ear work is, in order: (1) **hear each scale degree against a drone** and know
  it by its *character*; (2) *then* attach interval names, and attach them to **tunes the
  reader has already played in this course**, not to a generic list.
- The **movable-do / Sa** syllables from Movement A are the handle: sing the syllable every
  time you play the note, and the function seeps in. Kodály's folk-song-and-movable-do method
  is the documented model.

### The drone drill (this is the engine)

The tuner **·** drone **·** metronome bar already exists (`CLAUDE.md` pipeline). Turn on a
**tonic drone** (say D), and:

- Play the scale up and down, singing/saying `do re mi …` — hear every note *pull against* the
  drone. ^1, ^3, ^5 settle onto it; ^7 grinds and wants up; ^4 leans down to ^3; ^2 and ^6
  hang.
- This *is* the `notes-that-rest-and-notes-that-lean` lesson (A3) turned into a daily drill.
- Then: drone on, play a **single random degree**, name it before you check. Then **two
  notes**, name both. Then a **3–4 note fragment of a known tune**, name the degrees.
- Then the discrimination drills: "is that ^3 or ^5?", "major 3rd or minor 3rd from the
  tonic?" — the seed of Stage 8's dictation and chord-quality work.

### Tuning by ear — the free feedback loop

- Stop a D on the A string against the open D; a G on the D string against the open G; the
  ringing tells you. Every scale in a sharp key has these check-points built in.
- **Thirds and sixths are harder to tune** and sound slightly different depending on the
  system (just vs equal) — that's a real thing, and it's Movement J. Here we just notice it.

## Pass 9 — Triads: the four chords

*(research batch 9: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. This pass + Pass 10 + Pass 11 pay the
`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §2 chord debt. Movement G may split in `stage-4.md`.)*

**Tie-in.** Stage 3's `reading-double-stops-and-chords` already prints `[G,DB]` and calls it
"a G-major chord" — open G, open D, B on the A string. Stage 4 explains *why* that's a G-major
chord, and connects it to the K.216 opening, which is **that same triad, rolled and then
arpeggiated** (D–B–G).

### What a triad is

Three notes, **stacked in thirds**: **root** – **third** – **fifth**. On the staff a
root-position triad is a little snowman — three noteheads all on lines, or all on spaces. Root
to fifth is always some kind of 5th; the character comes from *which* thirds are inside.

### The four qualities — a two-third recipe

| Quality | Lower 3rd | Upper 3rd | Root→5th | Sound | Built on C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Major** | major | minor | perfect 5th | bright, settled | C–E–G |
| **Minor** | minor | major | perfect 5th | dark, settled | C–E♭–G |
| **Diminished** | minor | minor | **diminished** 5th | tense, unstable (it *is* a tritone plus a note) | C–E♭–G♭ |
| **Augmented** | major | major | **augmented** 5th | floating, no gravity | C–E–G♯ |

The reader can *hear* all four on one root — that's the lesson's spine. Major and minor differ
by one note (the 3rd); diminished and augmented each squeeze or stretch the 5th too.

### Inversions — same three notes, different note on the bottom

| Position | Bass note | Figure | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Root position** | the root | (none) | most stable — the chord "standing up" |
| **1st inversion** | the 3rd | **6** | lighter, mobile — common under a melody |
| **2nd inversion** | the 5th | **6/4** | unstable, needs resolving — often a cadence decoration |

- The figures are **figured bass** — the numbers are *intervals above the bass note* (a "6"
  chord has a 6th and a 3rd above the bass; a "6/4" has a 6th and a 4th). Baroque players read
  a bass line plus these numbers and built the chords live. Name the system; don't drill it.
- A composer inverts a chord to give the **bass line a smooth shape** or to keep a particular
  note singing on top. Same harmony, different surface.

### Spelling one from any root

Stack two thirds and then bend the notes to the quality you want: from G → G-B-D (major, it's
already right); from D → D-F♯-A in a sharp key (major), D-F-A (minor). This is a quick drill,
not a concept lesson.

### On the violin

- A rolled 3- or 4-note chord (Stage 3, Stage 6) is a triad played **close position, bottom
  to top.** The `[G,DB]` stack is G-major, root position (G lowest).
- **Broken** into one-note-at-a-time, a triad is an **arpeggio** (Movement I) — and that's the
  K.216 opening: G-major triad, D on top coming down through B to G.
- `read` (name the chord, name its quality and inversion) = **now**. `perform` (a clean rolled
  triple stop) = **Stage 6**. Kept separate (`RULES.md` #7).

## Pass 10 — Diatonic harmony: the chords of a key

*(research batch 10: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. Draft lessons → Movement H.)*

### The seven triads a key gives you

Build a triad on **each degree** using **only the key's own notes**, and every major key
produces the same pattern of qualities:

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman numeral | **I** | **ii** | **iii** | **IV** | **V** | **vi** | **vii°** |
| Quality | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor | diminished |
| In **G major** | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | F♯° |
| In **C major** | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | B° |

- **Notation convention:** UPPER-CASE numeral = major triad, lower-case = minor, **°** = diminished.
  You can read a whole analysis off the numerals without naming a single note.
- **Minor keys** (using the harmonic-minor raised 7th, which is what gives a strong V):
  **i – ii° – III⁺ – iv – V – VI – vii°**. In **E minor**: Em – F♯° – G⁺ – Am – **B** – C – D♯°.
  The natural-minor forms give a minor v and a major ♭VII instead — real music uses both, so
  minor technically has ~13 diatonic triads. The workhorses are **i, iv, V**.

### The three jobs a chord does

Every chord belongs to a **function family** — what it's *for* in a phrase:

| Function | Chords | Feel | Scale degrees it's built from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tonic (T)** | **I**, vi | home, rest | 1 3 5 (6) |
| **Predominant (PD)** | **IV**, ii | leaving home, building | 4 6 (2) |
| **Dominant (D)** | **V**, vii° | maximum tension, *must* resolve to I | 5 7 2 (4) — contains the leading tone |

The standard phrase arc is **T → PD → D → T** — home, step away, tension, home. Once the reader
hears that shape, they hear the bones of almost every tune they've played.

### The primary chords — I, IV, V harmonise anything

- Between them, **I (do-mi-sol)**, **IV (fa-la-do)** and **V (sol-ti-re)** contain **all seven
  scale degrees.** So any diatonic melody note is in at least one of them — three chords can
  accompany a huge amount of music.
- Rough rule for putting chords under a tune: a bar leaning on **do / mi / sol** → **I**; a bar
  leaning on **sol / ti / re / fa** → **V** (or V7); a bar leaning on **fa / la** → **IV**.
- This is where "**understand what you're playing**" (`PRD.md` §6.2) gets concrete: from here
  on, every repertoire lesson can say *"this phrase sits on the tonic; this one pushes to the
  dominant and doesn't come home until the repeat."*

### Harmonising the course's own tunes

- **Ode to Joy** (G major): almost all **I** and **V**, one **IV** near the end. *Twinkle*: I –
  IV – I – V – I shapes. *Amazing Grace*: I – IV – I – V – I, the classic hymn frame.
  *Minuet in G*: I to V at the double bar (the modulation Stage 3 flagged), V back to I after.
- The play-around: melody on the violin, a **drone or a simple two/three-chord track**
  underneath (the drone bar can hold a tonic; a chord track is a static audio file, no new
  component). Hear the moment the harmony *needs* to change.

### Lesson implications (Pass 10)

- **Three new lessons**, all `understand` + `hear`: `the-chords-of-a-key` (the 7 triads, the
  quality pattern, roman numerals, G major + E minor), `the-three-jobs-chords-do` (T/PD/D, the
  arc), `harmonising-a-tune-you-know` (I-IV-V under Ode to Joy / Twinkle / Amazing Grace).
- **No new component.** Chords under the melody = a static audio track + the existing drone
  bar. If that's fiddly, a `<NotatedExample>` with the melody note *and* its chord written as a
  stack, rolled, is enough (`RULES.md` #17).
- Every pitched example fully labelled; the chord stacks get a combined label like the Stage-3
  `[G,DB]` pattern (`{ name: "G + B + D", string: "…", finger: "…" }`).
- Tag: `the-chords-of-a-key` NEXT, the other two NEXT — concept understood now, fluent later.

## Pass 11 — The dominant seventh (and the other sevenths)

*(research batch 11: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. This is the harmony ceiling for Stage 4.
Draft lessons → Movement H.)*

### Adding the seventh

Stack **one more third** on top of a triad and you have a **seventh chord** — four notes:
root, 3rd, 5th, 7th.

The one that matters for everything Classical is the **dominant seventh, V7** — built on the
**5th degree**, a **major triad plus a minor 7th** above the root. In G major: **D – F♯ – A –
C** (sol – ti – re – fa).

### Why V7 is the engine of tonal music

- V7 contains a **tritone**: its 3rd and its 7th — which are scale degrees **7 (leading tone)**
  and **4 (subdominant)** — sit a tritone apart. These are the two most restless notes in the
  key, stacked into one chord.
- That tritone **resolves**: **^7 → ^1** (up a half step, the leading-tone pull) and **^4 → ^3**
  (down a half step). The two notes squeeze inward — or spelt the other way, splay outward —
  and the whole chord *collapses onto the tonic triad*.
- So V7 → I is the **strongest "we're home" gesture in the language.** It's why the circle of
  fifths works (every step is a dominant resolving), why a piece can travel to a new key by
  tossing in that key's V7 first ("the dominant is transportation"), and why K.216's phrases
  land the way they do.
- The reader should **hear** it: play D–F♯–A–C (rolled), then G–B–D. The pull is unmistakable.
  That's the lesson.

### The other seventh chords — named, for reference

This is the edge of Stage 4. Name them, play one of each, move on:

| Chord | Recipe | Sound | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Major 7th** (Cmaj7) | major triad + major 7th | lush, still | jazz; Romantic colour |
| **Minor 7th** (Cm7) | minor triad + minor 7th | mellow | jazz; ii7 in a progression |
| **Dominant 7th** (C7) | major triad + minor 7th | *wants to move* | **everywhere in tonal music** — the one that counts |
| **Half-diminished 7th** (Cø7 / Cm7♭5) | diminished triad + minor 7th | tense, aching | ii°7 in minor; vii7 in major |
| **Fully diminished 7th** (C°7) | diminished triad + diminished 7th | maximum tension, no clear home | vii°7 in minor; a pivot to anywhere |

### On the violin

- A four-note seventh chord is **rolled** like any chord (Stage 3 notation, Stage 6 technique).
- Reading one — name it, name its function, know it resolves — is the **now** skill.
- `read` vs `perform` stays split (`RULES.md` #7).

## Pass 12 — Cadences

### Cadence = the punctuation at the end of a phrase

A **cadence** is the short chord formula that closes a phrase. It's **punctuation** — some
cadences are full stops, some are commas, one is a semicolon-with-a-swerve. Every phrase of
every tune the reader has played ends with one.

### The four, in G major

| Cadence | Chords | Punctuation | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect authentic (PAC)** | **V (or V7) → I**, melody ends on **^1**, both root position | full stop **.** | the strongest "done." How most pieces end. |
| **Imperfect authentic (IAC)** | V → I but melody ends on ^3/^5, or a chord is inverted | a weaker full stop | "done, but keep the paragraph open" |
| **Plagal** | **IV → I** | a settled full stop | the **"Amen"** — hymn endings; warm, undramatic |
| **Half (HC)** | ends **on V** (anything → V) | comma **,** | unresolved — the phrase leans forward, expecting an answer |
| **Deceptive / interrupted** | **V → vi** (you expected I) | semicolon / "…!" | sets up the full stop, then sidesteps it |

- **Spotting one in a score:** look at the end of a musically complete unit — often a longer
  note or a rest, a slur bracket closing, the barline before a new idea. Check what chord the
  harmony lands on: **I** = authentic or plagal; **V** = half.
- **The Phrygian half cadence** (iv⁶ → V in a minor key, the bass falling a half step to the
  dominant) — name only; the reader will meet it in Baroque slow movements.

### In the course's own repertoire (the play-around)

- ***Ode to Joy*** — phrases 2 and 4 close **V → I, perfect**; phrases 1 and 3 stop on ^2, an
  open, half-cadence feel. That AABA-ish "question, question, answer" is *made of* cadences.
- ***Minuet in G*** — a **half cadence** at the double bar (ends on V — exactly what Stage 3
  flagged as "not home yet"), a **PAC** at the very end.
- ***Amazing Grace*** — **IV** under "how sweet the **sound**", a **PAC** to finish.
- ***Twinkle*** — "little **star**" is a textbook **PAC**.

### The era changes it (beat 6, ties to `LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4)

The **Picardy third** — ending a minor-key piece on a **major** tonic chord (raise the 3rd of
the final i). Bach does it in roughly **half** his minor-mode pieces; by the Classical era it
had largely fallen out of fashion. Same cadence symbol on the page (V → i), different note in
the final chord depending on **when** it was written. "The symbol tells you *what*; the era
tells you *how*."

- **Fix `RULES.md` #1:** the chord examples need real `{ name, string, finger }` combined

## Pass 13 — Phrase and structure

*(research batch 13: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. Draft lessons → Movement I. Recaps the

### Three sizes of musical idea

| Unit | Size | What it is | In the course's tunes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Motif** | 2–6 notes | the smallest recognisable seed; a piece is often built almost entirely from one | Ode to Joy's three-note step-up; Beethoven 5's "da-da-da-DUM" |
| **Phrase** | ~4 bars | a musical sentence — beginning, middle, end, **closed by a cadence**; "one breath" | each line of Twinkle; each line of Ode to Joy |
| **Period** | ~8 bars | **two phrases**: an **antecedent** (ends weak — half cadence — "question") and a **consequent** (ends strong — PAC — "answer") | Ode to Joy's first 8 bars are a textbook parallel period |

- **Antecedent → consequent = question → answer.** The listener *hears* the first phrase leave
  a door open and the second close it. That effect is created entirely by the **cadences** at
  the two phrase-ends (Movement I part 1) — structure and harmony are the same subject.
- **Sentence** — the other common 8-bar shape: a short idea, its echo, then a longer
  continuation to the cadence (1 + 1 + 2). Name it lightly; the period is the one to *hear*.
- **Binary / ternary form** (Stage 3 plant): a phrase → a period → a **section** (the A of a
  minuet) → the whole **AABB** or **ABA**. The circle-of-fifths trip to the dominant and back
  (Movement C) is the tonal plot underneath.

### Phrases have edges, and a shape

- **Phrase mark vs slur** (recap Stage 3 `a-slur-is-one-bow`): a **short** curve over a few
  notes = **one bow**; a **long** curve spanning bars, often over barlines = a **phrase mark**
  — it means *shape this as one thought and breathe at the end*, not "one bow." Tell them
  apart by length and by whether it crosses barlines.
- **Phrases breathe.** At a phrase-end you **lift / retake / ease** — a tiny grammatical
  silence, like the gap after a spoken sentence. Nothing in the notation always tells you where;
  the cadence and the phrase mark do.
- **Phrases have an arc** — usually grow toward a high or stressed note, then settle to the
  cadence. This is *phrasing*, and shaping it is **Stage 7**; Stage 4's job is to make the
  reader **see the phrase** so the bow-plan and the breath have something to serve.
- **Bow-plan connection** (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §1): knowing where the phrase peaks lets
  you plan the bow so you're not stranded at the tip on the important note. Light pointer to
  Stage 5's bow-distribution work.

## Pass 14 — Arpeggios and broken chords

*(research batch 14: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. Draft lessons → Movement J. The K.216

### What an arpeggio is

A **broken chord** — the notes of a chord played **one at a time**, up or down, instead of
together. From Italian *arpeggiare*, "to play like a harp." A **scale moves by step**; an
**arpeggio skips through the chord tones** (1–3–5–8). Practising both teaches the two ways a
melody ever moves.

### The first-position arpeggios (verified fingerings)

One octave, first position, every note labelled:

| Arpeggio | Notes | Fingering (string · finger) |
|---|---|---|
| **G major** | G–B–D–G | G:0 · G:2 high · D:0 · D:3 |
| **D major** | D–F♯–A–D | D:0 · D:2 high · A:0 · A:3 |
| **A major** | A–C♯–E–A | A:0 · A:2 high · E:0 · E:3 |
| **C major** | C–E–G–C | G:3 · D:1 · D:3 · A:2 low |
| **E minor** | E–G–B–E | D:1 · D:3 · A:1 · E:0 |
| **D⁷ (dominant of G)** | D–F♯–A–C(–D) | D:0 · D:2 high · A:0 · A:2 low (· A:3) |

- Each shape **repeats an octave higher on the next string pair** — the two-octave G arpeggio
  is the one-octave shape done twice. Same principle as the tetrachord (Movement B).
- These become `AnnotatedScore` lines and join `/practice`.

### Broken thirds

The scale played **in thirds** — G-B, A-C, B-D, C-E, … — the halfway house between a scale
and an arpeggio. A standard graded-syllabus element (ABRSM "thirds"); it drills the ear for
the 3rd and the hand for the little back-and-forth. NEXT-tagged.

### Arpeggios in real music — including the debt

- **Alberti bass** — a broken chord in the pattern **low–high–middle–high**, the stock
  Classical keyboard accompaniment (Mozart's piano sonatas are full of it). The violinist
  plays *over* it, but should recognise the shape when reading a score.
- **The Mannheim rocket** — a fast **rising arpeggio** that crescendos as it climbs, a
  dramatic curtain-up gesture the Mannheim orchestra made famous (`stage-3-research.md`
  Pass 2 already cites Mannheim for the crescendo).
- **The K.216 solo entrance is a G-major arpeggio.** The violin comes in on a high D and falls
  **D → B → G** — scale-degrees **5 → 3 → 1**, the tonic triad broken downward. A reader who
  has practised the G-major arpeggio in Movement J will look at that opening and see *a shape
  they already own*, not a wall. **This is the payoff of the whole stage** (`LESSONS-FROM-
  MOZART.md` §2).
- **Canon in D** — eight chords, endlessly re-arpeggiated; a first-position arpeggio étude in
  disguise.

### Lesson implications (Pass 14)

## Pass 15 — The physics: overtones, resonance, temperament

*(research batch 15: 2026-09-03 — 3 searches/fetches. Draft lessons → Movement K. Mostly
`understand` / REFERENCE, but `why-your-violin-rings` is a NOW intonation tool.)*

### The harmonic (overtone) series

A vibrating string doesn't just sound its whole length. It **also** vibrates in halves,
thirds, quarters, fifths… all at once, each quieter than the last, at **exact whole-number
multiples** of the fundamental frequency (1×, 2×, 3×, 4×…). That stack is the **harmonic
series**, and its intervals arrive **in order of consonance**:

| Harmonic | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interval above the fundamental | unison | octave | octave + P5 | 2 octaves | + M3 | + P5 |
| Frequency ratio to the one below | — | 2:1 | 3:2 | 4:3 | 5:4 | 6:5 |

- The **octave, 5th and 4th sit at the bottom** with the simplest ratios — which is *why*
  they're the "perfect" intervals (Movement F) and why the violin is tuned in fifths.
- **Harmonics 4 : 5 : 6 spell a major triad.** The major chord — and by extension the major
  scale — is literally the low overtones of any single note. That's the honest answer to
  "why does the major scale sound natural" (Movement B).
- A **natural harmonic** on the violin (Stage 3 notation, Stage 6 technique) is you touching
  the string at 1/2, 1/3, 1/4… to silence the fundamental and let **one member of the series**
  sound alone.

### Why your violin rings

- The open strings **G–D–A–E** are always vibrating-ready. Play a stopped note that matches an
  open string, or a strong **overtone** of one, and that open string **vibrates
  sympathetically** — the note blooms and keeps sounding. **B rings** too (it's a strong
  overtone of the open G).
- The **ring-tone notes in first position**: G string G/A/B · D string D/E/G · A string
  A/B/D (· E-string E/F♯/A). Land one dead in tune and you *hear* the instrument light up.
- This is a **free, built-in intonation check** — no tuner needed. It's the practical form of
  `tuning-by-ear` (F8) and the reason `the-keys-the-violin-loves` (E3) are the sharp keys.

### "In tune" is not one fixed thing

Three tuning systems, and a violinist slides between them by ear:

| System | Built from | Best for | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pythagorean** | pure 5ths only | **melody** — leading tones sit **high**, tight to the tonic; expressive lines | thirds come out wide/harsh |
| **Just** | pure intervals from the harmonic series (incl. 3rds/6ths) | **chords / drones** — maximum ring | you can't stay in one key and modulate |
| **Equal temperament** | octave split into 12 identical semitones | **keyboards** — every key equally usable | every interval but the octave is slightly impure |

- A violinist's melodic **F♯** (Pythagorean, leaning high toward G) is **not** the piano's F♯
  (equal), which is **not** the F♯ inside a ringing D-major chord (just, a touch lower). Good
  intonation on the violin means **placing the note where the musical context wants it** — and
  that's why a great player never sounds quite "in tune with the piano," on purpose.
- **The tabla / drone bridge** (guarded, `RULES.md` #8): North-Indian classical intonation is
  **drone-based and just-intonation-like** — every note tuned as a pure interval from Sa, which
  is exactly how a violinist tunes against an open string or a drone. Say that much. **Do not**
  assert shruti counts or map specific shrutis to Western cents — the 22-shruti framework is a
  debated theoretical model, not a fixed tuning table.

### The families to name

The major and natural-minor scales are the course's spine. These are the ones the reader
should **recognise**, not drill:

| Family | Notes | Character | An anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chromatic** | all 12 semitones | no tonic, pure colour / connective tissue | the "creeping" line in film suspense |
| **Whole-tone** | 6 notes, all whole steps | unmoored, dreamy — no leading tone, no gravity | Debussy *Voiles* |
| **Major pentatonic** | 1 2 3 5 6 (no half steps) | open, folk, "can't sound wrong" | *Amazing Grace* (mostly), *Auld Lang Syne*, the piano black keys |
| **Minor pentatonic** | 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7 | blues, rock riffs | *Sweet Child O' Mine* riff (a target) |
| **Blues** | minor pentatonic + ♭5 "blue note" | vocal, bent, expressive | any 12-bar blues |
| **Octatonic / diminished** | 8 notes, alternating W–H | symmetrical, eerie, ambiguous | Stravinsky; horror scores |

### The seven modes

Take the major scale and **start on each of its degrees** — same seven notes, a different note
as home, a different character (because the half steps now fall in different places relative
to the tonic):

| Mode | Start on ^ | = major/minor with… | Character | Meets it in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionian** | 1 | (the major scale) | bright, settled | everything |
| **Dorian** | 2 | minor with a **raised 6th** | minor but hopeful | *Scarborough Fair*, folk, "So What" |
| **Phrygian** | 3 | minor with a **♭2** | dark, Spanish | flamenco |
| **Lydian** | 4 | major with a **♯4** | floating, wondrous | film themes (*The Simpsons* opening leap) |
| **Mixolydian** | 5 | major with a **♭7** | bright but earthy | folk, bagpipe tunes, rock |
| **Aeolian** | 6 | (the natural minor) | melancholy | folk, minor-key pop |
| **Locrian** | 7 | minor with **♭2 and ♭5** | unstable, rare | almost nothing — a curiosity |

The lesson's one big idea: **same notes, different tonic, different music** — the mirror of
relative keys (Movement D), except here you *stay* on the unusual home note.

### A raga is not a scale — the bridge (get this exactly right)

This is the user's home ground, and the accuracy bar is absolute (`RULES.md` #8):

- In Hindustani music a **thaat** is a **parent scale** — a seven-note pitch collection. There
  are ten, and they exist **only to classify ragas**; a thaat is **never performed.**
  (Carnatic music's **melakarta** is a parallel 72-way system.)
- A **raga is not a scale.** It's built from a thaat's notes but it has its **own** ascending
  line (*aaroha*) and descending line (*avaroha*) — which can differ and can zig-zag —
  plus stressed and skipped notes, a characteristic phrase (*pakad*), ornament conventions
  (*meend*, *gamak*, *andolan*), an emotional world (*rasa*), and often a time of day. Two
  ragas can share a thaat and sound nothing alike.
- **Fair to say:** "the *notes* raga Yaman draws on line up with the Lydian-flavoured thaat
  Kalyan." **Wrong to say:** "raga Yaman is the Lydian mode."
- **What the two systems genuinely share** — and this is the useful part for the reader: a
  **fixed tonal home** (tonic / **Sa**), tuning and hearing **against a drone**, and every
  other note understood as **a distance and a tendency from home**, not as an absolute pitch.
  Western functional listening (Movement A, F) and raga both work that way. The tabla/drone
  ear the reader already has is the *right* ear for this whole stage.

## Pass 17 — Scale practice: the how and the why

*(research batch 17: 2026-09-03 — 4 searches/fetches. Draft lessons → Movement M. Splits the

### Why scales — the honest answer

A scale is **a map of a key laid out under your hand.** Practise the G-major scale and every
piece in G major is already half-learned — the finger pattern, the intonation of each note,
the ring-tone checkpoints, the string crossings. Scales train, all at once:

- **intonation** — the ring-tone notes (Movement K) are built-in "am I in tune" checks;
- **the finger patterns** (Movement B) — the hand shape for the key becomes automatic;
- **evenness** — equal note length, equal bow, equal weight, inaudible bow changes;
- **string crossings** and (later, Stage 6) **shifting**.

They are **vocabulary practice, not punishment.** The standard teaching line: *use scales to
work on specific weaknesses and to maintain core skills you can't afford to lose.*

### How to practise one — the routine

1. **Read it first.** Name every note, know the key signature, *before the bow moves* (the
   Stage-8 pre-read habit, started here).
2. **Slow, separate bows** — one note per bow, full sound, listening for each ring-tone.
3. **Slurred** — 2 notes per bow, then 4, then the whole octave in one bow; **divide the bow
   evenly** (4 up / 4 down for an 8-note slur).
4. **Rhythms** — dotted (long–short), reverse-dotted (short–long), triplets — to expose
   hesitation and even out the fingers against the bow.
5. **Metronome** — for *evenness*, not speed. Speed is the last thing that comes.
6. **Up and down.** The descent is where intonation quietly slips — practise it as hard as
   the ascent.

### Which scales, when

The order (the PDF's §58, matched to the ABRSM Grades Initial–3 ladder as an external
benchmark — *not* the curriculum):

| Stage | Scales | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **NOW** | G, D, A, C major (1 octave); **E natural minor** (1 octave) + matching arpeggios | all first position; the stage's playable target |
| **NEXT** | **G major 2 octaves** (still first position!); F, B♭ major (the low-1 pattern); E minor harmonic & melodic; E minor 2 octaves (needs a shift → Stage 6) | fingering expands |
| **LATER** | the full major/minor set; 2 octaves everywhere; **broken thirds**; the **chromatic** scale; faster bowings | |
| **ADVANCED** | 3-octave scales & arpeggios; **double-stop scales** (3rds, 6ths, octaves) | Stage 6+ |

- **ABRSM treats scales and sight-reading as separate skills** (the PDF flags this) — a scale
  drill is not a substitute for reading practice, and vice versa.

## Pass 18 — Transposition and concert pitch

*(research batch 18: 2026-09-03 — 3 searches/fetches. Small movement → Movement N. No existing

### Transposition — the same tune, a different level

Move every note of a melody **up or down by the same interval.** Every interval *inside* the
tune, and every rhythm, stays identical — only the pitch level changes. The tune sounds "the
same"; it's now in a different key.

- **The tool is movable-do / Sa.** Because the syllables stay glued to the scale degrees when
  the key moves, transposing is *"same syllables, new home."* Stage 1 already showed this — the
  `do-re-mi-fa` shape played in G major, then the identical shape in A major a string over.
- **On the violin, transposition is usually a hand-shift, not arithmetic.** Play Twinkle
  starting on open D (D major) or starting on open A (A major): **same finger pattern, moved to
  the next string pair.** A violinist "transposes up a fifth" by sliding the whole shape over.
- **The written method** (for reading, not playing by ear): choose the new tonic, write the
  new key signature, shift every note by the fixed interval — letter name *and* accidental —
  and check the accidentals that aren't in the new signature.

### Why a violinist needs it

- To match a **singer's** comfortable range.
- To play alongside a **B♭ or E♭ instrument** from its part, or to read a part written for one.
- To brighten a tune by taking it **up a third**, or to make a hard key **readable** by
  mentally moving it to an easy one while sight-reading (a Stage 8 trick).

### Concert pitch and the C instruments

- The **violin is a non-transposing / "C" instrument**: you read at **concert pitch** — the
  note you read is the note that sounds. A written C sounds a C.
- **Transposing instruments** (B♭ clarinet, B♭ trumpet, E♭ alto sax, F horn) read a written C
  and sound something else. The convention exists so a player can switch between instruments
  of one family and keep the **same fingerings** for the same written notes.
- The reader needs this only to **not be confused** when a clarinettist says "my C is your
  B♭" in a chamber rehearsal. One atom, REFERENCE.

### The first note of Mozart — decoded with Stage-4 tools

|---|---|
| **one sharp** in the signature | G major **or** E minor — and since the orchestra has been in G and the tune rests on G, it's **G major** (Movements B, D) |
| the **stacked chord** `D–B–G` | a **G-major triad** — scale degrees **5–3–1** — rolled bottom-to-top because the bridge is curved (Movement G; Stage 3 G1) |
| the **whole opening gesture** | the **G-major arpeggio**, the tonic triad broken — *a shape you practise* (Movement J), not a wall |

| **f** at the entrance | a dynamic relative to the orchestra's level (Stage 3 B) |

## Pass 21 — Re-verification: gaps found and closed

*(2026-09-03 — audited the 20 passes against a music-theory-fundamentals syllabus
(NYU / Juilliard *Elements of Theory* / AP Music Theory / Coursera Edinburgh), `PLAN.md`
Part A Stage 4, `CURRICULUM.md` §Stage 4, and the PDF's Part V. Four real gaps, several
one-line touches.)*

### Real gaps — three new lessons

1. **Non-chord tones.** Every fundamentals syllabus pairs "chords" with **non-harmonic /
   non-chord tones** — the melody note that *isn't* in the chord under it. Without this,
   `harmonising-a-tune-you-know` (H3) and `read-the-harmony-of-a-line` (O1) are dishonest: not
   every melody note is a chord tone, and the reader will hit one and think they've got the
   harmony wrong. The four to name: **passing tone** (step in, step on through), **neighbour
   tone** (step away and back), **suspension** (held over, resolves down by step), **appoggiatura**
   (leap in, step out — Stage 3 already taught the *ornament* spelling; here it's the
   *harmonic* idea). → **new lesson `notes-that-arent-in-the-chord`, Movement H, NEXT.**

2. **Harmonic rhythm.** The rate the chords change — once a bar, twice a bar, every beat — and
   the way it **speeds up into a cadence.** This is a natural bridge lesson *for this user*:
   harmony has a pulse, and hearing that pulse is a rhythm skill (their strength) pointed at
   harmony (their growth axis). Short. → **new atom `harmony-has-a-rhythm-too`, Movement H, NEXT.**

3. **Key character (*affekt*).** Before equal temperament flattened every key to the same
   colour, keys had *personalities* — **Schubart** (1806): D major "the key of triumph, of
   hallelujahs, of victory"; C minor "the lament of unhappy love." **Charpentier**, **Rameau**,
   **Mattheson** all wrote key-character tables. It's mostly lost to modern ears *because* of
   equal temperament (Movement K3) — which makes it a perfect REFERENCE aside that ties
   `the-twelve-keys`, `the-keys-the-violin-loves`, `in-tune-is-not-one-thing` and Stage 3's
   "the era tells you how" together. → **new atom `keys-used-to-have-characters`, Movement E,
   REFERENCE.**

### One-line touches folded into planned lessons

- **`the-intervals-in-the-major-scale` (F2)** — add the method + play-around for working out
  the quality of *any* interval, not just from the tonic: number by counting letters, quality
  by half-steps *or* by comparing the top note to the bottom note's major scale (D→F: F is not
  in D major → it's been lowered → **minor** 3rd).
- **`near-keys-and-far-keys` (C2)** + **`cadences-in-your-own-tunes` (I3)** — make **modulation**
  explicit: "the music changes home key mid-piece; you spot it by a new accidental appearing
  consistently, and a cadence in the new key." The Minuet-in-G double bar is the worked case.
- **`motif-phrase-and-period` (I4)** — name the **melodic sequence** (a motif repeated a step
  or a third higher/lower — the engine of Ode to Joy's development, and of circle-of-fifths
  progressions).
- **`what-an-arpeggio-is` (J1)** / **`what-a-scale-is` (A1)** — the unifying line: **every
  melody is some mix of steps (scale) and leaps (arpeggio)** — the two things Movements A and
  J teach are the two things a tune is *made of*.
- **`relative-major-and-minor` (D4)** — state the reverse direction too: **relative major =
  the 3rd degree of the minor = a minor 3rd up** (E minor → G major).
- **`what-a-scale-is` (A1)** — beat-1 flavour: *scala* = Latin for **"ladder."**
- **`what-a-cadence-is` (I1)** — one line on the **cadential 6/4** (the I⁶⁴–V–I "double
  suspension" that decorates so many perfect cadences).
