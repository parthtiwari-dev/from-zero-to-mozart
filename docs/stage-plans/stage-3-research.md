# Stage 3 — Everything else on the page: the research

**Research only. Written in passes — ~10 searches, then a section, repeat — so context doesn't
get lost.** This is the "research to the core" for Stage 3, the score-symbols stage.

Read `docs/PRD.md` §7, `docs/CONTENT-STANDARD.md` §3a–3b, `RULES.md`, and
`docs/LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §3–§4 alongside this.

**Why Stage 3 is make-or-break** (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §3): the K.216 run showed that a
real page carries a dense working vocabulary — ornaments with era-dependent realisation,
editorial-vs-composer marks, position numbers that aren't finger numbers, a bow-word glossary,

separate.

**Scope note:** Stage 3 adds no new *positions* (that's Stage 6) and no new *bow strokes* to
*perform* (that's Stage 5). Everything here is **reading** — recognise the mark, know what it
asks for, know the era caveat. `read` and `perform` stay separate milestones (`RULES.md` #7).

**Lesson count:** deliberately broad. The PDF's Part IV is ~13 sections; the research landed

## Pass 1 — Tempo, motion, and the style question

*(research batch 1: 2026-09-02 — 8 searches/fetches)*

### The tempo words themselves

The standard Italian ladder, slow → fast, with the **character** each carries (the speed is
secondary and contested — see below):

| Term | Character / literal | Rough BPM (a guide only) |
|---|---|---|
| Grave | heavy, solemn | ~20–40 |
| Largo | broad, dignified | ~40–55 |
| Larghetto | a little less broad than Largo | ~55–65 |
| Lento | slow | ~45–60 |
| Adagio | "at ease" — slow, expressive | ~55–70 |
| Adagietto | a touch quicker than Adagio | ~65–75 |
| Andante | "walking" | ~70–85 |
| Andantino | *ambiguous* — usually a little faster than Andante, sometimes slower | ~80–95 |
| Moderato | moderate | ~90–110 |
| Allegretto | moderately quick, lighter than Allegro | ~100–115 |
| Allegro | "cheerful, lively" → fast | ~115–140 |
| Vivace | lively, vivacious | ~130–150 |
| Presto | very fast | ~160–180 |
| Prestissimo | as fast as possible | ~180+ |

**Andantino is a known trap** — it means "a little Andante", and composers disagreed whether
that's faster or slower; even Mozart used it both ways. Flag it, don't drill a number.

### Modifiers — the words that bend a tempo word

- **molto** = very · **assai** = very / quite · **poco** = a little · **poco a poco** =
  little by little · **meno** = less · **più** = more · **ma non troppo** = but not too much ·
  **sempre** = always · **non** = not · **quasi** = almost / as if
- **meno mosso** / **più mosso** = "less / more motion" — a *new* tempo, not a gradual change
- so: *Allegro ma non troppo* (fast, but not too much), *Andante molto* (very walking-pace),
  *poco più mosso* (a little more moving)

### Changing the tempo mid-piece

| Mark | Means | Gradual or sudden |
|---|---|---|
| **rit.** (ritardando), **rall.** (rallentando) | slowing down | **gradual** |
| **ritenuto** (riten., rit.) | held back | **sudden** — the trap: `rit.` can abbreviate either |
| **accel.** (accelerando) | speeding up | gradual |
| **stringendo** (string.) | "pressing / tightening" — speeding up with urgency | gradual, more driven than accel. |
| **allargando** (allarg.) | "broadening" — slower *and* usually louder | gradual, for grand endings |
| **calando**, **morendo** | dying away — slower *and* softer | gradual |
| **a tempo** | back to the tempo before the change | immediate |
| **Tempo primo / Tempo I (Tempo Iº)** | back to the *movement's opening* tempo | immediate |
| **l'istesso tempo** | "the same tempo" — the beat stays, though the metre/notation changed | — |
| **rubato** (tempo rubato) | "stolen time" — flexible, expressive give-and-take | see the style note |

### German and French equivalents

- **German** (Bach's chorales, Schumann, Mahler, Berg): *Langsam* (slow), *Mäßig*
  (moderate), *Lebhaft* (lively), *Rasch* (quick), *Schnell* (fast), *Bewegt* (with motion),
  *Ruhig* (calm), *Getragen* (sustained), *Feurig* (fiery), *Nicht zu schnell* (not too fast).
- **French** (Debussy, Ravel, Fauré): *Lent* (slow), *Modéré* (moderate), *Animé* (animated),
  *Vif* / *Vite* (fast), *Retenu* (held back), *Cédez* (yield / slow), *En pressant*
  (pressing on), *Très* (very), *Assez* (rather), *Moins* (less), *Plus* (more).

### Metronome marks

- Written **♩ = 120** (or **M.M. ♩ = 120**), meaning 120 quarter-note beats a minute. **M.M.**
  = *Maelzel's Metronome*.
- **History matters for the style caveat:** Johann Maelzel patented the metronome in **1815**
  (the double-pendulum design was actually Dietrich Winkel's). Beethoven adopted it in 1817
  and published metronome marks for his Symphonies 1–8. **So metronome marks post-date the
  Classical era entirely** — K.216 (1775), all of Mozart, most of Haydn, have **none**.
- Beethoven's own marks are **famously disputed** — many sound implausibly fast; theories
  range from a faulty early metronome to Beethoven misreading the scale to a shift in taste.
  Good lesson material for "a number on the page is still an interpretation."

### The style question — what "Allegro" *meant* (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4)

This is the seed Stage 3 must plant, and it's cleanest to plant it here, right at the top:

- **A tempo word started life as a *mood*, not a speed.** Brossard's first music dictionary
  (1703) defines *Allegro* as "well animated… cheerful and lively" — the *speed* reading
  ("quicker than any except Presto") only hardened by his 1769 edition. *Adagio* literally =
  "at ease". *Andante* = "walking / going". You read the character first; the tempo follows
  from the character, the note-values, and the metre together.
- **Before metronome marks, the metre carried tempo.** A 6/8 *Allegro* moves faster than a
  4/4 *Allegro*; **C** and **₵** (cut time) at the same word are different speeds; lots of
  small note-values in the bar slow the felt tempo. This is *tempo giusto* — the "right
  tempo" implied by how the piece is written, before any word is added.
- **Classical Allegro was more contained than a modern one** — "a precise balance of elegance
  and briskness; technical clarity never sacrificed for raw speed" (Marty, *The Tempo
  Indications of Mozart*). Not slow — but not a showpiece scramble.
- **Leopold Mozart on rubato:** the *accompaniment stays strictly in time*; the *soloist*
  may sing slightly ahead of or behind it, and a good director lets them. Early rubato =
  the melody flexes over a steady bass, not "everyone slows down together." (This is a Stage
  7 topic in full; Stage 3 just names it correctly.)

### Lesson implications (Pass 1)

- **Tempo is character + speed, and you read the character first.** Every tempo lesson leads
  with what the word *feels* like, uses a rough BPM band, and never asserts an exact number.
- **The metre-carries-tempo point** connects straight back to Stage 2's simple/compound and
  C/₵ lessons — one lesson can bridge them.
- **The style caveat gets its own short lesson here** ("the symbol says *what*, the era says
  *how much*"), pointing forward to Stage 7. K.216's bare "Allegro" — no metronome mark, a
  Classical not a Romantic Allegro — is the worked example.
- German/French terms: one **REFERENCE** lesson (recognise, don't drill) — the violin canon
  the user is heading for is overwhelmingly Italian-marked, but Bruch, Bach chorales and the
  French school show up.

## Pass 2 — Dynamics

*(research batch 2: 2026-09-02 — 8 searches)*

### The level marks

**Softest → loudest**, and they are **relative, never a decibel level** — *mp* just means "a
bit louder than *p*, a bit softer than *mf*":

`pppp` · `ppp` (pianississimo) · `pp` (pianissimo) · `p` (piano) · `mp` (mezzo-piano) ·
`mf` (mezzo-forte) · `f` (forte) · `ff` (fortissimo) · `fff` (fortississimo) · `ffff`

- **piano** = "soft" (Italian: literally *quiet*), **forte** = "loud/strong", **mezzo** =
  "half". More letters = more extreme.
- Extremes beyond `fff` / `ppp` are a Romantic-and-later thing (Tchaikovsky's `pppppp`);
  Classical scores rarely leave `pp`–`ff`.

### Change marks — words vs hairpins

| Written | As a symbol | Means |
|---|---|---|
| `cresc.` (crescendo) | **hairpin opening `<`** | grow louder |
| `dim.` / `decresc.` (diminuendo / decrescendo) | **hairpin closing `>`** | grow softer |
| `cresc. poco a poco` | a long hairpin, often dashed | louder *little by little*, over many bars |
| `molto cresc.` | a short steep hairpin | a lot louder, fast |
| `cresc. al ff` | hairpin ending at a target dynamic | grow *to* a stated level |

The **word** form is looser (starts where written, no defined end); the **hairpin** is
precise (its length and endpoints show exactly where and how much). A hairpin crammed into
two bars feels breathless; the same rise stretched over sixteen feels like a slow swell.

### The accent / stress marks (dynamic, not articulation)

| Mark | Full | Effect | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| `sf`, `sfz`, `fz` | sforzando / *sforzato* — "forced" | a sudden strong accent | **just that note/chord** |
| `rf`, `rfz` | rinforzando — "reinforcing" | emphasise a few notes / a short phrase | **until the next dynamic** |
| `fp` | forte-piano | strike **loud, then instantly soft** on the same note | one note |
| `sfp`, `sfzp`, `fzp` | sforzando-piano | a sforzando attack, then immediately piano | one note |
| `subito p` / `subito f` | subito = "suddenly" | drop / jump instantly, no taper | at that point |
| `più f`, `meno f` | more / less forte | relative to the current level | at that point |

The **`sf` vs `>` trap**: an accent `>` is context-relative (a stress *within* the prevailing
dynamic); an `sf` is an absolute-ish jolt regardless of the surrounding level. And `sf` in a
*piano* passage is still soft — it's a *relative* sudden emphasis.

### Dying away

`morendo` (dying), `calando` (falling — softer *and* slower), `smorzando` (smothering —
softer, a touch more sudden), `perdendosi` (losing itself — fading, often slowing),
`al niente` ("to nothing" — fade to silence), plus `mezza voce` / `sotto voce` ("half voice"
/ "under the voice" — a hushed, held-back tone, not just quiet).

### The style layer — how dynamics *worked* in Mozart

- **Baroque = terraced dynamics.** Fixed levels, changed in steps like a terrace — partly a
  harpsichord/organ limitation (no touch-sensitive volume). Written dynamics are sparse.
- **The Mannheim orchestra** (1740s–60s) made the long orchestral **crescendo** and
  **diminuendo** famous — the "Mannheim roller", a rising line over a held bass, whipping the
  whole band from `pp` to `ff`. This is the bridge from terraced to graduated.
- **Classical (Mozart, Haydn) = graduated, and rhetorical.** Mozart shapes form with dynamics
  — **sudden `f`↔`p` contrasts** (a "surprise"), **echo effects** (a phrase repeated softer),
  the crescendo used sparingly and structurally. Dynamics **"emulated the inflections of
  spoken language"** (Leopold Mozart's *Violinschule* framing).
- **Leopold Mozart on `fp`:** he explains it as *a paired forte–piano stress on a single
  note, for emphasis* — not a mistake, a gesture.
- **`subito p`** is a Classical favourite — the music swells as if to a big arrival, then
  ducks to a whisper instead.

### On the violin — dynamics are a bow decision (Stage 5 preview, but name it here)

Louder / softer on the violin = **bow speed × weight × contact point**, together:
- **forte** — more bow, more arm weight, contact point nearer the **bridge** (it can take the
  weight), often lower half.
- **piano** — less bow, light, contact point toward the **fingerboard**, often upper half;
  the *impression* of piano is smoothness and no accent as much as low volume.
- **messa di voce** — the controlled swell (`< >`) on one long note. Tartini: mastering it
  "is equal to mastering the bow." Stage 3 *reads* it (`< >` on a whole note); Stage 5 & 7
  *do* it. Not something to apply to every long note — a chosen expressive moment.

- **Relativity first.** The opening idea of the dynamics movement is "these are ratios, not
  volumes." Then the ladder.
- **Words vs hairpins is one lesson** — same meaning, different precision, and the *shape* of

- **The accent family (`sf` `fp` `rf` `subito`)** needs its own lesson — it's dense and the

- **The terraced→graduated→Classical story** is a short style lesson, twin to Pass 1's

- **"Dynamics are a bow thing"** is one lesson, explicitly a *reading→playing* bridge, marked

## Pass 3 — Articulation

*(research batch 3: 2026-09-02 — 5 searches)*

Articulation = **how a note starts and stops, and how it connects (or doesn't) to the next.**
A duration-length spectrum:

`staccatissimo` — shorter — `staccato` — normal — `tenuto` — `legato` (fully connected)

### The marks

| Mark | Symbol | Means (read it as) | Note on the violin (perform → Stage 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **staccato** | a **dot** above/below the head | short and detached — roughly half the written value, silence for the rest | a lifted or stopped bow; brush/spiccato later |
| **staccatissimo** | a small **wedge / spike** (▾) | *very* short, spiky | the shortest on-string or off-string stroke |
| **tenuto** | a short **horizontal line** — | hold the note its *full* value, leaning into it, connected | sustained weight, no lift |
| **accent** | **`>`** | stronger attack on this note — *relative* to the prevailing dynamic | more bow speed / a bite at the start |
| **marcato** | **`^`** (open wedge, above the head) | "marked" — heavier and more separated than an accent; accent + a touch of length off | a firm martelé-type attack |
| **portato / louré / mezzo-staccato** | **dots *under* a slur**, or a dot + tenuto line | gently pulsed but connected — each note ~¾ value, no re-articulation | several notes in one bow with a slight give between |
| **legato** | a **slur** over the notes | smoothly connected, no gap | one bow (see the slur note below) |
| **fermata** | the **"bird's eye"** ⌢• | hold longer than written | — |
| **breath mark** | a **comma** ' above the staff | a small lift / breath — for strings, lift the bow briefly | a retake |
| **caesura** | **`//`** ("railroad tracks") | a definite break — stop, then go on | bow off, silence, then resume |
| **G.P. / grand pause** | "G.P." over a whole rest | everyone silent for the bar | — |

### Articulation *stacks*

Marks combine because their jobs combine:
- **staccato + slur** = **portato** — one bow, gently detached notes.
- **staccato + accent** = short *and* stressed.
- **tenuto + staccato** = held to near-full length but still lifted off the next note —
  "leaned on, then released."
- **staccato + tenuto line** (over one note) = a *slightly* separated note, not spiky.
- **accent + tenuto** = a stressed, sustained note.

### The fermata — how long?

The **duration is the performer's / conductor's call**, but with era conventions:
- **Baroque generally:** about **twice** the written value. **In Bach chorales** a fermata
  usually just marks the **end of a phrase** — take a breath, don't necessarily lengthen.
- **Classical (Mozart):** more individual; often marks a **cadence or a structural hinge**.
- **Romantic:** a dramatic device — stretch time, suspend the moment. Held much longer.
- So: "hold longer" is the *what*; how much is *style + judgment*. Twice as long is the safe
  default.

### The style layer — articulation in Mozart (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4)

This is where the style thread bites hardest, because articulation was **assumed**, not
always printed:
- **The late-18th-century norm was *non-legato*.** Notes with *no* mark were played
  **shorter than written, with a little air between them** — a light, speaking détaché. A
  modern player's instinct is the opposite (join everything); reading Mozart means
  *un-learning* that.
- **Slurs stopped at the barline** — composers were "compelled by tradition to show metrical
  accentuation" (the hierarchy of the beat). A slur crossing a barline in a Classical score
  is unusual and meaningful.
- **Under a slur**, Leopold Mozart: the **first note slightly stronger and longer**, the rest
  "slurred on to it with a diminishing of the tone… without the slightest accent." A slur is
  a *shape*, not just a legato.
- **Dots vs strokes/wedges in Mozart** — the famous mess: Mozart wrote articulation
  **inconsistently and often illegibly**; copyists diverged; **two Urtext editions
  disagree** on the same passage. The rough consensus: a **wedge/stroke is longer and
  heavier, with a slight accent and a lifted separation**; a **dot is lighter and shorter**.
  K.216's solo part has both — a genuine reading problem, and a perfect Pass-8 example of
  "Urtext ≠ manuscript."
- **Slur vs phrase mark** — in string parts a slur means **one bow** (literal); a longer
  curve meant as *phrasing* should be a **dashed line** (and orchestrators are told not to
  print long phrase slurs in string parts at all). When there's one ambiguous curve, it's a
  bow.

## Pass 4 — Bow & string words (the bow-word glossary)

*(research batch 4: 2026-09-02 — 6 searches)*

### Bow direction

| Mark | Symbol | Direction | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **down-bow** | **⊓** (a little bracket/staple) above the note | frog → tip, you *pull* | naturally stronger, gets a touch heavier |
| **up-bow** | **V** above the note | tip → frog, you *push* | naturally lighter |
| **retake** | a **comma** or a lift/hook sign | lift the bow and reset (usually back toward the frog) | resets weight; a small silence |

- The symbols probably evolved from the letters **n** (*nobilis* — strong/noble) and
  **v** (*vilis* — the opposite): Renaissance/Baroque players wrote n/v over notes to show
  strong vs weak, and the shapes stuck as ⊓ / V.
- **The rule of the down-bow** (Lully and the *24 Violons du Roi*, ~1660s; described by Georg
  Muffat): **the first note of the bar takes a down-bow.** The Baroque bow makes a stronger
  down-stroke, so this aligns the strong beat with the strong stroke. Corollaries: **no two
  successive down-bows within a bar** (French practice), but a **down-bow across the barline**
  is fine and gives the bar a lift. → A **reading aid**: a lone ⊓ near a bar start is telling
  you where beat 1 is. (Ties to Stage 2 `rests-ties-and-slurs-in-the-bow`.)

### The string / bow words a score throws at you

| Word | Means | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **arco** | with the bow (used to cancel a `pizz.` passage) | normal |
| **pizz.** (pizzicato) | pluck the string with a finger | short, percussive, decays fast |
| **left-hand pizz.** | pluck with a *left-hand* finger — marked with a **`+`** above the note | for fast plucks between bowed notes |
| **snap / Bartók pizz.** | pluck so hard the string slaps the fingerboard — marked with a **circle-and-line** | harsh, edgy — Bartók onward, REFERENCE |
| **col legno** | with the **wood** of the bow: *battuto* (struck) / *tratto* (drawn) | thin, brittle, eerie — REFERENCE |
| **con sordino** / **senza sordino** (or *via sord.*) | mute **on** / **off** — a small clamp near the bridge | veiled, softer, darker |
| **sul ponticello** (sul pont.) | bow **right by the bridge** | glassy, nasal, metallic |
| **sul tasto** / **flautando** | bow **over the fingerboard** | soft, flute-like, few overtones |
| **naturale** / **ord.** / **modo ordinario** | back to normal contact point | — |
| **divisi** (div.) / **unis.** / **non div.** / **a 2** | a section *splits* to play different notes / back to one line | (orchestral — REFERENCE for a soloist) |

### Which string

- **sul G / sul D / sul A / sul E** — play this passage on the named string (for tone colour,
  or to keep a fast line on one string).
- **String numbers in Roman numerals:** **I = E, II = A, III = D, IV = G.** The **highest**
  string is **I**. This is a genuine trap — "III" is the *D* string, not the third-from-bottom.
- **`sul G`** and **`IV`** mean the same thing.
- A circled Roman numeral + a small **°** = a **harmonic** on that string (→ Pass 7).
- A string is only marked when there's a real choice or a colour reason — most notes, you
  pick the string yourself.

### Bow-stroke words that appear in scores — recognise now, *do* in Stage 5

These are words, not symbols; a Classical/Romantic part may print any of them. Stage 3 =
"know it names a way of moving the bow"; Stage 5 = learn to do it.

`détaché` (smooth separate strokes) · `legato` / slur (connected, one bow) · `martelé`
("hammered" — sharp attack, stop, release) · `staccato` (short) · `portato` / `louré`
(pulsed legato in one bow) · `spiccato` (controlled off-string bounce) · `sautillé` (fast
springing bounce) · `ricochet` / `jeté` (thrown, several bounces per stroke) · `collé`
(pinched from the string) · `bariolage` (rocking across strings, often one open) ·
`tremolo` (below).

### Tremolo notation — the 4th case after beam / stack / vibrato

**Strokes drawn through the note-stem** (or where a stem would be):

| Strokes | Play as | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | eighth notes | measured |
| **2** | sixteenth notes | measured |
| **3** (or `trem.`) | **as fast as possible** | unmeasured |
| strokes *between two noteheads* | alternate the two pitches rapidly | *fingered* tremolo (a slow trill in effect) |

Ties straight back to Stage 2's `beamed-notes-double-stops-and-vibrato`: a *bowed tremolo*
is one repeated note bowed fast (marks on the stem, no extra noteheads); a *fingered tremolo*
alternates two written pitches; *vibrato* is neither (a pitch wobble, no marks).

### Lesson implications (Pass 4)

- **Bow direction + the rule of the down-bow** is one lesson, and it's a *reading* lesson
  (where's beat 1?) as much as a technique one — pair it with Stage 2's bow-half material.
- **The colour words** (`sul pont.` / `sul tasto` / `pizz.` / `con sord.`) are one lesson —
  they change the *sound*, and connecting them to Stage 0's bow-lanes and Stage 5's contact
  point makes them concrete rather than a vocabulary list.
- **The string-number trap (I = E)** must be its own beat — it's exactly the
  `LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §3 "position numbers ≠ finger numbers ≠ string numbers" tangle,
  and Pass 7's fingering lesson leans on it.
- **The bow-stroke word list is a REFERENCE card**, not a drilled lesson — `RULES.md` says
  the atlas is one real card per stroke, and the *doing* is Stage 5.

## Pass 5 — Repeats & the road-map

*(research batch 5: 2026-09-02 — 11 searches)*

### Repeat barlines

- **`‖:` … `:‖`** — a thin+thick barline pair with **two dots** (above and below the middle
  line). `‖:` opens the repeated section (dots on the right), `:‖` closes it (dots on the left).
- **No opening `‖:`** → repeat **from the very beginning**.
- **Baroque origin.** Binary dance forms and the French overture were written as two halves,
  each **enclosed by a double bar and repeat dots** — the sign saved paper and fixed the
  composer's intent. The repeat is not filler; in these forms it *is* the structure.

### Volta brackets — 1st / 2nd (/ 3rd) endings

- *volta* = "time"; **prima volta** / **seconda volta**. A numbered bracket sits over the last
  bar(s) of the repeated section.
- **Route:** play through, take the **1.** bar(s), hit `:‖`, go back. Second time: play up to
  the **1.** bracket, **skip it**, drop into the **2.** bracket, carry on. Three-plus endings
  stack the same way (for verse songs).

### The jumps — D.C., D.S., segno, coda, Fine

| Mark | Italian | Does |
|---|---|---|
| **D.C.** | *da capo* — "from the head" | go back to **the very beginning** |
| **D.S.** | *dal segno* — "from the sign" | go back to the **𝄋** (an S with a slash and two dots) |
| **Fine** | "the end" | where you **stop** (a fermata over the final barline can mark it instead) |
| **𝄌** + "**To Coda**" / "**al Coda**" | *coda* — "tail" | when you reach *To Coda*, **jump forward** to the matching **𝄌** and play the coda to the end |

- **The four routes:** *D.C. al Fine* (top → Fine, stop) · *D.S. al Fine* (sign → Fine, stop) ·
  *D.C. al Coda* (top → *To Coda* → jump to 𝄌 → end) · *D.S. al Coda* (sign → *To Coda* →
  jump to 𝄌 → end).
- **Origin — 18th-century opera.** The **da capo aria** (ABA, ternary): the third section was
  *never written out* — the composer just wrote "da capo" and the singer repeated section A
  (with added ornaments). The **dal segno aria** is the variant where the opening orchestral
  ritornello is skipped on the return, so the **𝄋 sits just after the ritornello**. The
  **Minuet–Trio–Minuet da capo** is the instrumental counterpart — same idea, no third section
  printed.

### The convention that trips everyone up — *senza replica*

- On the **D.C. / D.S. pass you do NOT take the inner repeats** (the `:‖` and volta brackets
  you took the first time). It is **tradition, rarely printed.**
- Historically composers wrote **"senza replica"** / **"senza repetizione"** ("without
  repetition") when they wanted the inner repeats **dropped**, and **"con replica"** when they
  wanted them **kept**. Classical practice on this varied; the settled **modern habit** is:
  **observe repeats on the first play-through, ignore them on the da capo** — even when (as in
  some Beethoven scherzos) the opening phrase barely makes sense unpaired.
- This is a textbook **"the page says one thing, the style says another"** moment
  (`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4) — the score prints `D.C. al Fine` and expects you to *know* to
  strip the inner repeats.

### Rehearsal marks

- **Boxed or circled letters** (A, B, C…) or **numbers**, placed at structural points, so a
  conductor can call "from **G**" instead of counting bars.
- **Romantic era onward** — credited as **starting with Louis Spohr**; once movements ran to
  many hundreds of bars, counting from the top stopped being practical. Rehearsal **numbers**
  in modern scores are often the bar number, or land at a fixed interval.
- A student reading a concerto part **sees** them — they're a map label, **not a sound**.

### Multi-bar rest

- A thick horizontal **"H-bar"** with a **number above** = rest that many complete bars.
  Lives in **orchestral parts**, not full scores (the score shows every bar).
- You still count the bars — the sign just compresses the page.

### Repeat-bar & *simile* signs

- **One-bar repeat:** a single slash with a dot either side (looks like **`%`**), centred in
  the bar = "play the **previous bar** again."
- **Two-bar repeat:** the `%` with a small **"2"** above, spanning two bars = "play the
  **previous two bars** again."
- **Beat repeat:** the slash inside a bar = "repeat the previous beat."
- **simile** / **simili** = "in the same manner" — keep the articulation / pattern / stroke
  that was just written out, without re-marking every note.

### Why the repeats are there (plant for Stage 4)

Every Baroque/Classical dance movement is **binary** (`‖: A :‖: B :‖`) or **rounded binary**
(`‖: A :‖: B A′ :‖`) — both halves repeated. A **sonata-form exposition** carries the same
repeat. So decoding a minuet's road map *is* reading its form: two halves, each twice, then
(after the Trio) the Minuet once more, straight through. Full treatment is Stage 4 — Stage 3
just names it when the repeat signs appear.

### K.216 tie-in

The finale is **"Rondeau: Allegro," 3/8** — a rondo: a theme that keeps returning, with
contrasting episodes between (a brief **G-minor gavotte**, then the folk **"Strassburg"** tune
with drone bass). A rondo's returns are usually **written out**, but reading one drills the
same reflex: *"we're back at the theme."*

### Lesson implications (Pass 5)

  times through.
- **The jump family** (D.C./D.S./𝄋/𝄌/Fine + all four "al" routes) = one lesson, taught as
  *follow the route on a map*, with a worked diagram of a piece that uses D.S. al Coda.
- **senza replica** is its own beat inside that lesson — the "read vs play in style" hinge.
- **Rehearsal marks + multi-bar rest + `%`/simile** = one REFERENCE-leaning "other road signs"
  lesson — a soloist meets them in orchestral parts and lead sheets, not in a Twinkle line.
- The **"repeats are the form"** point is the *where it comes from* beat of the volta lesson,
  with a forward pointer to Stage 4 — not a standalone lesson (that would duplicate Stage 4).

## Pass 6 — Ornaments (the big movement)

*(research batch 6: 2026-09-02 — 11 searches)*

### The core idea

An ornament is **shorthand**: a small symbol (or a cluster of small-print notes) standing for a
quick flurry of extra notes around the written note. **The symbol tells you WHICH ornament; the
era tells you HOW to play it.** This is the `LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §4 keystone — *"the symbol
tells you WHAT; the era tells you HOW MUCH."* In Stage 3 you **read** them: recognise the
symbol, know its family, know the rough shape, know the era shifts the details. **Performing**
them up to speed is Stage 6 — kept separate (`RULES.md` #7).

### The symbol inventory

*(P = principal/written note · U = the step above · L = the step below)*

| Ornament | Symbol | Rough shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Trill** | `tr`, often + a wavy line showing how long | rapid **P↔U** for the note's length |
| **Upper mordent** (a.k.a. *Pralltriller* / *Schneller*) | short zigzag, **no** line through | **P–U–P**, one quick flip |
| **Lower mordent** (the plain "mordent") | short zigzag **with a vertical line** through | **P–L–P**, one quick flip |
| **Turn** / *gruppetto* | a lazy sideways **S** above the note | **U–P–L–P** |
| **Inverted turn** | the S with a short vertical stroke (or upside-down) | **L–P–U–P** |
| **Appoggiatura** (the "long" grace) | a small note, **no slash**, slurred to P | leans **on the beat**, **takes value from P** |
| **Acciaccatura** (the "short" / crushed grace) | a small note **with a slash** through the stem | as quick as possible, **before the beat**, steals no value |
| **Slide** / *Schleifer* / *coulé* | two small rising notes slurred to P | **L2–L1–P**, filling in from below |
| **Arpeggiated / rolled chord** | a **vertical wavy line** before the chord | notes fast, **bottom → top** (arrowhead down = top→bottom; square bracket = *don't* roll) |

### Trill — the era really does change it

- **Baroque:** start on the **upper auxiliary**. The trill is a harmonic suspension that
  resolves down onto P. C.P.E. Bach, Marpurg, Türk — *all* trills begin on the upper note.
- **Classical:** in transition — mixed practice right through the 1790s, teacher to teacher.
- **Later (Hummel 1828, Czerny, Moscheles):** start on the **principal note** — Hummel's rule,
  partly just because it's easier under the hand. Many modern players carry this as the default.
- → A `tr` on a Mozart note is **genuinely ambiguous**; the note, the harmony and the edition
  all weigh in. The clearest **"read ≠ play in style"** moment in the whole stage.
- **Accidental above / just right of `tr`** = the pitch of the **upper** note (e.g. `tr` + ♯ =
  trill up to the sharpened note).
- **Termination / *Nachschlag*** = a small turn-figure (L–P) rounding the trill off, often
  printed as two little notes after the wavy line, sometimes just implied.
- **Leopold Mozart:** *"All short trills are played with a quick appoggiatura [from above] and
  a turn"* — upper-note start **+** a termination. (He took his trill and vibrato examples
  wholesale from Tartini's *Traité des agréments*, only transposing them.)

### Mordent — the naming trap

- **Modern editions:** *upper* mordent = **P–U–P** (zigzag, no line); *lower* mordent = **P–L–P**
  (zigzag with a line).
- **Baroque:** the bare word **"mordent"** meant the **lower** one (P–L–P); the upper flip was
  the **Pralltriller** or **Schneller** (Pralltriller ties from the previous note on a
  descending second; Schneller doesn't).
- Same zigzag, different meaning by era — flag it, don't drill it.

### Turn — where it sits on the beat

- **Directly above the note:** play the turn *on* that note (U–P–L–P).
- **Offset to the right, between two notes:** **delayed turn** — hold the first note, then turn
  into the next.
- **Accidental above** the sign → alters the **top** note; **accidental below** → the **bottom**
  note.

### Appoggiatura vs acciaccatura — the value question (a rhythm lesson in disguise)

- **Appoggiatura** (no slash): **on the beat**, **takes time from the main note** — classic
  rule **half** the value, **two-thirds** if the main note is dotted. The weight is *on the
  grace note*: a leaning dissonance that resolves onto P. Leopold Mozart, Ch. IX: a small note
  before a descending quarter / eighth / sixteenth = a **long** appoggiatura, half the value.
- **Acciaccatura** (slash): **crushed in before the beat**, as fast as possible, **steals no
  value** — P still lands squarely on its beat. The weight is *on P*.
- Historically many small printed notes were meant as **long appoggiaturas** even without a
  clear rule; playing *every* small note fast-and-before-the-beat is a Romantic import. In
  Mozart, **default to on-the-beat** and let the harmony and note length set the length.
- Duration always flexes with tempo, the main note's length, what's around it, and the effect.

### Free ornamentation — name it, don't teach it

In the Baroque and early Classical, players also added **unwritten** ornaments — cadential
trills, filled-in leaps, passing divisions (Leopold Mozart gives whole chapters to it). Stage 3
only needs the reader to know: **a clean-looking Classical line was not played dead straight.**
*Adding* your own ornaments is a Stage 7+/repertoire-module skill.

### K.216 tie-in

The solo part has trills at cadences, appoggiaturas all through the Adagio's lyrical line, and
turn figures. A modern **Urtext** prints the bare symbols; a **teaching edition** (Flesch,
Oistrakh) often writes the realisation out in small notes above the staff — and **two editions
disagree**. Reading the concerto means recognising the symbol *and* knowing the printed
realisation is one editor's opinion (→ Pass 8).

### Lesson implications (Pass 6)

- **The largest movement in Stage 3.** The PRD's "the era tells you how" keystone lives here.
- **One lesson per ornament family**, each with: the symbol; the rough shape **written out in
  our notated line, every small note fully labelled** (`RULES.md` #1 — a realisation is still
  pitched notation); what to listen for; the era note; a "you'll *play* this in Stage 6"
  pointer.
- The **trill** lesson carries the Baroque-vs-Classical start as its own beat.

  note steal the beat or not? — which plays straight to the user's strength.
- A closing **"ornaments are an opinion"** lesson: symbol vs written-out, why editions differ,
  the K.216 example, a read-vs-style checkpoint.

## Pass 7 — String-part notation (chords, harmonics, fingerings, position numbers)

*(research batch 7: 2026-09-02 — 11 searches)*

### Chords & double stops on the page

- A **double stop** = two notes on two strings sounded together, drawn as **two noteheads on
  one stem**. Strictly it isn't a "chord" (a chord = 3+). Three / four strings = **triple /
  quadruple stop**; together, **multiple stops**.
- The violin's **curved bridge** lets only **two** strings truly sound at once. So a printed
  **3- or 4-note chord** is played **broken, bottom → top**: the lower pair, then the upper
  pair, on one bow (often 2 + 2, or a fast roll). This is how the Bach *Chaconne* chords work.
- **On the page** it's just a stack of 3–4 noteheads on one stem — no "roll" mark needed, the
  technique is assumed. A **vertical wavy line** (Pass 6) can *force* a roll; a **square
  bracket** forbids one.
- **divisi (div.)** vs a multiple stop: in an orchestral part `div.` = the section **splits**
  (players take one note each); **no `div.`** on a stack = **everyone plays the double stop**.
  `unis.` / `non div.` = back to one line. A soloist doesn't act on it but will see it in a score.

### Harmonics

- **Natural harmonic:** a small **circle (○)** above the note, or an **open diamond notehead**.
  You *touch* (don't press) the string at a node; the tone is pure, flute-like, whistling,
  usually far higher than the finger's position suggests. Sometimes **two figures** appear —
  `0` (it *is* a natural harmonic) plus a finger number (4, sometimes 2 or 3).
- **Artificial harmonic:** **two noteheads on one stem** — a **normal oval** (1st finger presses
  firmly) plus a **diamond a fourth above** (4th finger lightly touches). It sounds **two
  octaves above the stopped note**. A small note in ( ) may show the resulting pitch.
- Circled string numeral + ○ = "this harmonic, on that string" (Pass 4).

### Fingerings ≠ positions ≠ strings — the numeral tangle

`LESSONS-FROM-MOZART.md` §3, and a genuine reading hazard:

| On the page | Means |
|---|---|
| **Arabic 1 2 3 4** (0 = open) hovering over a note | which **finger** |
| **Roman I–IV**, or "sul G / D / A / E" | which **string** (**I = E**, the highest — Pass 4) |
| **Roman numeral / "III. pos." / "3rd pos."** at a passage start | which **position** (where the whole hand sits) |
| a **circled** numeral | a **string** (drawn circled to tell it from a finger number) |

- The same "**III**" can mean the **D string** *or* **third position** — told apart by
  placement: a numeral over a single note = string; a numeral (often boxed, or with "pos.") at
  a phrase's start = position.
- **Editorial fingerings** are the *editor's* suggestion, not the composer's — often the tell
  that you're holding a teaching edition (Pass 8).

### Glissando & portamento

- **Glissando:** a **line** (straight or wavy) between two notes, often + "gliss." — you
  **hear the pitches slide through**. Wavy = sound every step; straight = smooth glide.
- **Portamento:** an **expressive slide** that "carries" one note into the next — shorter,
  subtler, begins late (just before the target). **Frequently not notated** — the player adds it.
- **Style layer:** glissando/portamento were **not** part of Classical or Baroque violin
  technique — they became the hallmark of **Romantic** playing (19th c.). A slide added to the
  K.216 Adagio is a Romantic-flavoured interpretive choice, not something the page asks for.
  (Contested at the margins — some argue tasteful slides predate the Romantics — but the safe
  reading is Classical = sparing, Romantic = an expressive staple.)

### Octave & register signs

- **8va** (*all'ottava*), dotted line above a passage = play **one octave higher** than written
  — keeps high notes off a ladder of ledger lines.
- **8vb** (*ottava bassa*), below = one octave **lower**.
- **15ma** = **two** octaves higher; **15mb** = two lower.
- **loco** ("in place") = cancel it, return to written pitch. Note *names* never change — only
  the octave.
- Violin parts essentially never leave **treble clef** (unlike viola/cello) — the extreme top
  uses **8va**, not a clef change. Worth a sentence so the reader isn't bracing for a bass clef
  that never comes.

### K.216 tie-in

The solo part uses double stops (thirds and sixths at cadences), the occasional natural
harmonic, and editorial fingerings + position numerals throughout (Flesch, Auer, Oistrakh …) —
and, in Romantic-tradition editions, suggested portamento in the Adagio. A clean Urtext strips
most of the fingerings; the notes are unchanged.

### Lesson implications (Pass 7)

- **Chords / double stops on the page** = one lesson — *reading* the stack and knowing "3–4
  notes → rolled bottom-to-top." Playing them is Stage 6.
- **Harmonics notation** = one lesson — the circle, the open diamond, the two-notehead
  artificial. Producing them is Stage 6.

- **Slides + octave signs** = one lesson — two slide marks plus 8va / 8vb / loco, with the
  Classical-restraint style note riding along.
- Everything here is **read**, not perform — say so in every lesson.

## Pass 8 — Editions: what the composer wrote vs what the editor added

*(research batch 8: 2026-09-02 — 10 searches)*

### The core idea

The notes on your stand passed through an **editor** between the composer's pen and the print
shop. Some marks are the composer's; some are the editor's helpful additions; a few are the
editor's **guesses** where the sources are unclear or disagree. A skilled reader knows which is
which — and knows that **"it's printed" ≠ "the composer wrote it."**

### The four kinds of edition

| Kind | What it is | For |
|---|---|---|
| **Facsimile** | a photo of the manuscript / first print | scholars |
| **Urtext** | the composer's text, rebuilt from all authoritative sources, editorial additions flagged | players who want to decide for themselves |
| **Performing / "artist" edition** | Urtext text **+** a named soloist's fingerings, bowings, dynamics, phrasing | learners; a particular school (Flesch, Auer, Joachim, Oistrakh) |
| **Critical edition** | Urtext **+** a full critical report (every source, every decision) | the reference standard — e.g. the *Neue Mozart-Ausgabe* |

### What "Urtext" really means (and doesn't)

- German for "original text." Aim: the music **as the composer wrote it**, free of an editor's
  fingerings, bowings, interpretation.
- **Urtext ≠ the manuscript.** The autograph is usually the top source but not always final —
  it can be lost, incomplete, or superseded by a corrected first edition. The editor **weighs
  all authorised sources** to settle the most reliable reading.
- **Urtext ≠ infallible.** Where sources are ambiguous or contradictory the editor makes a
  **judgement call**, and two Urtext publishers can land on different answers for the same bar.
  (A Henle Urtext of Schubert's *Arpeggione* has been shown to diverge from the autograph in
  many spots — accents confused with diminuendos, slurs misplaced.)
- Urtext editions arose in the **1950s**, reacting against a century of heavy editorial
  "improvement."
- **Two philosophies:** **Bärenreiter** tends to **reconstruct** what the composer "truly
  meant" (may depart from any single source); **Henle** tends to **print what the main source
  says** and flag the doubt in the commentary. Same piece, subtly different page.

### How editorial additions are marked

- **Square brackets `[ ]`** around an accidental, dynamic, articulation or note = **the editor
  added this** (modern standard, borrowed from text editing).
- **Parentheses `( )`** = an **optional or doubtful** mark (a courtesy accidental; a
  questionable reading).
- **Dashed / dotted slur or tie** = an **editorial** slur (vs a solid one from the composer).
- **Small-print** notes, accidentals or fingerings = editorial suggestion.
- **The preface / a footnote states *that edition's* system — read it first.** Many string
  editions separate the composer's own bowings from the editor's suggested ones (by line style,
  or by a note in the front matter); there is **no single universal "above = composer, below =
  editor" rule**, so check the edition's own key.

### The *ossia* stave

A small extra stave above or below the main one = an **alternative** (*"or it may be"*): an
easier version, a written-out ornament, a suggested cadenza, or a variant reading from another
source. You choose which to play.

### K.216 specifically

- The reference text is the **Neue Mozart-Ausgabe** (critical edition). Bärenreiter's practical
  edition ships **two solo parts** — a clean Urtext and a **separately annotated** one with
  bowings, fingerings, performance hints.
- Mozart's articulation in the autograph is **not always legible or consistent** — the
  dot-vs-stroke problem (Pass 3) is an editorial decision in *every* printed edition.
- The concerto's **cadenzas and Eingänge (lead-ins) are not by Mozart** — the common ones are
  by **Joachim, Auer, Franko** and others, printed in a supplement. Which one you play is a
  choice, and the page should name whose it is.

### The payoff line

**Reading a score in Stage 3 = reading the composer's marks *and* seeing the editor's hand.**
When you later "make your own interpretive choices" (the PRD north star), step one is knowing
which marks are actually up for choice.

  ossia, with our line showing a composer mark beside a bracketed editorial one.
- **One "kinds of edition" lesson** — facsimile / Urtext / performing / critical; Urtext ≠
  manuscript ≠ infallible; the preface holds the key. REFERENCE-leaning but important — it
  reframes every score they'll open.
- Tie hard to the PRD: interpretive freedom begins with knowing what's editorial.
- Conceptual, not a symbol pile — **2 lessons**.

### The two closing lessons

**1. Decode a dense line, mark by mark.** One 8–12 bar line we author, deliberately loaded:
a tempo word + a modifier, an opening dynamic, a hairpin, a `cresc. poco a poco`, a slur
group, staccato dots, one accent, a `rit.`, a fermata, a `D.C. al Fine`. Then walk it — every

the composer's intent, not just naming notes" — but the intent-**in-style** layer is
explicitly deferred, and saying so out loud is the lesson.
